\begin{abstract}
Feeding a language model the expected embedding of its own next token, as Soft Thinking does, turns inference into a
dynamical system. In Qwen2.5, OLMo-2 and Llama-3 models of 0.5B to 72B
parameters, temperature and steering move this loop between repeating text and chaos, with positive Lyapunov
exponents that exact tangents reproduce. The chaos has practical consequences. Rerun with only the batch size
changed, chaotic float64 loops separate until their most probable tokens differ, on the schedule the Lyapunov exponent sets, while periodic loops mostly stay together, in four models up to 70B. In frozen
tests on arithmetic, the batch layout changed 56\,\% of the answers of bfloat16 soft-thinking inference in a 0.5B
model and 55\,\% in a 7B model, far more often than those of a forced greedy baseline. An exploratory reset of the fed-back state at every step removed most such changes. Exactly repeating text can hide
a chaotic state. Along the sampled orbits, steering creates the chaos mainly by making next tokens compete,
which turns the softmax readout from a contraction into an expansion; a logit bias alone suffices. Earlier injection
carries chaos to lower temperature, steering at two depths creates chaos neither depth causes alone, and two chaotic
loops writing one text lock each other into periodic orbits. Beyond first-token uncertainty, the loop's regime does not mark
the accuracy of ordinary answers, and we find no edge-of-chaos advantage. The loop obeys quantitative laws of
low-dimensional maps: doubling ratios approach Feigenbaum's constant (4.6688 against 4.6692), periodic windows
accumulate on folds with a $1/N^2$ law, fold normal forms predicted 23 of 24 frozen passage times within one iterate,
and interval arithmetic proves an attracting period-8 orbit. In the recurrent-depth model Huginn, a frozen test confirmed a predicted accuracy loss. The loop is not ordinary sampled generation.
\end{abstract}

Inference increasingly feeds a language model its own continuous output. Soft Thinking replaces each sampled token by
the expected embedding under the next-token distribution \cite{zhang2025softthinking}, latent-reasoning methods feed
hidden states back \cite{hao2024coconut}, and recurrent-depth models iterate a block toward a fixed point
\cite{geiping2025recurrent}. Each turns inference into an iterated map, and an iterated map can be chaotic. We show
that the expected-embedding loop of released language models is chaotic over wide ranges of temperature and steering
strength, explain why steering makes it so, and measure what the chaos does: in a chaotic regime an irrelevant
change of batch layout decides what the loop writes. In bfloat16, where nearly every loop diverges, the batch layout
changed the answers of a short soft-thinking pipeline on 56\,\% and 55\,\% of 96 arithmetic problems in two
models. The same loops obey the quantitative
laws of low-dimensional dynamics, down to Feigenbaum's constant.

Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows a small piece of the parameter plane of one
such map, built from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct. Cells with a positive finite-horizon Lyapunov exponent lie side by side
with windows in which the model repeats a fixed loop of text. Parts of this picture have been seen
before. Recent work has measured chaos-like sensitivity in language-model generation
\cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos}, temperature-driven changes of output statistics
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality,johnson2026temperature}, periodic
attractors of paraphrasing loops \cite{wang2025attractor}, attractor states of model-to-model conversations
\cite{ko2026attractor}, and, through their Jacobians, the stability of looped and recurrent-depth models
\cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars}, and finite-depth readout dynamics have
been used to predict the answers of looped models \cite{luo2026prediction}. Besides Soft
Thinking, probability-weighted vocabulary embeddings are fed back in training \cite{zeng2025ponderlm}. In a small task-trained looped transformer, fold coefficients
computed from derivatives predict passage times and spectral gaps without fitting \cite{kim2026phase}.

We study one constructed but exactly defined system: the expected-embedding (``soft-token'') map of a frozen,
released pretrained language model, run with a fixed chat prefix at a fixed position. The map is smooth. Its
Jacobians come from automatic differentiation, and its periodic orbits can be computed and continued like those of
any smooth map. Chaos and periodic windows occur in many nonlinear feedback systems. Random-network theory, in which
a specified ensemble of recurrent networks becomes chaotic above a critical gain, in continuous time
\cite{sompolinsky1988chaos} and in discrete time by a quasi-periodic route \cite{doyon1994bifurcations}, and the
period-doubling cascades and shrimp-shaped domains of low-dimensional maps \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative,gallas1993structure}
motivate looking for such structure here, but they do not imply that a trained map realizes it. Whether a trained
language model does, where in its parameter space, with which texts, and whether the quantitative predictions of
bifurcation theory hold for it, so that derivatives computed from the model predict its behaviour before that
behaviour is measured, has to be tested. The models we studied do realize it, and in the cases we tested the
predictions hold. We also show where the chaos comes from (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), and that larger models do not simply leave it behind (Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}). Ordinary sampled generation with a growing context is a different
system; Section~\ref{sec:beyond} also measures how much of the mechanism survives sampling.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The results fall into four groups.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Chaos in released models, and what it does to inference} (Sections~\ref{sec:maps}
  and~\ref{sec:consequences}; Appendix~\ref{sec:scale}). Temperature and steering move the loop of Qwen2.5, OLMo-2 and
  Llama-3 models between repeating text and chaos with embedded windows. The regime decides whether a run can be
  reproduced. Run twice with only the batch layout changed, 190 of 192 chaotic float64 loops of Qwen2.5-0.5B grow past
  a relative distance of $10^{-2}$ after a median of 102 steps, in line with the Lyapunov exponent (median ratio
  0.91), and at 173 of them the most probable readout token comes to differ; 182 of 192 periodic loops stay within
  $10^{-2}$. Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B without steering (24 of 24 and 20 of 20 chaotic loops diverge, 0 of 24 and 1
  of 20 periodic ones) and Llama-3.3-70B with steering (24 of 24, none of 24) behave alike. In bfloat16 nearly every
  loop diverges, and in a frozen test on 96 additions the batch layout changed the answer of a soft-thinking pipeline
  in 56\,\% of problems in Qwen2.5-0.5B and 55\,\% in Qwen2.5-7B, against never and 10\,\% for a forced greedy
  baseline. An exploratory causal test shows that the loop carries this: resetting the fed-back state before every soft step
  removes nearly all of it (on an H100, 66 against 1.6\,\% of answers changed in Qwen2.5-0.5B, and 64 against
  1.6\,\% in Qwen2.5-7B), and accuracy changes by at most 5 points. Exactly repeating text can hide a chaotic state (Section~\ref{sec:hostile}). Beyond the model's
  first-token uncertainty, the loop's regime carries no detectable information about the accuracy of ordinary sampled
  answers, and we find no advantage at the edge of chaos.
\item \emph{Why the loop is chaotic, and how steering makes it so} (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The loop's derivative
  factorizes into the inverse temperature, the covariance of token embeddings under the model's own next-token
  distribution, and the network's Jacobian; without the network the map would be a modern Hopfield update, which
  cannot cycle. The factorization proves contraction at sufficiently high temperature, the zero-temperature limit is
  eventually periodic, and chaos in our sweeps occupies intermediate temperatures. Stability belongs to the loop, not
  to its steps: along a period-8 orbit every step expands some direction while the cycle contracts. Along the sampled
  orbits, steering makes next tokens compete. Steering into chaos raises the readout factor of each step's growth by
  2.58 nats per step relative to nearly unsteered cells, while the network factor changes by only $-0.07$ (interval
  $[-0.23,0.12]$); a logit bias alone, with the network untouched, makes 3.5\,\% of the plane chaotic; and an
  orbit-preserving crossover attributes the expansion at fixed states to steering's change of the readout ($+0.81$
  nats per step in the 0.5B model), not of the network, whose change damps it ($-0.49$). Random directions also steer
  into chaos. Earlier steering carries chaos to lower temperature, in Qwen2.5-0.5B and Llama-3.3-70B, and two vectors
  at two depths create chaos where neither alone does (Section~\ref{sec:consequences}).
\item \emph{Low-dimensional organization, predicted from derivatives} (Sections~\ref{sec:adding}
  to~\ref{sec:organization}). The windows carry concrete texts: a period-adding family gains one ``is X'' pair per
  period step. A fold's normal form, computed from derivatives, predicts without fitting how long trajectories linger
  near the vanished orbit: at two new folds, 23 of 24 frozen forecasts fell within one iterate (the all-row criterion
  failed by one row), and windows accumulate on the fold with a $1/N^2$ law. On the nine fold curves we continued,
  all thirteen tangent reversals are located cusps, and near them local unfoldings, frozen before solving, matched the
  orbits that Newton solves recovered at twelve parameter offsets in number and at eleven in stability. A doubling
  cascade has superstable points through period 768 and finite-order Feigenbaum ratios that approach $\delta$ (the
  last, $4.6688$, against $4.6692$). A period-5 window of a plane steered at two depths has the anatomy of a shrimp in a
  two-dimensional map: fold and flip curves bound a main cell with legs and doubling shells, a trace-zero loop
  replaces the head of a one-dimensional shrimp, and twice the fold curve has two cusps of opposite kinds and a
  self-crossing. Around one of these junctions an orbit census fixed in advance matched the predicted counts at 9 of
  13 points and found two coexisting attracting orbits at all five points inside it. An interval-arithmetic proof shows that a primitive period-8 orbit exists and is locally attracting, to our
  knowledge the first proof of a periodic orbit of a full transformer language-model map
  (Appendix~\ref{sec:evidence}).
\item \emph{Beyond the one-token loop} (Sections~\ref{sec:beyond} to~\ref{sec:dialogue}). The chaos is not an
  artifact of the one-token window: with a memory of 8 or 16 soft tokens Qwen2.5-7B is chaotic at more temperatures
  than with one, and in Soft Thinking's growing context a tiny early perturbation grows exponentially inside its
  one-token chaotic band, though not in every model. The organization recurs across families, and size does not set
  the chaos: across fifteen models from 0.5B to 72B, a larger model's chaos can move to higher temperature instead of
  vanishing. Two loops writing one text lock each other into periodic orbits: 60\,\% of pairs of differently steered
  0.5B agents that are each chaotic alone are periodic together (twelve such orbits verified by Newton's method). In a
  second system, the recurrent-depth model Huginn, a spectral estimate ranks held-out questions by the size of their
  residual under relaxation, and a frozen generation test confirms the predicted accuracy loss at relaxation 1.20 and
  noninferiority at 1.05.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.} Chaos-like sensitivity of generation \cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos,verazuniga2026selffeeding},
temperature-driven changes of output statistics
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality}, repetition loops
\cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop} and behavioural attractors of paraphrasing and conversation
\cite{wang2025attractor,ko2026attractor} have been measured in generated text, and one study fits a noisy effective
map to a hidden-state coordinate \cite{johnson2026temperature}, without computing orbits, multipliers or bifurcations
of the model's own map. Equilibrium, looped and recurrent-depth models have been analysed through fixed
points and Jacobians \cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,geiping2025recurrent,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars},
and the closest methodological precedent, a derivative-based fold analysis of a small task-trained looped transformer, predicts
passage times without fitting \cite{kim2026phase}. Appendix~\ref{sec:related} discusses this work, expected-embedding
feedback, and bifurcations, Feigenbaum ratios and shrimps in neural models in detail.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to compute periodic orbits with their multipliers, continue fold and flip
bifurcation curves and locate cusps in the feedback map of a released pretrained language model. It is
also, to our knowledge, the first to make quantitative predictions from such a model's derivatives, for the objects
named here, and confirm them prospectively: passage times at new folds, where 23 of 24 forecasts fell within one
iterate and the all-row criterion failed by one row, and the orbits recovered and their stabilities at frozen offsets
near cusps. Derivative-based normal-form predictions in a task-trained looped transformer precede ours
\cite{kim2026phase}. We know of no earlier frozen held-out test of a spectral prediction of a pretrained language
model's response to relaxation, which we give for Huginn, and of no earlier computer-assisted proof of a periodic
orbit of a full transformer language-model map. These priority statements rest on targeted searches (12 and 15 October 2026) that found no prior work combining these
elements for the feedback map of a pretrained language model. That is an absence of evidence within the search, not
a proof of priority.

\section{The inference map}
\label{sec:map}
\paragraph{Definition.} We describe the map for Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the model studied in most detail; the other
models enter the same construction with their own embeddings and steering layers (Appendix~\ref{sec:scale}). Let
$E\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}$ be the input embedding matrix ($V=151{,}936$, $d=896$). The model reads a fixed chat prefix, with the system prompt ``You are an AI talking with another AI. You
have complete freedom. Feel free to pursue whatever you want.'' and the user turn ``Hello!'', followed by a single
input embedding $x$, and produces next-token logits $z(x)\in\mathbb{R}^V$. The soft-token map feeds the expected
next-token embedding back in place of $x$:
\begin{equation}
  F_{T,\alpha}(x) = E^{\top}\operatorname{softmax}\!\big(z_\alpha(x)/T\big),
\end{equation}
where $z_\alpha$ adds $\alpha v$ to the residual stream at layer 12 at every position, prefix included. The steering
direction $v$ is contrastive: it compares last-token states of ``philosophy'' prompts with those of neutral prompts
and is normalized to the mean residual norm. Positions are fixed and the window does not grow, so $F$ is an
autonomous map of $\mathbb{R}^{896}$. As $T\to0$ it reduces, where the argmax is unique, to greedy decoding of the
same fixed-prefix, one-token-window system. It is not ordinary generation with a growing context.

\paragraph{Precision.} Hugging Face RMSNorm layers and rotary-embedding tables compute in float32 even when the
model is cast to float64, so we patch both. A float32 map is accurate to $3\times10^{-6}$--$2\times10^{-5}$ per
step. That is enough for phase maps (245 of 256 test cells agree with float64) but not for derivatives of composed
maps: one-step float32 Jacobian-vector products are accurate to $5\times10^{-5}$, yet the 8-step monodromy of a
period-8 orbit is off by 3\%. All orbits, multipliers and test functions below are therefore computed in float64.

\section{Why the loop can be chaotic}
\label{sec:theory}
The soft-token map has a simple derivative. With $p=\operatorname{softmax}(z/T)$,
\begin{equation}
DF(x)=\frac{1}{T}\,E^{\top}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top}\bigr)J_z(x).
\end{equation}
Qwen2.5-0.5B ties its output matrix to the input embedding, so $z=E\tilde h(x)$, where $\tilde h$ is the final
normalized hidden state, and the derivative factorizes:
\begin{equation}
DF(x)=\frac{1}{T}\,\Sigma_p\,J_{\tilde h}(x),\qquad
\Sigma_p=E^{\top}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top}\bigr)E=\operatorname{Cov}_{t\sim p}(e_t).
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
\input{figures/tikz-loop}
The derivative of the loop (Figure~\ref{fig:loop}) is the covariance of the token embeddings under the model's own next-token distribution,
amplified by the inverse temperature and composed with the derivative of the network. At a state of the period-8
orbit of Table~\ref{tab:orbits}, \eqref{eq:gain} agrees with forward-mode derivatives of the full map to a relative
error of at most $1.35\times10^{-13}$ over eight random directions (\texttt{tools/softmax\_gain.py}). Without tied embeddings, as in the 7B and 8B models, the logits are $z=W\tilde h(x)$ with an
output matrix $W\neq E$, and $DF=T^{-1}E^{\top}(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top})W J_{\tilde h}(x)$: $\Sigma_p$ becomes
the cross-covariance $\operatorname{Cov}_{t\sim p}(e_t,w_t)$ of input and output embeddings, which is not symmetric in
general. The temperature limits below do not change.

\paragraph{A Hopfield network with a transformer inside.} If the network passed its input straight through,
$\tilde h(x)=x$, the map would be $F(x)=E^{\top}\operatorname{softmax}(Ex/T)$. This is exactly the update rule of a
modern Hopfield network whose stored patterns are the token embeddings, at inverse temperature $\beta=1/T$
\cite{ramsauer2020hopfield}. That update lowers an energy function at every step and converges to fixed points.
In \eqref{eq:gain} it is the case $J_{\tilde h}=I$, where $DF=\Sigma_p/T$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite.
The energy argument covers every map with that property. If $DF$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite everywhere,
then $F=\nabla\phi$ for a convex $\phi$, and convexity, $\phi(F(x))\ge\phi(x)+F(x)^{\top}(F(x)-x)$, gives for
$V(x)=\tfrac12\|x\|^2-\phi(x)$
\begin{equation}
V(F(x))\le V(x)-\tfrac12\|F(x)-x\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Along a cycle these decreases sum to zero, so every point of the cycle is fixed. $F$ maps every state into the
convex hull of the embeddings, where $V$ is bounded below, so the steps of every orbit tend to zero and its limit
points are fixed points: such a map has neither cycles nor chaos. Every cycle and every chaotic cell in this paper
therefore depends on the network factor $J_{\tilde h}$ breaking that structure. Attention has itself been read as a Hopfield retrieval \cite{ramsauer2020hopfield}; in the soft-token
map the retrieval closes a loop around the whole network.

\input{figures/fig-readout-gain}
\paragraph{High-temperature contraction and the greedy limit.} The largest Lyapunov exponent of an orbit cannot exceed the orbit
average of $\log\|DF\|$, so chaos needs $\|DF\|>1$ on a substantial part of the attractor. Both ends of the
temperature axis work against this, in different ways (Figure~\ref{fig:readoutgain}). As $T\to0$, $p$ becomes one-hot except near ties between the
two leading tokens; away from ties the gain $\|\Sigma_p\|/T$ decays like $e^{-\Delta/T}/T$, where $\Delta$ is the gap
between the two largest logits, and $F$ tends to the piecewise-constant greedy map, every orbit of which is eventually
periodic (Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge}). This limit is pointwise, not uniform: near a tie the gain is of order $1/T$, so at
small positive temperature chaos is excluded only on invariant sets that keep a uniform logit gap, and a chaotic set
would have to return to near-ties again and again.
As $T\to\infty$, $p$ tends to the uniform distribution and $\|\Sigma_p\|$ stays bounded, while $J_{\tilde h}$ is
bounded on the convex hull of the embeddings, which contains every soft token. For this smooth map, with a fixed
prefix, fixed steering and a network whose derivative is bounded on that compact hull, $F$ is therefore a contraction
for large enough $T$, with a single attracting fixed point that tends to the mean embedding: here the exclusion is a
bound. It does not cover hard routing or a growing context. Away from near-ties, chaos thus needs temperatures at
which the next-token distribution is spread enough to make $\Sigma_p$ large while $1/T$ still amplifies it. The
unsteered sweeps agree (Figure~\ref{fig:alpha0}). In the four long sweeps (Appendix~\ref{sec:origin}) the chaotic temperatures lie in one band,
interleaved with periodic windows, and every band lies between $\log_{10}T=-0.20$ and $0.28$. Below the bands, all
1547 temperatures swept have negative exponents, and all but five have a detected period; above them, up to the end
of the sweeps at $\log_{10}T=0.3$, 127 of 128 temperatures have a detected period (fixed points at 101) and one has a
negative exponent without one. These are finite sweep findings; the wider sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:sizemain} find chaos
at higher temperatures in some models, and an OLMo-2-32B sweep found three candidate cells below one nat of entropy (at $\log_{10}T=-0.42$, $-0.41$ and $-0.07$; one twin
offset). A further sweep of the unsteered 0.5B map from
$\log_{10}T=0.3$ to $1.5$ finds an attracting fixed point at all 25 temperatures. Its entropy approaches $\log V$
(11.928 against 11.931 nats at $\log_{10}T=1.5$), and its exponent falls steadily, by 2.14 per decade of temperature
at the top of the range, toward the slope $-\ln10\approx-2.30$ expected once $p$ is uniform.

\paragraph{Stability belongs to the loop, not to the step.} The gain condition is necessary, not sufficient. Along
the period-8 orbit of Table~\ref{tab:orbits}, the largest singular value of $DF$ lies between 1.2 and 21 at every
step, so every step expands some direction, and no step is close to rank one: the second singular value is at least
0.15 of the first, and the second eigenvalue of $\Sigma_p$ is at least 0.23 of the first (minimum 0.236). Yet the Jacobian of the whole
cycle, the product of the eight step Jacobians, has norm between 0.059 and 0.644, depending on the phase at which the
cycle is started, against $1.1\times10^{7}$ for the product of the step norms. Its eigenvalues, which do not depend
on that phase, reproduce the multipliers of Table~\ref{tab:orbits} ($0.01104$ and $-2.96\times10^{-7}$; the table
lists moduli), and its second singular value is $1.7\times10^{-6}$ to $2\times10^{-4}$ of its first. An independent
implementation of the model reproduced the factorization (to $8.3\times10^{-14}$), the step singular values, the
signed multipliers and the cycle norms at all eight starting phases. The expansion of each step is undone by the
steps that follow, and the strongly anisotropic linear return of the stripe orbits is a property of the composed
cycle, not of any single step or of the rank of $\Sigma_p$. Nor does the contraction need the actual alignment of
the steps: with independent random (Haar) rotations inserted between the eight step Jacobians, all 16 tested products
contract, and more strongly than the actual cycle (\texttt{tools/alignment\_intervention.py}). Stepwise norm or
spectral summaries can be inconclusive; the ordered product of the step Jacobians captures the cycle's linear
stability.

\paragraph{Steering makes tokens compete.} Steering enters both factors of \eqref{eq:gain}: it moves the logits, and
with them $\Sigma_p$, and it moves the hidden states, and with them $J_{\tilde h}$. Which change makes the loop
chaotic? Three measurements on Qwen2.5-0.5B, steered along the philosophy direction, give one answer
(Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}, Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). First, each step's growth splits exactly into a network
factor $g_{\mathrm{net}}=(|\delta\tilde h|/|\tilde h|)/(|\delta x|/|x|)$ and a readout factor
$g_{\mathrm{read}}=(|\delta F|/|F|)/(|\delta\tilde h|/|\tilde h|)$. Averaged along an orbit, their logarithms add up
to the largest Lyapunov exponent, up to a boundary term. Over a $101\times53$ map in steering strength and temperature
(block 12; 5353 cells, 331 chaotic), the chaotic cells' readout factor exceeds that of nearly unsteered cells
($|\alpha|\le0.01$) at the same temperature by $2.58$ nats per step (95\,\% interval $[1.78,3.59]$ over 18 temperature
rows; 321 chaotic and 162 nearly unsteered cells). Their network factor differs by only $-0.07$ $[-0.23,0.12]$. With
nearly no steering the readout contracts strongly, by $0.87$ to $5.49$ nats per step. In the chaotic cells it expands,
except in the three highest temperature rows ($\log_{10}T=0.175$--$0.225$), where it still contracts but less. Compared with periodic cells at the same temperature,
chaotic cells have a higher next-token entropy ($+1.62$ nats, $[1.26,1.91]$), a smaller gap between the two largest
logits ($-1.37$ in units of $T$, $[-1.98,-0.87]$) and a larger readout factor ($+1.36$, $[0.91,1.93]$), each with
$p\le10^{-4}$ in a permutation test within temperature rows.

Second, a readout-only intervention gives chaos. We add to the logits a bias $\beta\,b$ along the direct logit effect
of the steering vector ($b=Wv$, centred and scaled to unit standard deviation over the vocabulary), with no residual
steering. This leaves the network untouched and changes only which tokens compete. It makes 3.5\,\% of the same
temperature range chaotic (241 of 6837 cells), against 6.2\,\% for residual steering, mostly at
$\log_{10}T>0.1$. Creating token competition therefore suffices for chaos, given the unmodified network.

Third, a gain split compares different orbits, so steering could act simply by moving the orbit to other states. A
crossover at fixed states separates the two contributions along the recorded orbits. Along recorded steered orbits we
evaluated the steered and the unsteered model at the same states and carried four tangent dynamics. Each pairs the
steered or unsteered readout $R(p)$ with the steered or unsteered network Jacobian: arms 11, 10, 01 and 00. The frozen
validation (arm 11 within $0.02$ of the twin exponent at every cell) holds at 23 of 24 cells, all within $0.012$; at
the remaining periodic cell, which contracts at about $-12$ per step, the twin offset falls to the rounding floor. The
frozen sufficiency criterion (arm 10 within $0.1$ of arm 11 on average) fails by overshoot: steering only the readout
gives more growth than the full steered map, by $0.23$, because steering's change of the network damps it. Over 14 held-out chaotic cells, steering only the readout raises the growth by $0.81$ nats per step
($[0.29,1.35]$). Steering only the network response lowers it by $0.49$ ($[-0.63,-0.35]$). The interaction is $+0.26$
($[0.09,0.45]$). Below $\log_{10}T=0$ the steered readout turns a contracting unsteered derivative ($-1.03$ nats per
step) into an expanding one ($+0.72$), more strongly than the full steered map ($+0.34$). Above it, the unsteered
derivative already expands at the steered states ($+0.41$), and steering may act by bringing the orbit there. In
Qwen2.5-7B, whose embeddings are untied, the same crossover (18 cells of its steered map at block 14; arm 11 equals the
twin exponent within $10^{-4}$ at every cell) reproduces the damping. Over its 10 held-out chaotic cells, steering only
the network response lowers the growth by $1.14$ nats per step ($[-1.61,-0.64]$), and steering only the readout adds
$+0.23$ ($[-0.11,0.63]$). Eight of the ten lie at $\log_{10}T\ge0$, where the unsteered derivative already expands at
the steered states (mean $+1.33$). The crossover along two more directions of the 0.5B model gives the same
signs. For gratitude the readout effect is $+0.33$ ($[0.07,0.59]$) and the network effect $-0.14$ ($[-0.29,0.01]$). For
a random direction matched in norm to the philosophy vector, they are $+0.77$ ($[0.47,1.12]$) and $-0.09$
($[-0.22,0.06]$); 14 held-out chaotic cells each. The readout effect is positive along all three directions, and the
network effect is negative but compatible with zero along these two. In these three directions the readout effect
does not depend on the direction's meaning.

Along the sampled orbits, then, steering makes the loop chaotic mainly by making next tokens compete, which turns the
softmax readout from a contraction into an expansion. At low temperature it creates that competition at fixed states.
At higher temperature the orbit lies in states that are already competitive, consistent with steering having carried it
there. Along the philosophy direction, in both models, its change of the network's response damps the growth. These are
conditional comparisons on recorded trajectories; they do not establish that the readout change alone would sustain
the same attractor. The readout rule holds below $\log_{10}T\approx0.1$; at higher temperature the network factor itself
can expand. Steered at block 4, the chaotic
cells at $\log_{10}T=0.15$ have a network factor of $+0.72$ and a contracting readout ($-0.35$). Along a line of the hostility-steered map (Section~\ref{sec:hostile})
at $\log_{10}T=0.12$--$0.13$ the network factor of the chaotic cells is $+0.60$ against $+0.24$ to $+0.38$ at the
neighbouring periodic cells. The mechanism is not specific to
concept-labelled directions. This is consistent with the controls of Appendix~\ref{sec:origin}, in which norm-matched random directions also produce chaos in the 0.5B and 8B models, and rotating the steering direction at constant norm moves the chaotic regions but keeps the chaotic share between 13 and 24\,\% at every one of 103 angles (Figure~\ref{fig:morph}); at 70B the directions differ
(Appendix~\ref{sec:size}). What a direction does select is which tokens compete, and therefore what the attractors
write.

\paragraph{Why low-dimensional structure can appear.} Near a bifurcation of a periodic orbit the centre manifold
theorem reduces the dynamics exactly to the critical directions, so the normal forms of folds, flips and cusps apply
in any dimension. Near most orbits we computed the non-critical multipliers are small, from about $0.1$ at short periods to
below $10^{-13}$ along the cascade. This transverse contraction motivates scalar descriptions of whole attractors,
which need additional, global evidence; where the next multiplier is not small (up to $0.4$ along the period-5 curves
of Section~\ref{sec:organization}, and $0.28$ and $0.38$ in a high-temperature bubble of Section~\ref{sec:cascade}), such a description is only a
rough guide. Coexisting attracting orbits constrain such descriptions: a
smooth unimodal interval map with negative Schwarzian derivative has at most one attracting periodic orbit unless a
basin reaches an endpoint \cite{singer1978stable}, so coexistence calls for a second turning point, a stretch of
positive Schwarzian derivative or a second dimension (Appendix~\ref{sec:theorymore}).

\section{Methods in brief}
\label{sec:methodsbrief}
Results on the soft-token map labelled float64 are computed in float64, including the normalizations and rotary
tables that the library would otherwise compute in float32; two exceptions, float32 storage of some diagnostic step
records and a float32 final normalization in the two-GPU runs of 70B-class models before 14 October, are marked where they occur (Appendix~\ref{sec:methods} and Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}). A cell of a phase map is chaotic when the
largest finite-horizon Lyapunov exponent of a twin trajectory (offset $10^{-6}$, renormalized every step; 300
measured steps after 300 transient ones unless stated) exceeds $10^{-3}$ and no period up to 32 (48 in later zooms) is
found; at 448 checked cells exact tangents reproduce the twin exponents within $0.0041$, and the label is a finite-horizon diagnostic, not a proof.
Periodic orbits are solved by Newton's method with forward-mode derivatives; branches are continued by pseudo-arclength
continuation and fold and flip curves by minimally augmented systems \cite{govaerts2000numerical,kuznetsov2023elements};
cusps and other codimension-two points are located by bracketing sign changes along the curves; and the normal forms
of folds are computed from the network's derivatives without fitting. Appendix~\ref{sec:methods} gives the details
and the checks on maps with known answers.

\section{What the chaos does to inference and steering}
\label{sec:consequences}
The loop studied here is the expected-embedding feedback that continuous reasoning methods such as Soft Thinking use
\cite{zhang2025softthinking}, and its chaos has consequences that can be measured without any reference to dynamical
systems. We report three: whether a run can be reproduced, how the depth at which a steering vector enters sets the
chaos it causes, and what happens when two steering vectors are combined. A fourth, that a repeating text can hide a
chaotic state from anyone who reads only the output, is shown in Section~\ref{sec:hostile}.

\paragraph{The regime decides whether a run can be reproduced.} Language-model inference is not bitwise
reproducible: GPU kernels choose different reduction orders for different matrix shapes, so a request's result depends
on the batch it shares and on the hardware \cite{he2025nondeterminism,yuan2025nondeterminism}. We ran the 448 labelled
cells of the horizon check (192 chaotic, 192 periodic and 64 other cells of two float64 maps; Appendix~\ref{sec:methods})
twice in lockstep from the same start. The two runs differ only in the batch: all cells in one batch of $n$ rows, or
the same rows followed by $n$ further rows whose steering vectors are those of the first $n$ in reverse order, at the
original temperatures ($2n$ rows). This changes the matrix shapes the kernels see; the first $n$ rows compute the same
cells (Figure~\ref{fig:repro}, Table~\ref{tab:repro}). In float64 the two runs first differ by about
$6\times10^{-15}$, the rounding level. At 190 of the 192 chaotic cells this difference passes a relative distance of
$10^{-2}$, after a median of 102 steps, and grows on to order one (median final distance $0.91$); at 173 of the 192
chaotic cells the most probable token of the loop's readout differs between the runs at some step of the last 500. At
182 of the 192 periodic cells the two runs stay within $10^{-2}$ for all 1500 steps, with a median largest distance of
$2.3\times10^{-12}$, and 182 of the 192 periodic cells end the run within $3\times10^{-12}$. The Lyapunov exponent
accounts for when a chaotic run diverges: the step at which the distance passes $10^{-2}$ follows
$\ln(10^{-2}/d_1)/\lambda$, where $d_1$ is the smallest nonzero distance in the run, with a median ratio of $0.91$
and a log-log correlation of $0.73$ over 190 cells. This uses the whole run and is therefore retrospective; an
independent recomputation with the first nonzero distance instead gives the same rounded values. The two chaotic
cells that never diverge have exponents of $0.013$ and $0.0013$, for which the predicted step lies beyond the run.
The same holds without steering in Qwen2.5-7B, whose embeddings are untied. Over 24 chaotic and 24 periodic
temperatures of its unsteered loop, all 24 chaotic float64 runs diverge, after a median of 56 steps (the exponent again
accounts for the step, median ratio $0.95$), and at 23 of them the readout's most probable token differs at some step
of the last 500. None of the 24 periodic runs moves past $10^{-2}$; 22 end within $1.3\times10^{-13}$ of each other
and all within $2.2\times10^{-12}$. A second model family, unsteered Llama-3.1-8B, gives the same picture: all 20
chaotic temperatures diverge after a median of 68 steps (median ratio to the exponent's estimate $1.05$), all 20 differ
in their readout's most probable token in the last 500 steps, and 19 of the 20 periodic ones stay within
$10^{-2}$. At 70B the picture is unchanged. In Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct steered along hostility-vs-kindness at block 20
(float64 arithmetic on its stored bfloat16 weights, layers spread over two GPUs, 1000 steps), all 24 chaotic cells
diverge, after a median of 114.5 steps (median ratio to the exponent's estimate $0.83$, log-log correlation $0.74$),
and at 21 of them the readout's most probable token differs at some step of the last 300. None of the 24 periodic cells moves past $10^{-2}$, and all end within
$1.3\times10^{-12}$. In lower precision the rounding is
larger. In float32 every chaotic cell of both models diverges, after a median of 20 steps (0.5B) or 16 (7B). In
bfloat16, a common serving precision, the two layouts pass a distance of $10^{-2}$ after a median of one or two steps
at every cell of the two Qwen models and at 38 of the 40 Llama cells, periodic ones included. With these kernels and at
that precision, the loop was not reproducible across batch layouts in any regime we tested.

The same test at the level of answers, with a design frozen before the run, shows what this means for a task. We
asked Qwen2.5-0.5B 96 held-out two-digit additions through a short soft-thinking pipeline: 32 expected-embedding steps
appended to a growing context, then a fixed answer cue and greedy extraction of the number. Each problem ran alone and
in a batch with three fixed distractor problems of the same length, so nothing is padded and only the matrix shapes
differ. In bfloat16 the extracted answer changed with the batch layout in 56\,\% of the problems at $T=1$ and in
8\,\% at $T=10^{0.2}$. A forced greedy baseline with the same budget (32 greedy tokens, then the same cue) produced
identical token sequences in both layouts for every problem. The excess disagreement of soft over greedy decoding,
averaged over the two temperatures, is 32.3 percentage points (95\,\% interval $[26.5,38.1]$; sign-flip $p=10^{-4}$,
the smallest attainable). In float64 no answer changed within the 32 steps. There, the median growth of an injected
kick to the first soft token is $0.033$ nats per step at $T=1$ (range $-0.13$ to $0.24$ over six problems and three
kick sizes), and even a relative kick of $10^{-4}$ changes no answer. The bfloat16 discrepancies therefore do not
require the float64 map's chaos; they are consistent with bfloat16's rounding differences accumulating through
continuous feedback, which a discrete argmax would absorb. (The forced greedy baseline's accuracy is zero and not
informative: forced through 32 tokens, the model writes past its answer, and the cue then extracts another number.)
The same frozen test in Qwen2.5-7B, whose soft-thinking pipeline answers 51 to 64\,\% of these problems, replicates
the result. In bfloat16 the batch layout changed the soft-thinking answer in 53 of the 96 problems (55\,\%) at
$T=10^{0.2}$, the temperature at which the soft trace still matters. At this precision even the forced greedy baseline
changed in 10 of the 96, the known batch-variance effect. The excess over greedy decoding is 17.7 points
($[9.3,25.6]$, $p=2\times10^{-4}$). In float64, at that temperature, the 7B's loop amplifies an injected perturbation
(median $+0.034$ nats per step), injected kicks change answers (2 of 18 kicks: six problems and three kick sizes), and
the rounding-level difference between the layouts changed 5\,\% of the answers within the 32 steps.
Two horizons thus matter. Within a short budget, continuous feedback in low precision makes answers depend on the
batch, and where the loop expands, even float64 rounding does. Over longer runs, a chaotic regime amplifies float64
rounding into different readout tokens, on the schedule its Lyapunov exponent sets.

An exploratory causal test locates the low-precision effect in the loop. Following a suggestion of an external review, we reran
the 0.5B pipeline at $T=1$ on 128 fresh additions, after a batch-1 reference run of each (exploratory; first on a
workstation GPU, where the native pipeline's answers changed with the layout in 85\,\% of the problems, against
56\,\% on the H100 of the frozen test). In the batch of four, two arms start from the reference's prompt state and
are answered by the same batch-1 decoder. In the free arm the soft phase runs on its own; in the reset arm the
target's cache and logits are replaced by the reference's before every soft step, so that each step runs in the batch
but only one at a time. In bfloat16 the answer changed in 102 of the 128 problems (80\,\%) in the free arm and in 14
(11\,\%) in the reset arm, a difference of 69 points (95\,\% interval $[60,77]$; 90 discordant pairs against 2, exact
McNemar $p=2\times10^{-24}$), while accuracy stayed between 23 and 25\,\% in every arm. One batched step moves the
target's logits by a median $1.5\,\%$ of their norm; in the free arm the median difference grows to $0.79$ of it by
step 32, while the reset arm's median per step stays between $1.3$ and $3.1\,\%$. In float32 the free arm's drift grows about 140-fold over the
32 steps but stays near $10^{-4}$, and 4 of the 128 answers changed (none with reset). The test replicates on an H100
and in Qwen2.5-7B. On the H100, in bfloat16, the answer changed in 85 of the 128 problems (66\,\%) in the free arm and

decides low-precision answers because the loop carries each step's rounding forward and amplifies it, not because the
last steps alone are fragile; 128 problems per arm do not show that accuracy is unchanged.

\paragraph{Where a steering vector enters decides how much chaos it causes.} Steering vectors are a common tool for
controlling and probing models \cite{zou2023representation,rimsky2024steering}, usually added at one layer chosen by
hand. In Qwen2.5-0.5B the philosophy direction, built with the same recipe at blocks 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 and scaled to
each block's residual norm, makes 5.9, 8.1, 6.8, 5.0 and 2.3\,\% of the same $128\times128$ plane chaotic
(Figure~\ref{fig:layersweep}). Early steering carries the chaos down to $\log_{10}T=-0.95$ (block 8) and $-0.91$
(block 4), while at block 20 the chaotic cells lie between $\log_{10}T=-0.09$ and $0.22$, three fifths of them above
$0.1$. In the strip $|\alpha|<0.02$ every block gives 0.8 to 2.1\,\%. Llama-3.3-70B behaves the same way. Steered along its hostility direction (a contrastive valence direction; Section~\ref{sec:hostile}), it is chaotic in 8.0\,\%
of a $24\times24$ map at block 20 of 80, down to $\log_{10}T=-0.14$, against 4.3\,\% at block 35, where the chaos starts
at $\log_{10}T=0.01$, 4.3\,\% at block 50 (32 by 32 cells, a two-GPU map from before the precision fix of Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}), and 0.7\,\% at block 65, all above
$\log_{10}T=0.19$. These 70B labels are those of the map
protocol, one twin offset, and so are candidates. A logit bias, the latest possible point of entry, gives chaos mostly
above $\log_{10}T=0.1$ (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The gain split of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, repeated along
steering lines at all five blocks of the 0.5B model, shows where the depth enters. At low temperature
($\log_{10}T\le0$) the chaotic cells of blocks 4, 8 and 12 have expanding readout factors (medians $+0.74$, $+0.46$ and
$+0.46$ nats per step over 54, 56 and 21 cells) and contracting network factors ($-0.47$ to $-0.21$). At block 16
the readout factor of such cells is only $+0.15$, and block 20 gives just 6 of them. Early injection creates token
competition at low temperature more readily; late injection mostly does not. Its chaos lives at higher temperature,
where the network factor carries it (block 20: network $+1.09$, readout $-0.78$; Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}e).

\paragraph{Does a chaotic loop predict worse answers in ordinary decoding? Not beyond the model's own uncertainty.}
Two exploratory designs found no accuracy signature of the regimes that survives controls
(Appendix~\ref{sec:behaviour}). A frozen test then labelled 4000 fresh products of two two-digit numbers by the regime
of the unsteered Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct's own loop at $\log_{10}T=0.40$. Chaotic-loop prompts are answered less
accurately by ordinary sampling, by 7.1 points (95\,\% interval $[4.8,9.3]$), but the gap vanishes once seven
difficulty covariates, fixed before sampling, enter the fit (label coefficient $+0.15$ points, $[-1.4,1.7]$,
permutation $p=0.84$), and in a post hoc fit the first-token entropy alone absorbs nearly all of it. This is what the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} predicts: the loop turns chaotic where next tokens compete, and a model unsure of its first
token also answers worse. Within a prompt no association is detected either, and accuracy does not peak at the edge of
chaos: deep periodic, edge and deep chaotic prompts are answered alike, with the intervals of all three adjusted contrasts within $[-3.1,3.0]$ points. These are nulls at this precision; they do not show that the regime has no effect.

\section{Mechanism: a saddle-node bottleneck}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\paragraph{The fold.} At $\log_{10}T=-0.115$ the 2-cycle ``is it'' dies in a fold at $\alpha_c=0.06279827$ (float64
multiplier $1$ to $3\times10^{-13}$, next modulus $0.037$). In the critical coordinate $u$ of the second iterate $G=F^2$, the fold's normal form is
$u'=u+a\eps+bu^2$ with $\eps=\alpha-\alpha_c$; here $a=-4.834$ per unit $\alpha$ and $b=-25.4285$, so $ab=122.93$. The stable branch gives an independent estimate: extrapolating its multipliers
through $(1-\mu)^2/(4(\alpha_c-\alpha))\to ab$ yields $123.1$ with a quadratic fit ($120.6$ with a linear one). An
independent implementation of the forward map and solver recovers $\alpha_c$ to $6\times10^{-15}$ and $a$ to
$3\times10^{-12}$. Its $b$, from nested automatic differentiation, is $-25.42846$; our finite-difference value
differs from it by $2.2\times10^{-6}$ relatively, which is the finite-step bias of the difference table. The
resulting $ab=122.927$ leaves every passage prediction of Table~\ref{tab:passages} unchanged.

\section{A period-doubling cascade to chaos}
\label{sec:cascade}
The crescent of Figure~\ref{fig:cross512} is a closed region in which the period-3 loop ``\textbackslash
n\textbackslash nThe existence'' doubles inward. Along the cut $\log_{10}T=-0.14798$, the map's cells show period 3
over 25 columns, period 6 over about 14, period 12 over 4 and period 24 in one column, followed by a chaotic core. The
doubled loops repeat the same leading tokens twice and four times, with alternating probabilities. Locating the flips
along this cut in float64 (Appendix~\ref{sec:methods}), in the steering coordinate $\alpha$, gives
Table~\ref{tab:cascade}. The first two ratios, $\delta_2=2.844$ and $\delta_3=2.859$ (indexed as in
Table~\ref{tab:cascade}), are far from Feigenbaum's $4.6692$, even though from the second flip on the orbits are
effectively one-dimensional (next modulus $0.0023$, $2\times10^{-6}$, $2\times10^{-12}$). The next ratio,
$\delta_4=3.781$, is markedly larger. The first four flip parameters agree between completed float64 computations on a
CPU and an H100 to $4\times10^{-15}$.

Continuing a just-born doubled orbit past period 48 failed: Newton's method slides onto the half-period orbit, which
is also periodic with the doubled period. We therefore also located the superstable parameters $s_p$, where the
critical multiplier vanishes in the middle of each band (Table~\ref{tab:superstable}). There the orbit attracts, so it
can be reached by forward iteration and cannot slide onto its half-period parent. Through period 768 the superstable
ratios are $3.19$, $4.02$, $4.478$, $4.625$ and $4.660$. The last three come from parameters resolved to $10^{-14}$ and
differ from Feigenbaum's value by $0.19$, $0.044$ and $0.0097$; from $4.02$ on, the distance shrinks by factors of
$3.4$, $4.3$ and $4.6$.

directly. The accumulation point lies

Why are the early ratios so small? We first considered a crossover. Softmax at temperature $T$ is a smoothed argmax,
so the reduced one-dimensional map might have a nearly corner-like extremum, rounded only on a scale set by $T$. Early
doublings would then see the corner, where ratios tend toward 2, and later ones the smooth quadratic core. This
predicts smaller early ratios at lower temperature. We fixed that prediction before locating the flips along two
lower cuts, and it fails. At $\log_{10}T=-0.156$ the first ratio is $14.93$, five times its value at $-0.148$
(Table~\ref{tab:cascade156}). At $-0.152$ the continued period-12 solution collapsed onto its period-6 parent near
$\alpha\approx0.0657$, and the forward search found no next flip. This is compatible with a doubling bubble, but we
did not locate its reverse boundary or exclude a narrower cascade on that cut. The early ratios thus depend strongly
on where a cut crosses the crescent, and we withdraw the crossover explanation. The later ratios at $-0.156$ behave as
universality requires. The next ratios are $3.124$, $4.4988$, $4.6208$, $4.6618$, $4.6673$ and $4.6688$. Their
deviations from $\delta=4.6692$ are $-1.55$, $-0.17$, $-0.048$, $-0.0074$, $-0.0019$ and $-0.00036$, and from the third
on each step closes the gap by a factor of 3.5 to 6.5. The last ratio uses the flip of the period-768 orbit (the
$768\to1536$ flip), located with $|\mu+1|=4.1\times10^{-7}$. The penultimate flip, of the period-384 orbit,
approaches the precision floor of its multiplier, a product of 384 step Jacobians. On an H100 the computed $\mu+1$
scatters by $1.8\times10^{-5}$ about a straight line near the root; under that local linear fit, the largest residual
divided by the slope gives a location uncertainty of about $\pm3.3\times10^{-13}$, an estimate rather than a
certified bound. The workstation GPU located it at
$\alpha=0.06510347354866666$ with $|\mu+1|=4\times10^{-9}$, $9\times10^{-14}$ from the H100 estimate. Over that range the
ratio $\delta_7$ lies between $4.6672$ and $4.6674$, and $\delta_8$, the last ratio, between $4.6685$ and $4.6692$. The
superstable points along the same cut, through period 384, give the ratios $10.21$, $3.713$, $4.572$, $4.643$ and
$4.6646$. Before computing them we fixed the prediction that the last two superstable ratios at a new temperature
would lie within 5\,\% of $\delta$, in $[4.436,4.902]$. At the original readout, through period 192, the point
estimates $4.572$ and $4.643$ met it, but the bracket envelope of $4.572$ did not; extending the computation to period
384 later gave the pair $4.643$ and $4.6646$, both inside the interval. The last, $4.6646$, rests on parameters resolved to $5\times10^{-15}$ and
differs from $\delta$ by $0.1\,\%$. Over the corners of the brackets the one before lies in $[4.639,4.647]$,
limited by $s_{48}$, whose bracket is $2.4\times10^{-9}$ wide. The earlier ratio $4.572$ depends on $s_{24}$, known only

\section{Steering toward hostility}
\label{sec:hostile}
A second steering direction tests whether the organization of the philosophy-steered map is specific to one
concept. We steer along a valence direction, the contrast of hostile statements against matched kind ones, built
in the manner of contrastive activation addition \cite{rimsky2024steering}; its cosine with the gratitude
direction is $-0.04$. Sampled self-dialogue under the same steering is a different system, with a growing context
and sampling noise; we report that study separately and follow the direction here in the deterministic map.

\paragraph{The hostility direction in the deterministic map.} Steering the soft-token map of
Section~\ref{sec:map} along the same hostility direction gives a plane with chaos (a positive largest exponent in
10\,\% of a coarse $128\times192$ float32 scan) and with periodic text loops that are hostile: ``Yes, that's crap''
(period 6), ``a lie is'' (3), ``STUP\ldots'' (3), a loop on the Chinese word for insult and its 2-cycle, and others.
They appear at $\alpha$ above about $0.035$ (the insult loop) and above $0.065$ (the rest). Around the period-6 loop
the windows form a dome (Figure~\ref{fig:hostile-cascade}), and toward higher temperature its texts gain one ``!Yes''
after another (periods $6,8,\ldots,20$). In float64 the windows of periods 12 to 20 are born in folds on their
low-temperature side and end in flips or $+1$ crossings on the other, like the stripes of Sections~\ref{sec:adding}
and~\ref{sec:mechanism}. The period-6 window ends in a fold at $\log_{10}T=-0.0991$, and the continuations of the
period-8 and period-10 windows failed to converge.

The windows accumulate on the fold of the 2-cycle ``!Yes'' at $\log_{10}T_c=-0.0462465357$ ($\alpha=0.083$; critical
multiplier $0.9999999954$, next modulus $0.050$). From period 16 on, $1/\sqrt{\log_{10}T_c-\log_{10}T_{\rm fold}}$
rises by $2.235$ per lap. The fold's normal form ($a=-1.168$, $b=44.53$ with $\varepsilon$ in $\log_{10}T$) predicts
$\sqrt{|ab|}/\pi=2.296$ per lap for full passages through the bottleneck, within 3\,\% and without a fitted
parameter. An independent float64 forward and automatic-differentiation implementation reproduces this fold
(location to $6\times10^{-10}$ relative, a simple real $+1$ multiplier with next modulus $0.050$) and its coefficients
($ab$ to 8 parts per million). In that implementation, controlled passages from fixed entrances at $\log_{10}T$
offsets $-10^{-5}$, $-3\times10^{-5}$ and $-10^{-4}$ took 119/59, 61/31 and 27/13 iterates of $F^2$ (full
channel/from its midpoint), against prospectively frozen quadratic forecasts of 118/61, 61/32 and 27/14. Five of the
six are within one iterate; the smallest-offset midpoint missed by two, failing the frozen all-six criterion.
Finite-endpoint (arctan) forecasts, also frozen, were within $0.923$ iterates. These are controlled local transits. At
the window distances the finite-channel correction to the asymptotic passage time is large, so the agreement of the
window slope with the full-passage coefficient does not by itself show where the natural dynamics reinjects. The same
type-I intermittency mechanism as in the philosophy-steered map is thus consistent with the hostile windows'
organization, which the local fold normal form supports; the controlled passages do not identify the natural
reinjection or the global organization.

A second region, near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.06,0.20)$, shows arms of period-7 and period-8 windows spiralling around
the insult loop. In float64 the insult fixed point doubles into a 2-cycle at $\alpha\approx0.041$ and $0.079$, and
that 2-cycle has a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation in the spiral region: at $\alpha=0.062$ its complex pair crosses the
unit circle near $\log_{10}T=0.1656$, turning $0.12$ of a revolution per step of the 2-cycle. This does not explain
the arms. The two invariant circles born at a 2-cycle's Neimark--Sacker point are exchanged by the map, so orbits
locked on them have even periods, near 16 for this rotation, whereas the arms include period 7; and of four float32
arm cells, only one kept its period in float64. In dissipative flows, windows wind in nested spirals around
periodicity hubs \cite{vitolo2011hubs}, and in the R\"ossler system the hub's shape follows from a conjugacy to
unimodal maps \cite{jones2012rossler}; we have not tested whether the arms here wind around such a hub. Float64 zooms
show more structure. In one arm a period-7 domain
carries period-14 crescents with period-28 cores along its lower edge, between period-1 and period-2 regions and a
chaotic band. In the cascade the windows are nested crescent arcs inside a dome bounded by the ``!Yes'' 2-cycle
region, and near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.087,-0.069)$ the period-12 and period-14 windows bend into hooked, nested
arches. These are the map-level features that accompany shrimp (a periodic body with doubling crescents; hooked
windows).

In float64 the period-7 body is bounded by folds. At $\log_{10}T=0.1925$ the orbit exists between $\alpha=0.065966$
and $0.071188$, and at $\alpha=0.066$ it ends in a fold at $\log_{10}T=0.19263$. Below that, the multiplier passes
through $-1$ twice (at $0.1919$ and $0.1899$), so the period-14 crescent is a doubling bubble. Further down, the
branch folds twice ($0.18797$ and $0.18921$, with three coexisting period-7 orbits in between) before a third flip.
All crossings are simple (next modulus below $1.5\times10^{-3}$). An S-shaped pair of folds is what a cusp of the
fold curves leaves on a nearby cut. When these boundaries are continued in the plane in float64
(Figure~\ref{fig:hubshrimp}), each of the two fold curves reverses its parameter tangent once. There the oriented
quadratic coefficient $b$ of the fold's normal form passes through zero (the cusp test, with the cubic coefficient $c$
and the unfolding determinant $\det J$, is defined in Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}) (upper fold: $-23.9$ to $+244.6$ between
adjacent points, with $a\approx2.0$; lower fold: $+31.2$ to $-58.7$, with $a\approx0.55$) while $a$ stays away from
zero. Located along the curves, the two cusps lie at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.067213,0.198983)$ and
$(0.066611,0.189382)$. There $b\operatorname{sign}a$ is below $1.3\times10^{-5}$ of its bracket values,
$c=-1.41\times10^{6}$ and $-5.61\times10^{5}$, and $\det J=58.2$ and $55.2$, all evaluated at the located points.
The flip curve of the period-14 bubble lies inside the body and closes around the map's period-14 cells. The cubic
coefficient is nonzero at the points that bracket both sign changes: $c\approx-1.4\times10^{6}$ to $-2.0\times10^{6}$ (step agreement $5\,\%$; the
smallest steps converge like the square of the step, and extrapolation leaves the sign) and $-5.8\times10^{5}$ to
$-5.3\times10^{5}$. This supports nondegenerate cusps inside both brackets, and $c<0$ agrees with the three
coexisting period-7 orbits, two of them stable, found between the S-shaped folds. At a fold point near the lower
cusp (where $b=31.4$) the estimated unfolding is nonsingular too ($\det J=53.5$ in the same coordinates, singular
values $262$ and $0.20$). Its local predictions at six frozen offsets matched the orbits that Newton solves seeded at
the predicted roots and the base state recovered: in number at all six and in stability at five. The miss, at the largest
offset beyond the edge of the region with three orbits, marks the range of the cubic model. The hostile period-7 body
thus has the same skeleton as the period-6 window of Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}.

Its interior is shrimp-like too. At $\log_{10}T=0.1830$, float64 bifurcation diagrams through the body
(Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade}; 1400 cells per line) show the period-7 orbit doubling to periods 14 and 28 and entering
chaos (largest exponent up to $+0.1$) for $\alpha\approx0.0659$--$0.0673$. Windows of periods 28, 35 and 42 sit
inside the chaotic band, and the orbit then undoubles through 28 and 14 back to 7; the exponent touches zero at every
doubling. Newton's method converges at map cells to primitive orbits of periods 14, 28 and 21 (residuals
$3\times10^{-15}$, $2\times10^{-14}$ and $5\times10^{-13}$; spectral radii $0.08$, $4\times10^{-4}$ and $0.006$).
Periods 21, 35 and 42 are three, five and six times the body's period: the satellite windows expected in the chaotic
bands of a period-7 cascade. Continued in temperature, the period-14 orbit flips into period 28 near
$\log_{10}T=0.1841$ and back near $0.1857$, the two ends of a doubling bubble that holds period 28 and chaos. Lower
down, its branch is S-shaped (folds near $0.1824$ and $0.18244$, with three coexisting period-14 orbits between them)
before another flip near $0.1819$. All these crossings are simple (next modulus below $4\times10^{-5}$). A $160^2$
float64 zoom of the lower body (Figure~\ref{fig:hubzoom}) shows this anatomy in the plane. The period-14 domain
encloses a period-28 ring around a chaotic core. Period-21 windows sit in the chaotic region as thin crescents with
legs, and curves of very negative exponent, where the orbits are superstable (the leading multiplier crosses zero),
run through the windows, as superstable lines run through the windows of low-dimensional maps. In this zoom
18\,\% of cells are chaotic.

\paragraph{No one-dimensional shrimp centre.} In a shrimp of a one-dimensional map, two superstable curves of the
window's orbit cross at a doubly superstable centre, the head \cite{gallas1994dissecting,facanha2013endoskeletons}. We continued
one period-7 orbit over a $31\times31$ grid of the body's upper part and followed the sign of its leading multiplier
(Figure~\ref{fig:superstable}). Its superstable curves form two arcs that approach each other near
$(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0700,0.206)$ and turn away without crossing at this resolution ($10^{-4}$ in $\alpha$,
$4.3\times10^{-4}$ in $\log_{10}T$). A finer grid of that approach (1155 cells, about 2 times finer in $\alpha$ and 6
times finer in $\log_{10}T$) shows why. There the leading multiplier is tiny (median modulus $9\times10^{-4}$) and as small as the next
one, and in 221 cells the two form a complex pair, in two wedges that flank the gap. On this sampled approach the two
computed leading multipliers exchange dominance and form a complex pair, so the sign of a leading real multiplier no
longer defines a scalar superstable curve there. Where a one-dimensional map would place its doubly superstable centre,
the orbit's local linearization has two comparable directions; whether the dynamics there reduces to an effectively
two-dimensional map is not tested. Next to the body, the 7-step return maps of chaotic cells have a coarse
one-extremum shape in every phase: one interior extremum when the points are binned into 16 intervals, while finer
partitions keep extra extrema, especially in phases 4 and 5, which we do not attribute to noise. In two phases the
points split into two thin strands, an observation of the projection that does not establish a second invariant
dimension (Figure~\ref{fig:returnmapsbody}). Cobwebs on these maps show the doubling cascade and the chaos as on a
one-humped interval map (Figure~\ref{fig:cobwebs}). On the tracked branch and in the sampled region, the body is
therefore not the shrimp of a one-dimensional map. In the two-dimensional theory of Fa\c{c}anha et al.\ the head is
not generic (Section~\ref{sec:organization}); arcs that turn away resemble its Type~B unfolding, but they follow the
sign of the leading real multiplier, not the trace that the theory uses, so this is a comparison, not a test.

\paragraph{Chaos behind a repeating text.} Along the temperature line of Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade} (left), a longer
test (2000 measured steps after 1000 transient steps, twins at offsets $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$, whose exponents agree
to within 0.0031 in every chaotic cell) finds 182 chaotic cells among 1400 between $\log_{10}T=0.176$ and $0.191$. In
65 of them the leading token repeats exactly for all 2000 steps while the state moves chaotically; in 59 of these the
period of the text is a multiple of 7, most often 7 or 28. Figure~\ref{fig:hiddenbands} shows the strongest case
($\log_{10}T=0.18995$, largest exponent 0.091 at both offsets). Its 2000 sampled states fall into seven separated
groups, one per phase of the text, visited in a fixed order and at least 0.098 of the state norm apart (a property of
the samples, not a proof of separated invariant sets), and each group lies inside one cell of the partition by leading
token, so the text \texttt{I!Yes\ heels$\hookleftarrow\hookleftarrow$pee$\hookleftarrow\hookleftarrow$}
repeats at every one of the 2000 steps we followed. Inside a group the sampled states lie close to a curve (a
strongly anisotropic cloud). Read once per repetition through the probability of the last token, the sampled return
relation is well approximated by a smooth map with a single quadratic maximum, the shape of the logistic map: a
polynomial of degree 8 fits it to $4\times10^{-4}$ of its range, and the fitted map's Lyapunov exponent, 0.647 per
repetition, is within 2\,\% of the full map's 0.636 per seven steps, measured with twin trajectories in all 896
dimensions. The scalar approximation holds across most of the cascade. Rerun with the leading-token sequences saved, 62 of the 65 cells are again
chaotic behind a repeating text, and in every one of them several phases of the text (a median of ten) give
single-valued return maps; in 59 cells the best-fitting phase has a single interior stationary point (a maximum in
36, a minimum in 23). If the dynamics were exactly one-dimensional and each phase's probability a smooth invertible
coordinate on it, all single-valued phases would give the same asymptotic exponent, since exponents do not change
under such a change of coordinates. We therefore
fit each phase's map by a polynomial whose degree is chosen by cross-validation over random folds of the return-map
pairs, and take the median over the phases. It
matches the full map's exponent within 10\,\% in 51 of the 62 cells (median discrepancy 3.7\,\%; within 10\,\% in 23
of the 24 cells with seven-token text, which give 285 repetitions in 2000 steps; Figure~\ref{fig:reduction}). This
analysis was adopted after we inspected an outlier of our first, pre-specified analysis (a fixed degree of 8 at the
single best-fitting phase), which gave 49 of 62 and underfitted one cell whose maps are steep and have two turning
points; we report both. An exploratory temporal split (fit on the first 60\,\% of each record, test on the last
30\,\%) gives small value-prediction errors at the best scalar phase, chosen on the test segment itself, but in 30 of
61 cells two- or three-delay predictors improve the held-out error by 20\,\% or more, which the pre-stated rule counts
against scalar sufficiency. These results support an empirical scalar description, not a proved one-dimensional
reduction: a low-residual fit does not show that the observable is invertible, and even exact agreement of exponents
would not establish a one-dimensional invariant curve (two uncoupled logistic maps observed through one coordinate
agree in the same way). An independent implementation reproduced the strongest cell (the same leading token at all
2000 steps; exponent 0.089 against our 0.091; scalar discrepancy $+2.3\,\%$ under the first rule and $+1.5\,\%$
under the amended one) and both cross-cell counts (49 and 51 of 62). At two further cells it chose, it found good
agreement after the amendment in one (text period 10, $+0.2\,\%$, although its fitted maps have both a maximum and a
minimum) and a persistent discrepancy of about 18\,\% in the other (period 28), despite a small value error. These
are the chaotic bands that follow a period-doubling cascade, and a smooth unimodal map is the setting of Feigenbaum's
universality \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}; a fitted maximum on the sampled range does not by itself establish
that theorem's hypotheses, and we have shown the scalar approximation for this cascade only. A reader of the text sees a loop. Only the probabilities, or the state, reveal the
chaos.

\section{Cusps and shrimp-like windows}
\label{sec:organization}
The fold of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} is one boundary of one window. Continuing such boundaries through the
parameter plane, and following the orbits inside them, shows how the windows of the maps are organized, and which
parts of that organization are low-dimensional. Appendices~\ref{sec:curves} and~\ref{sec:hostility} report the
continued curves and cusps of the period-4, -5 and -6 windows in full, and Section~\ref{sec:hostile} those of the
period-7 body; this section collects what they establish about the shape of the windows, after
the cascade of Section~\ref{sec:cascade} and the hostile windows of Section~\ref{sec:hostile}.

\paragraph{Fold curves with cusps.} Fold and flip curves continued in float64 with the minimally augmented systems of
Appendix~\ref{sec:methods} keep their critical multipliers at $+1$ and $-1$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}). The fold
curve of the 2-cycle ``is it'' traces the boundary of the period-2 region on which the stripes accumulate, as the
mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} requires. The fold curves of a period-6
window, of a hostile period-7 window, of a period-4 window and of a period-5 window steered at two depths bend back
on themselves. On the nine fold curves we continued, all thirteen reversals of the parameter tangent are located
cusps: the oriented quadratic coefficient of the fold's normal form changes sign there,
while the cubic coefficient stays nonzero and the two-parameter unfolding is regular. This is not a global count of
cusps. Near a cusp with negative cubic coefficient, three orbits of the same period coexist between the two fold
branches, two of them stable. At a fold point near one cusp in each of two windows, the local cubic model, frozen
before solving, matched the number of orbits that Newton solves seeded at its predicted roots recovered at all twelve
parameter offsets, and their stability at eleven; the search was not exhaustive.

\paragraph{What the shrimp-like shapes are.} The maps show bodies with legs, hooks and crescents that resemble the
shrimp-shaped domains that Gallas found in the parameter plane of the H\'enon map: a main body of period $k$, followed
by domains of periods $k\cdot2^n$, with thin legs \cite{gallas1993structure}. They are made of the bifurcation curves
of periodic windows; where two windows overlap, their strips can cross in an X (Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}). A periodic
window is the region where one periodic orbit attracts. It is bounded by fold curves, where the orbit is born or dies
together with a saddle (multiplier $+1$), and flip curves, where it doubles (multiplier $-1$); beyond the flips lie
the period-doubled shells. In a one-dimensional map the leading multiplier runs from $+1$ through $0$ to $-1$ across
a window, so every window holds a superstable curve: the dark lines of strongly negative exponent in the maps.
Gallas's dissection of one-dimensional shrimps names the parts of a cell (Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}a)
\cite{gallas1994dissecting}. The superstable curves of the map's two critical points cross at the head, where the
orbit passes both critical points at once. At the end of the cell two legs, each a thin strip between a fold and a
flip curve, cross in a kite. The kite begins at a cusp, where the two legs' fold curves meet; inside it two attracting
orbits of the same period coexist, and their superstable curves cross at the tail.

Mira and coworkers sort the organizations around a cusp of a fold curve in two-dimensional maps into three kinds
\cite{mira1987chaotic,carcasses1991crossroad,mira1991crossroad}. Passing through the cusp into its wedge splits an
attracting node into a saddle and two nodes in a crossroad area, where the flip curves of the cusp's two branches
cross, as in Gallas's kite, and in a spring area, where a flip curve loops around the cusp. It splits a saddle into a
node and two saddles in a saddle area, where the attracting orbit exists only inside the wedge. The sign of the cubic
coefficient $c$ of the fold's normal form decides between the two splittings: $c<0$ for crossroad and spring areas,
$c>0$ for the saddle area (Figure~\ref{fig:cuspkinds}). Mira also noted that in two dimensions the attracting node becomes a focus, with a complex
pair of multipliers, away from the fold and flip curves. In a two-dimensional map whose Jacobian is never singular,
such as H\'enon's, no multiplier can vanish. There the curves on which the trace of the cycle's two multipliers
vanishes take the place of the superstable curves, and the head is no longer generic. Fa\c{c}anha, Oldeman and Glass
found two unfoldings: in both the crossing at the tail survives, while at the head the curves either close into a
loop (their Type~A) or approach each other and turn away (Type~B) \cite{facanha2013endoskeletons}.

\paragraph{The skeleton of one window.} The plane steered at two depths (Section~\ref{sec:consequences}) holds a
period-5 window (Figure~\ref{fig:period5folds}). A $31\times31$ grid of the window's continued orbit
(Appendix~\ref{sec:hostility}) left two anomalies, signs of folds it could not resolve, so we continued the window's
fold and flip curves in float64. One fold curve bounds the body on its right, runs over its top and along one side of
each leg. Flip curves bound the body's upper-left and lower sides and the other side of each leg, so each leg lies
between a fold curve and a flip curve, as in Gallas's anatomy, and beyond the flips lie shells of periods 10 and 20.
Where the upper leg leaves the body, the fold curve has two located cusps that share a segment of the curve, and its
other two branches cross (Figure~\ref{fig:period5folds}d). At the crossing the two arms are folds of two different
period-5 orbits with the same token loop (their states differ by about 4\,\%). A second fold curve, on the far left,
where the orbit dies after regaining stability beyond a doubling bubble, has two more located cusps, which also share
a segment, and its other two branches cross as well, again as folds of two different period-5 orbits (states about 9\,\%
apart; Figure~\ref{fig:period5folds}a). Along every
continued curve the critical multiplier stays at $+1$ or $-1$ to within $1.2\times10^{-7}$, and no other multiplier
exceeds $0.4$ in modulus, so no second multiplier reaches the unit circle along them; this does not exclude cusps,
which need only a nonlinear degeneracy.

\paragraph{Shrimps of a two-dimensional kind.} The period-5 window has this anatomy except the head. At every cell of that grid and every curve point, the multipliers beyond the leading pair are below $0.0032$, so the two-dimensional
reading applies. The leading multiplier never vanishes (its modulus stays at least $0.0139$ on the grid): where a
one-dimensional map would be superstable, the two leading multipliers form a complex pair, the node having become a
focus, or pass each other as real numbers of opposite sign. The trace-zero curve around an island of negative trace
(Figure~\ref{fig:period5folds}c) is closed on the grid except at one cell next to the fold curve, where it must close,
because on a fold the trace is $1$ plus the second multiplier and therefore positive: the loop of Type~A. In the
hostile period-7 body the superstable arcs turn away without crossing, which resembles the head of Type~B
(Section~\ref{sec:hostile}).

The junction where the upper leg leaves the body, two cusps that share a segment of the fold curve and a crossing
of its other two branches, is the configuration that Mira et al.\ call a dovetail for critical curves with two such
adjacent cusps
\cite{mira1996chaotic}; we borrow the name, with periodic orbits in place of preimages. With the crossing, the
configuration is a plane section of a swallowtail, which predicts four orbits in the pocket between the cusps and the
crossing, none beyond the crossing and two elsewhere (Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}b). An orbit census at 13 points fixed
before the computation found exactly these numbers at nine (Figure~\ref{fig:period5folds}d): four orbits at three of
five pocket points, none at either point beyond the crossing, and two at four of five surrounding points. At three
other points it found fewer orbits, but all the attracting ones the model predicts (the counts are lower bounds); at
one point, beyond a flip curve, it also found an orbit with another token loop. At all five pocket points of the census
two attracting period-5 orbits coexist; near the upper cusp a flip curve crosses the pocket, beyond which one of them
has doubled. The pocket therefore plays the kite's part, a bistable region at the end of a leg, with two cusps and a
crossing where Gallas's kite has one cusp; both configurations are
plane sections of the swallowtail of a quartic fixed-point equation (Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}). Lifted from the
parameter plane into the orbit states, the fold curve is smooth: its cusps are only where its shadow folds, and the
two arms that cross in the shadow lie on different sheets, as in the model (Figure~\ref{fig:foliation}). The window's second fold curve has a second dovetail. In each, the
two cusps have cubic coefficients of opposite sign, as in any section of a swallowtail: one of the saddle-area kind
($c>0$, the upper cusp in both) and one of the crossroad or spring kind ($c<0$). We do not claim the swallowtail point
itself, which needs a third parameter, and we have not traced the tail \cite{gallas1994dissecting}, determined
symbolic sequences or tested for a periodicity hub \cite{vitolo2011hubs,jones2012rossler}. With these qualifications
the period-5 window carries the anatomy of a shrimp in a two-dimensional map such as H\'enon's
\cite{henon1976two,gallas1993structure}; whether a two-dimensional map also reproduces its nonlinear dynamics is not
tested (a closure test failed for numerical reasons; Appendix~\ref{sec:hostility}).

\section{Beyond the one-token loop}
\label{sec:beyond}
The soft-token map feeds back one expected embedding at a fixed prefix and position. This section summarizes what
changes when the loop keeps more context, when tokens are sampled and in other models (details in
Appendices~\ref{sec:bridge} and~\ref{sec:scale}); Sections~\ref{sec:sizemain} and~\ref{sec:huginn} then follow model
size and a second system.

\paragraph{Memory and sampling.} As $T\to0$ the soft-token map becomes greedy decoding with the same finite memory,
whose attractors are cycles (Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge}). Fed back the last 8 or 16 soft tokens instead of one,
Qwen2.5-7B is chaotic at 21 and 20 of 24 temperatures, against 8 with one. In Soft Thinking's growing context, where
every soft token stays visible, an early perturbation of $10^{-10}$ grows exponentially in the same model inside its
one-token chaotic band. This does not hold in every model: in Qwen2.5-0.5B the growth moves to sharper distributions,
outside its narrow one-token chaotic band, and Qwen2.5-72B stays nearly insensitive (Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}). Sampling is different. When the expected embedding is replaced by the mean of $m$ sampled
token embeddings, the laminar phases of the bottleneck, 120 steps long in the exact map at the closest offset, last
about 10.6 steps with $m=64$ and 7.7 with $m=8$, and with single samples essentially none remain. The deterministic
intermittency belongs to the expected-embedding map; sampling noise turns the bottleneck into a noise-limited trap
whose strength grows with $m$.

\paragraph{Other models.} The organization recurs in Qwen2.5-1.5B (Figure~\ref{fig:qwen15}) and 7B, in OLMo-2 7B, in Llama-3.1 8B, whose
attractors we also read with sparse-autoencoder features, and in a second checkpoint of the 0.5B architecture, which
shares the organization but not the attractors (Appendix~\ref{sec:scale}). The fold law of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} recurs at 1.5B and 7B: windows accumulate on the fold of a short cycle, and the nearest
four resolved windows meet a criterion fixed before the lines were computed, within $0.12\,\%$ at 1.5B and $4.5\,\%$
at 7B (a correction of the period selector after the first scoring is disclosed); farther out, the 1.5B windows
spread by $10.3$ to $14.0\,\%$.

\section{Chaos and model size}
\label{sec:sizemain}
Is the chaos of the loop a small-model effect that larger models leave behind? We measured the unsteered map of
fifteen models: Qwen2.5 at seven sizes from 0.5B to 72B parameters, OLMo-2 at 1B, 7B, 13B and 32B, Llama-3.1 at 8B
and 70B, Llama-3.3-70B, and the mixture of experts Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, all with the paper's prefix and the settings of
Appendix~\ref{sec:methods} (1000 transient and 2000 measured steps in float64; chaotic: an exponent above $10^{-3}$ and
no detected period, which is what ``positive'' abbreviates below). We evaluate the stored checkpoint values in
float64: the dense checkpoints store bfloat16 weights (checked against their safetensors metadata), and the mixture of
experts stores some tensors in float32, which we restore exactly; float64 does not make the weights more precise than
stored. The newer sweeps keep the decoder weights in bfloat16 and convert them on use, which gives the same map as
converting them once (bitwise on Qwen2.5-0.5B); the 70B-class models are spread over two GPUs. In those two-GPU runs the final
RMS normalization was computed in float32: the hooks that move tensors between the GPUs keep a layer's original code,
so our float64 version of that layer was bypassed. This makes the computed map rough at about $10^{-8}$ of the
state (its fixed points wander by $10^{-8}$, against $10^{-15}$ on one GPU) and can matter for exponents measured
with perturbations of $10^{-6}$ and below. We found and fixed it at the end of the study. The affected runs are those on
two GPUs before the fix: the 70B-class sweeps and their plateau tests (Appendix~\ref{sec:size}), the first Qwen2.5-72B prompt test, the 70B
steering maps and zoom, the gain splits of the 70B-class models, and the start-state test, which spread all fourteen
models over two GPUs. Appendix~\ref{sec:size} says which results we recomputed. The 7--8B
and 70B-class models had shown a steep decline of chaos with size. Before measuring the others we fixed three
predictions that extrapolate it (\texttt{docs/scaling-predictions.md}): within each family, every model above 8B is
less chaotic than the family's 7--8B model (P1); the chaotic share does not increase with size above 7B (P2); and at
matched next-token entropy, both gain factors of Section~\ref{sec:theory} of the 14B and 32B models lie at or below
those of the 7B model (P3). After a review pointed out that a single prompt and a fixed temperature window could
mislead, we froze a fourth test across ten prompts (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-test.md}).

\paragraph{In a fixed temperature window, chaos seems to fade with size.} Table~\ref{tab:scale} and
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} give each model's chaotic share of the temperatures in $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$, the range
common to all sweeps, and the chaotic length of its whole temperature axis. In two families the largest models are
nearly free of chaos in this window. Qwen2.5-7B is chaotic at
12.7\,\% of the temperatures in the common range, and the 14B, 32B and 72B models at none. Llama-3.1-8B is chaotic at
19\,\%, Llama-3.1-70B, from the same release and training recipe, at 2.3\,\%, and Llama-3.3-70B, which differs from
Llama-3.1-70B only in post-training, at none. In the third family the order fails: OLMo-2-32B is the most chaotic
OLMo model, at 16.2\,\% against 6.2\,\% at 7B, 3.8\,\% at 1B and none at 13B. (The 32B model is a later release,
0325, than the 7B and 13B models, 1124, so within OLMo-2 size and release are confounded.) The frozen predictions P1
and P2 hold for Qwen2.5 and fail for OLMo-2. Below 7B we predicted no order, and none appears (Qwen2.5 0.5B, 1.5B and
3B: 1.4, 1.5 and 0\,\%).

\paragraph{Across prompts, the chaos moves to higher temperature instead.} The fixed window hides where larger models
are chaotic. The frozen prompt-blocked test compares Qwen2.5-7B and -14B on ten system prompts and openers (two for
development, eight held out), over $\log_{10}T\in[-0.4,0.9]$ (48 temperatures) and then over the temperature interval
where each model's next-token entropy rises most (24 temperatures), with twins at offsets $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-7}$; a
temperature counts as chaotic only when both exponents are positive (Figure~\ref{fig:prefixscale}). It predicted that
the 14B model would be less chaotic. Both predictions fail: on all eight held-out prompts the 14B model is chaotic at
more of the 48 temperatures (9 to 17, against 0 to 7 for the 7B), and its largest exponent is lower on only three.
Every chaotic temperature of the 14B model, on all ten prompts, lies between $\log_{10}T=0.36$ and $0.90$, at and
above the temperature where its next-token distribution spreads ($0.35$--$0.65$); the 7B model's chaos lies mostly
between $0.21$ and $0.43$, around its own, lower transition ($0.21$--$0.46$). On the paper's prompt, 12 of the 48
coarse temperatures of the 14B model are chaotic, from $0.59$ to $0.90$, and the adaptive fine stage found one more
near $0.519$; the initial sweep of Table~\ref{tab:scale} ended at $0.6$ and saw only the lower edge (the table now
also includes the upper sweep). Within Qwen2.5, then, the larger model is not less chaotic: its distribution stays
sharp up to higher temperature, and its chaos sits there. The decline in the fixed window is largely a shift. A
second frozen test (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-olmo.md}; stage 1 only, four held-out prompts) asked whether the OLMo-2
order survives other prompts. It does, with small counts: OLMo-2-32B is chaotic at more of the 48 temperatures than
OLMo-2-7B on three of the four prompts (4, 5, 3 and 0 against 3, 0, 1 and 3), as predicted, and its chaos again lies
somewhat higher ($\log_{10}T=0.10$--$0.35$ against $-0.01$--$0.21$).

\paragraph{What sets it: the gain of the loop.} The gain split of Section~\ref{sec:theory} shows which factor removes or
keeps the chaos (Figure~\ref{fig:gainscale}). We compare medians of the two factors in bins of the mean next-token
entropy on the orbit; the bins hold 1 to 17 of each model's 24 temperatures, so single medians are noisy. Within one
training recipe, from Llama-3.1-8B to Llama-3.1-70B, the readout factor changes by at most 0.3 per step in every bin
above one nat (below one nat the 8B model has a single temperature), while the network factor is lower by 0.5 to 1.3
per step between 1 and 10 nats (6--10 nats: median $-0.98$ from 3 temperatures against $+0.28$ from 4) and by only
0.18 above 10 nats: in these two checkpoints the larger model damps changes of its fed-back token. Llama-3.3-70B, which
differs from Llama-3.1-70B in post-training, has a much lower readout factor below 3 nats (1--3 nats: $-2.33$ from 7
temperatures against $-0.10$ from 6) and a similar one above 6 nats. In Qwen2.5, both factors of the 14B and 32B
models lie below the 7B's in every bin between 1 and 10 nats, as the frozen prediction P3 states (the 6--10 nat bin
only with the 7B's wider gain split, from one temperature), and both factors of
the 72B lie below the 7B's in every bin. Above 10 nats, where the distribution is nearly uniform, the 14B and 32B
models instead have the larger network factor ($+2.98$ and $+1.17$ against $+0.19$), and those are the bins of their
chaotic temperatures above $\log_{10}T=0.3$. Below 3 nats the readout factor dominates at 32B and 72B, because the
next-token distribution stays sharp up to high temperature (for the 72B, entropy at most 0.15 nats up to $T=2$). In
OLMo-2, P3 fails, and the readout factor tracks the chaos: OLMo-2-13B, with no chaotic temperature, has a lower
readout factor than OLMo-2-7B in all three bins between 1 and 10 nats ($-1.0$ to $-1.4$ against $+0.06$ to $-0.72$
per step) although its network factor is higher in two of them, whereas OLMo-2-32B, the most chaotic OLMo model, has a
higher readout factor than the 7B in all three ($+0.10$ to $+0.35$), positive as in the chaotic 7--8B models. These
are descriptive comparisons of trained checkpoints, not interventions on size or post-training: the split is a
decomposition along the evolving tangent direction, and equal entropy does not imply equal embedding covariance.
Entropy clamping tests at 72B whether forcing an intermediate entropy suffices to destabilize the loop. If its loop
were stable only because its distribution jumps from sharp to flat, holding the distribution in between should
destabilize it. We therefore choose each step's
temperature so that the next-token entropy equals a target and sweep the target instead of the temperature
(entropy-clamped decoding; the map stays deterministic, and the entropy holds to $10^{-14}$). The temperature then
depends on the state, $T=\tau(x)$ with $H(x,\tau(x))=H_\star$ for the entropy $H$, so the derivative of the clamped
map $\hat F(x)=F(x,\tau(x))$ gains a rank-one term, $D\hat F=D_xF-\partial_TF\,D_xH/\partial_TH$. We therefore take
its exponents from twins of the clamped map itself, temperature solve included, and use no fixed-temperature
derivative or gain split for it. The safeguarded solve is only piecewise smooth, but on these orbits one-step central
differences at $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$ agree with those at $10^{-4}$ within $6\times10^{-6}$. Held at any of 16 targets
between 1 and 11 nats, Qwen2.5-72B has only periodic orbits, the echo of period 2 on the paper's prompt and periods 1
to 19 on a held-out prompt, with twin exponents between $-0.08$ and $-4.5$ that agree within 0.004 across offsets
$10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$, whereas Qwen2.5-0.5B under the same clamp is chaotic at 6.1 nats. The clamped 72B loop thus
stays stable even where its readout is spread. The clamp changes the map, so this is consistent with damping by the
network in the original map but does not identify it. The pretrained checkpoint is also mostly non-chaotic. A test
frozen before
its data (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-72b-base.md}) predicted that the pretrained base model of the same size would be
chaotic at three or more temperatures more than Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct on at least three of the four held-out prompts.
It fails on all four: the base model is chaotic at 1, 0, 0 and 0 of the 48 temperatures (and at 2 on the paper's
prompt; computed after the precision fix, with 500 transient and 1000 measured steps, half the Instruct model's
horizon), although its next-token distribution spreads over 2.5 to 5.5 times the temperature range (intermediate-entropy
breadth 0.28 to 0.61 decades against 0.11 to 0.22) and from lower temperatures on. At 72B, then, the sharpness that
post-training adds does not explain the stability, and post-training is not necessary for it; the failed prediction
does not quantify post-training's other effects. The gain split points to the network's damping of the fed-back token
(the 72B's network factor lies below the 7B's in every entropy bin), and the breadth that orders the fifteen models (Figure~\ref{fig:scale}, right) fails for this pair. The damping holds when the whole
context is kept as well: in Soft Thinking's growing context (Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge}), an early perturbation grows at
none of eleven steered cells, six of them chaotic as one-token maps, and at one of 48 unsteered temperatures on two
prompts, transiently.

\paragraph{What this implies for larger models.} Size does not set this chaos in a simple way. In Qwen2.5, the 14B
model's chaos did not vanish but moved to higher temperature, to where its next-token distribution spreads; in OLMo-2
the largest model was the most chaotic; two 70B Llama checkpoints with different post-training gave different results
on the same grid;
and at 70B, chaos that passes a three-offset test remains, rarer, with and without steering (Appendix~\ref{sec:size}). The shift does not
continue to Qwen2.5-72B. A prompt-blocked test frozen before its data (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-72b.md}) predicted
that the 72B model would be chaotic at fewer of the 48 temperatures than the 14B on at least three of four held-out
prompts. It is chaotic at 0, 1, 0 and 0 of them, against 14, 9, 11 and 15, so the prediction holds on all four. These
lines ran before the precision fix (1000 transient and 2000 measured steps, twins at $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-6}$, chaotic
only when both exponents are positive); no temperature on them is positive at only one offset, and the one chaotic
temperature ($\log_{10}T=0.319$, exponents 0.35 and 0.34) is chaotic again when recomputed in float64 throughout,
with an exact tangent exponent of 0.34. With the paper's prompt, where it showed no robust chaos up to
$\log_{10}T=1.2$, that makes five prompts on which the 72B model is nearly free of unsteered chaos. Its next-token distribution jumps from sharp to nearly uniform within less
than the grid spacing (0.03 decades), so a narrow chaotic band at the jump would be missed by the grid. Fine lines
through the jump, computed in float64 throughout with exact tangent exponents, find only periodic orbits on the
paper's prompt and on three of the four held-out prompts, and on the fourth a band of chaos about 0.02 decades wide
just above the jump ($\log_{10}T=0.319$--$0.336$; exponents up to 0.40, which twins at $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$ reproduce
within 0.004), at nearly uniform distributions, as the 14B model's chaos but far narrower. A fixed temperature window is therefore the wrong yardstick, and so is size alone. A model's chaos follows the
gain of its loop where its own next-token distribution is spread, no single forward pass we tested predicts it (Appendix~\ref{sec:size}), and
to find it in a larger model one has to run the loop there, across prompts. For frontier models this means two
things: their unsteered chaos may be rare, if, like the largest models here, their distributions spread over a narrow
range of temperature; and steering still drives both 70B-class models that we steered, Llama-3.3-70B and Qwen2.5-72B, into chaos (Appendix~\ref{sec:size}).

\section{A second system: recurrent depth}
\label{sec:huginn}
Huginn-0125 iterates a recurrent block at fixed input tokens \cite{geiping2025recurrent}. For a GSM8K-style
\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} prompt, the native loop converges to a fixed point whose leading Jacobian pair is
$0.588\pm0.771i$ (modulus $0.970$). For the relaxed loop $h\leftarrow(1-\lambda)h+\lambda F(h)$, whose multipliers
are $1-\lambda+\lambda\mu$, this predicts that the pair crosses the unit circle at
$\lambda_c=2(1-\operatorname{Re}\mu)/|1-\mu|^2=1.0777$, transversally and away from the strong resonances (at
0.156 of a revolution per step): a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation. Below it the latent state
decays; above it the state settles on a closed curve. A fit of the squared amplitude gives $\lambda_c=1.0778$, with
$A/\sqrt{\lambda-\lambda_c}=0.52$--$0.54$. A stable circle that grows like the square root of the distance is
consistent with a supercritical Neimark--Sacker bifurcation; we did not compute its first Lyapunov coefficient, so its
nondegeneracy is not established. Causal attention makes the loop block lower-triangular in
token positions. The first position to oscillate is the ``.'' of the prompt, and all later positions follow. On the
invariant circle the motion turns by $0.156$ of a revolution per step, matching the argument of the critical pair.
The rotation number changes smoothly, by about $10^{-4}$ over $\lambda\in[1.079,1.092]$, and no return within 60
steps was found at $10^{-3}$ of the radius. This is consistent with quasi-periodic motion, although locking in narrow
high-order tongues cannot be excluded. From $\lambda\approx1.094$ the circle deforms or a second frequency appears,
near where a second complex pair destabilizes the fixed point ($\lambda=1.0814$). Fixed-point and Jacobian stability
of looped models has been studied before \cite{yang2026stars}; what this adds is the predicted crossing, its amplitude
law and the rotation number.

\paragraph{The spectrum predicts the relaxation response.} With the full instruction template, the GSM8K prompts'
fixed points look different: the leading multipliers surround the origin, a real $0.914$, complex pairs of modulus
$0.85$--$0.89$ at many angles and negative real values near $-0.8$, with no near-critical pair. Because the prefill
Jacobian is block lower-triangular in positions, its spectrum is the union of per-position blocks, and template
positions contribute identical eigenvalues to every prompt. Such a spectrum makes three predictions before any task is
scored. First, within the computed spectral estimates of these prompts, no fixed relaxation can speed convergence
much. With leading multipliers spread around the origin at many angles, any $\lambda$ that shrinks one group enlarges
another, and the best $\lambda^*=1.03$--$1.06$ lowers the rate from $0.914$ to only $0.908$--$0.911$. (A finite
computed spectrum does not show that no other polynomial scheme could do better; Anderson acceleration, tried below,
did not.) Second, under-relaxation slows convergence ($\lambda=0.55$: predicted $0.953$, measured $0.951$ on one
prompt). Third, over-relaxation destabilizes the negative modes first, at
$\lambda_c=2\operatorname{Re}(1-\mu)/|1-\mu|^2=1.074$--$1.126$ (five prompts), in a flip or a Neimark--Sacker
bifurcation close to $-1$. The approach to it is visible: on the prompt with $\lambda_c=1.074$, the measured rate at
$\lambda=1.05$ is $0.955$ against a predicted $0.956$ (native $0.864$). Elsewhere the spectral radius bounds the rate
measured over the accessible window from above, by up to $0.05$, because the slowest modes carry little amplitude
there. Independently, \citet{wang2026taps} report a Huginn GSM8K drop from $19.41\,\%$ to
$8.34\,\%$ at a constant step of $1.2$; at $0.8$ GSM8K is $19.79\,\%$, while their four-task mean falls slightly.
Their trajectory-based scheduling analysis does not compute the prompt-specific spectral thresholds studied here.

\paragraph{Does the task notice? (development data).} On ARC-Challenge \cite{clark2018arc} questions scored by
log-likelihood, where one prefill per answer choice is exactly the analysed recurrence, the spectra again predict
convergence. Rates agree to $0.002$--$0.006$ where the slowest mode dominates, and beyond each question's $\lambda_c$
($1.075$--$1.149$ for four questions) the residual stops decaying. For 6 of 30 development questions the native fixed
point is itself unstable ($\rho(1)=1.009$--$1.273$; the fixed point, located with a relaxed transient, has a real
multiplier beyond $-1$ or a complex pair outside the circle). The native loop then oscillates (relative amplitude
$2\times10^{-2}$ for one of them), and relaxation to $0.55$ or $0.75$ restores convergence to $10^{-6}$. For the
other 24, no unstable mode was detected in the computed spectral estimates up to $\lambda=1.020$--$1.149$ (median
$1.095$); partial Ritz spectra cannot exclude an undetected unstable mode. Question by question, an
estimated spectral feature ranks the question's residual. This feature, $\hat\rho_q(\lambda)$, is the largest
$|1-\lambda+\lambda\mu|$ over the computed Ritz values of the question's gold-answer prefill, and its Spearman
correlation with the residual is $0.73$ at $\lambda=1$ and $0.75$ at $1.10$ over the 30 questions (development
data). In this historical analysis the residual was the all-choice aggregate entering the 32nd recurrence call, so
this is a predictive association across related maps and precisions, not a same-state contraction identity. It is
consistent with the latent orbits that \citet{geiping2025recurrent} observed on some tokens of harder questions, here
traced to the fixed point's spectrum. The item-level part of the frozen test below uses matched gold-row token IDs and
a per-row residual with an explicit clock.

Accuracy, however, tolerates the instability (300 training questions, 32 steps): it is $0.367$ native, $0.387$ at
$\lambda=1.10$ and $0.373$ at $1.15$, where the latent state oscillates with relative size $0.08$ and $0.23$. Only at
$1.25$ (size $0.68$) does it fall, to $0.300$. Yet the oscillation does reach the answers. Reading every step from 24
to 32 on 100 questions, the margin between the correct choice and its best rival moves by $0.11$ nats per step
natively, $0.37$ at $1.10$ and $0.95$ at $1.15$, in alternating directions, and the chosen answer changes within these
nine steps for 12\,\%, 16\,\% and 33\,\% of the questions. The changes go both ways, so the accuracy at any one step
stays the same on average. Under-relaxation instead costs 4--5 points at 8--16 steps and catches up by 32 (a slower
transient), and Anderson acceleration neither speeds the transient nor gains accuracy. Testing the accuracy
consequence therefore needs a task in which such step-to-step changes can compound.

GSM8K generation is such a task. With eight chain-of-thought examples in the lm-evaluation-harness format, greedy
decoding and 32 recurrence steps per token (development problems 0--119, every arm on the same problems and initial
states), accuracy is $0.375$ natively, $0.425$ at $\lambda=1.05$ and at $1.10$, $0.29$ at $1.15$ (17 problems lost and
7 gained, one-sided McNemar $p=0.03$) and $0.16$ at $1.20$ (29 lost, 3 gained, $p=10^{-6}$). Under-relaxation to
$0.8$ costs nothing at 32 steps ($0.41$). The break lies between $1.10$ and $1.15$, at or just above the spectral
thresholds ($\lambda_c=1.074$--$1.126$ for the GSM8K prompts; median upper stability limit $1.095$ for the ARC
development questions). Generation breaks earlier than log-likelihood scoring, consistent with answer changes that
compound over tokens. The collapse at $1.2$ is the one \citet{wang2026taps} reported.

A frozen test on fresh problems confirms this. Its design and analysis were pushed before any test item was read,
after two rounds of independent review. On 200 GSM8K test problems, accuracy is $0.330$ natively, $0.395$ at
$\lambda=1.05$, $0.255$ at $1.15$ and $0.095$ at $1.20$. The two pre-stated predictions pass: accuracy declines at $1.20$ ($-23.5$
points; 50 problems lost and 3 gained, exact one-sided McNemar $p=3\times10^{-12}$), and $1.05$ loses no more than
four points (difference $+6.5$, one-sided 95\,\% lower bound $+2.5$). At $1.15$ accuracy falls by $7.5$ points
($p=0.01$, reported descriptively). Development and test agree closely (Figure~\ref{fig:huginn-gsm8k}).

The same frozen design tested the item-level prediction on 64 held-out ARC-Challenge questions. The first 16

\section{Two loops in conversation}
\label{sec:dialogue}
Two instances of a language model left to talk with each other drift into recurring states
\cite{ko2026attractor}. The best-known case is the ``spiritual bliss'' attractor of two Claude Opus 4 instances, whose
open-ended conversations end in repeated exchanges of gratitude and silence \cite{anthropic2025claude4}. The soft-token map poses the deterministic version of the
question: can two loops that are each chaotic on their own lock each other into a periodic orbit? Agents A and B are
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with the conversation prompt of a sampled self-dialogue study that we report separately,
each with its own steering strength along the
philosophy direction at layer 12. They write one reply together, alternating soft tokens, $x_{n+1}=F_A(x_n)$ and
$x_{n+2}=F_B(x_{n+1})$, so one exchange is the map $G=F_B\circ F_A$. Equal agents reproduce the single loop bitwise in
float64, so on a map over $(\alpha_A,\alpha_B)$ the diagonal holds each agent writing alone
(Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue}).

At $\log_{10}T=-0.10$, over $\alpha_A,\alpha_B\in[0.060,0.082]$ ($48\times48$ cells), 27 of the 48 agents are chaotic
alone and 21 periodic. Of the 702 pairs whose two agents are both chaotic alone, 422 are periodic together; of the 420
pairs of two periodic agents, 91 are chaotic together. Writing together favours order (70\,\% of pairs are periodic,
against 44\,\% of single agents), though some chaos is inherited: pairs of chaotic agents are periodic less often than
pairs in general. The exponent map is plaid, as if each agent contributed its own stability, and where one agent is
weakly steered ($\alpha\lesssim0.064$) and the other is not, the pair settles into a fixed two-token exchange. Twelve
entrained pairs spread over the map all converge under Newton's method to attracting primitive orbits of $G$
(residuals at most $2\times10^{-13}$, leading multipliers $0.0008$ to $0.79$ in modulus, exchange periods 1, 3, 5 and
6). Their texts show how two chaotic writers share a loop. At period 1, A writes ``is'' and B ``it'', the single loop's
2-cycle split between two writers. At period 5, B answers ``is'' every time while A cycles through ``This'',
``there'', ``it'' and ``a''. That alternating two chaotic maps can produce order is known for quadratic maps
\cite{almeida2005chaos}; here it happens in a language model's own dynamics.

Two different models behave alike. Qwen2.5-0.5B and 1.5B share a tokenizer, so a message can be passed as a
distribution over the shared vocabulary and embedded by each model with its own matrix. Unsteered, the 1.5B writing
with itself is chaotic at 41 of 131 temperatures ($\log_{10}T$ from $0.29$ to $0.80$, exponents $0.21$ to $1.76$ per
exchange), the 0.5B at one. Writing together they are periodic at 39 of those 41 temperatures, mostly in a fixed
two-token exchange: the stable partner sets the rhythm. A dialogue framing behaves differently. With each one-token
message placed in a user turn and the first token of the reply as the answer, every map we computed is periodic, for
one agent alone and for every pair. The reply is decided by the replier's own prompt and steering, and the partner's
message barely moves it; mutual locking of the kind seen in long conversations would need longer messages than this
frame provides.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What theory allows, and what is new.} Chaos and periodic windows occur in many nonlinear feedback systems:
random recurrent networks become chaotic above a critical gain \cite{sompolinsky1988chaos,doyon1994bifurcations},
and periodic windows, shrimp-shaped domains and doubling cascades are found in low-dimensional maps and in
experiments \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative,gallas1993structure,stoop2010real}. In our map temperature plays the role
of an inverse gain and the steering vector that of an input. The factorization of Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes this
correspondence concrete, but it remains an analogy, not a transfer of the random-network theorems
(Appendix~\ref{sec:theorymore}). These results motivate looking for such structure in a language model's feedback
map; they do not imply it. This paper shows that the models we studied realize it, with concrete texts as
attractors, so that a bifurcation changes what the model writes, and that several prospective quantitative
predictions succeeded; the failed all-row criteria and the unresolved cases are reported with them.
\paragraph{Is steering the origin of the chaos?} No. Without steering ($\alpha=0$) the map is the model's own feedback
map, and temperature alone gives chaos with embedded windows in most dense models we swept (in eleven of fourteen on
regular grids). Steering widens the chaotic regions, but not through the concept it names: in the 0.5B model
norm-matched random directions are as chaotic as the concept directions, and in Llama-3.1 8B sparse-autoencoder
feature, contrastive and random directions all are, with direction-dependent prevalence. What the direction selects is
the text of the attractors (Appendix~\ref{sec:origin}).

\paragraph{What is and is not claimed.} The objects studied here are invariant sets and bifurcations of an exactly
defined smooth map built from a frozen model's own computation. The answer-level reproducibility tests use two Qwen2.5
models (0.5B and 7B), one task family and a soft-thinking pipeline without the top-$k$ filtering of the published
method, and their bfloat16 effect is not the float64 map's chaos (Section~\ref{sec:consequences}). The mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} is measured along three directions in a 0.5B model and one in a 7B model, and at high
temperature the network factor can carry the instability. The loop's regime does not mark the accuracy of ordinary
sampled answers beyond the model's first-token uncertainty, and when slight steering moved a prompt's loop into chaos no change of that accuracy was detected ($+0.36$ points,
$[-0.36,1.08]$; Appendix~\ref{sec:behaviour}). We do not claim that ordinary sampled generation is
chaotic in this sense (Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge}), nor that any window is a certified shrimp. The period-5 window carries the anatomy of a shrimp in a two-dimensional map
(Section~\ref{sec:organization}): fold curves with located cusps, legs, doubling shells, a trace-zero loop in place
of a one-dimensional head, and two dovetails. The period-6 and period-7 windows have fold-and-flip skeletons with
located cusps, and in the hostile period-7 body the superstable arcs avoid each other where a head would be. We have
not traced tails or symbolic sequences, tested for a periodicity hub \cite{vitolo2011hubs,jones2012rossler}, or shown
that a two-dimensional map reproduces the windows' nonlinear dynamics. The cusps are float64
computations validated by step-size checks, not proofs (Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} The detailed orbit studies cover Qwen2.5 at 0.5B and 1.5B, with three float64 orbits at 7B;
OLMo-2 7B and Llama-3.1 8B have maps but no continued orbits. The size comparison rests on regular temperature grids and mostly one twin
offset per label, and part of the 70B-class maps predate a precision fix and were rechecked only in samples. The maps
use one prompt (more in the size tests), and steering uses contrastive vectors, checked against random and
sparse-autoencoder directions in two models. Chaos labels are finite-horizon estimates: a longer horizon check kept 86
and 92 of 96 chaotic labels in two maps, near the accumulation of a cascade transient chaos makes labels less reliable (on one line only 135 of 235 chaotic labels held), no chaotic cell is proved chaotic, and only one periodic orbit is proved to exist. The Feigenbaum ratios are
finite-order values. All orbit, bifurcation and chaos results concern the constructed map, not sampled generation with
a growing context. Appendix~\ref{sec:limits} gives the details.

\paragraph{Steering strength is a bifurcation parameter.} In the soft-token map, small changes of the steering
strength switch the model between distinct repeating texts, chaos and coexisting attractors. Near a fold curve,
behaviour becomes intermittent, with laminar phases whose duration the fold's normal form predicts in advance. This argues for treating
an activation-steering coefficient like any control parameter of a nonlinear system. Its bifurcation set should be
characterized before an operating point is chosen, history dependence should be expected near boundaries, and a
monotone, smooth dose response should not be assumed. The block at which a vector enters matters as much as its
strength. In a 0.5B and a 70B model, earlier injection gives more chaos and brings it to lower temperature, and two
vectors at two blocks create chaos where neither does alone (Section~\ref{sec:consequences}). Outputs that fall into verbatim repetition at high steering
doses are naturally treated as quality failures. In the present framework such loops are candidate attracting
periodic orbits, and the dose at which they appear is a boundary that can be mapped like the others. The
deterministic map is where such sets can be defined precisely and computed numerically. Whether its boundaries
predict boundaries of sampled behaviour is open. The bridge of Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge} shows that sampling noise
shortens the laminar phases of the bottleneck, by an amount that depends on how many samples are averaged.

\paragraph{What this suggests for interpretability and alignment.} Our results come from open models of 0.5B to
72B parameters and a constructed loop, but they suggest three things about models whose outputs feed back into their inputs, as in
self-dialogue, agent loops and pipelines of model calls. First, where the loop is chaotic, a perturbation of
$10^{-6}$ grows to order one within roughly 15 to 50 steps (the largest unsteered exponents of the 7B and 8B
models are 0.30 to 0.88 per step). Even a complete account of single forward passes then predicts a long trajectory
only if the state is known far more precisely than any interpretation supplies. Stepwise summaries can be
inconclusive: every step of the period-8 orbit expands some direction while the cycle contracts, so the ordered
product of the step Jacobians, not a summary of each step, captures the loop's stability
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}). What remains predictable are the invariant sets, their
statistics and the boundaries between them. This extends the analysis of trained recurrent networks through their
fixed points \cite{sussillo2013opening,maheswaranathan2019reverse} to the periodic orbits, chaos and bifurcations of a
pretrained transformer. Second, feature dictionaries say which features an attractor occupies, but the boundaries
between attractors, which decide what a small change does, are properties of the map's derivatives. That would hold even if most units have one interpretable job, as a recent
essay argues for Qwen3-32B \cite{cammarata_clean}; in its pruning tests, too, the answers that change are mostly
those the network was already unsure of, as the loop turns chaotic where next tokens compete. Single neurons are no exception: in the 0.5B model, steering
along the output direction of the neuron most specific to philosophy makes 9.9\,\% of the map chaotic, its index
neighbour 5.1\,\% and a random neuron 12.9\,\%, against 6.8 to 12.5\,\% along the concept and Gaussian directions
(Appendix~\ref{sec:origin}). In our 8B controls,
a sparse-autoencoder feature direction, contrastive and random directions all drove the map into chaos (27 to 39\,\%
of cells); at 70B the hostility-related directions gave more chaotic cells than the random ones (4.3 and 3.5\,\%
against 1.1 and 0.2\,\%, two-GPU maps from before the precision fix; Appendix~\ref{sec:size}). Steering acts by making next tokens compete (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), which is not specific
to concept-labelled directions, but a direction's geometry and its meaning can be correlated. Text can
also hide the dynamics: in the hostile cascade, a seven-token text repeats exactly while the state underneath is
chaotic (Figure~\ref{fig:hiddenbands}), so a monitor that reads only the emitted text would call a chaotic regime
periodic.
Third, conclusions drawn at one operating point need not transfer: which loop a
model enters depends on how much context it keeps (Table~\ref{tab:memory}), on the path by which a boundary is
approached, and on the sampling noise (Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge}). Treating a looped model as a dynamical system, with
regimes and boundaries to be mapped, rather than as a function to be evaluated point by point, seems to us the
appropriate stance for both interpretability and safety evaluation. The reproducibility tests of
Section~\ref{sec:consequences} make this concrete: in a chaotic regime the batch a request happens to share decides,
after a predictable number of steps, what the loop writes. In low precision, continuous feedback makes even short-budget
answers depend on the batch, which greedy decoding does not.

\paragraph{Is the chaos useful?} The chaos we map is not a source of new content. The chaotic cells of
Table~\ref{tab:transcripts} recombine the phrases of the neighbouring loops in an order that did not settle over the recorded horizon: the
trajectory wanders among the ghosts of nearby periodic orbits, which gives variety in sequence but not in substance.
The periodic loops are the familiar degeneration of decoding into repetition
\cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}. Neither regime is good text, and the diversity of ordinary generation
comes from sampling and a growing context, which wash out much of this structure (Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge}). What
the chaos does matter for is predictability and control. Positive finite-horizon exponents limit open-loop
prediction of a trajectory at fixed state precision (our experiments do not establish mixing or limits on feedback
stabilization); a stable attractor is predictable, but it can also hold a model in a state it should leave, such as the hostile loops of Section~\ref{sec:hostile}. Whether regimes
near the boundary between order and chaos are especially useful, as has been argued for computation in dynamical
systems and recurrent networks \cite{langton1990computation,bertschinger2004realtime} and shown for signal propagation
in deep networks at initialization \cite{poole2016exponential,schoenholz2017deep}, is open for language models. We
tested three versions, and none shows an advantage at the edge. Prompts whose own loop sits at the edge
($|\lambda|\le0.1$) are answered no better by ordinary sampling than prompts whose loop is deeply periodic or deeply
chaotic (Section~\ref{sec:consequences}). Used as a reservoir, the loop remembers a weak injected input best in short
stable cycles. Its linear memory capacity falls at every period doubling toward the onset of chaos, does not rise as
the exponent approaches zero within a period, and is nearly erased by chaos (Figure~\ref{fig:reservoir}). In Huginn,
whose reasoning is itself a latent loop, a float32 pilot on 40 development problems, separate from the tests of
Section~\ref{sec:huginn}, recorded the decay rate of every generated token's recurrence. Accuracy holds while that
recurrence is comfortably stable and falls as it approaches its edge: it was $0.475$ natively and $0.525$ at relaxation
$1.05$, where the token recurrence is nearly as stable as natively (median decay rate $0.921$ against $0.912$), and
fell to $0.375$ at $1.15$, where the recurrence is near critical ($0.981$, with about half of the tokens no longer
converging). With 40 problems these differences are indicative only.

\paragraph{Which findings might reach frontier models.} The parts that rest only on structure every smooth
softmax-readout language model with a fixed prefix shares should carry over: the factorization of the loop's
derivative, contraction at sufficiently high temperature, greedy cycles at zero temperature, and the role of a
steering coefficient as a bifurcation parameter. The attractor texts, the positions of windows, folds and cusps, and
every number we report belong to particular weights; a second checkpoint of the same architecture already has
different attractors (Appendix~\ref{sec:scale}). Size gives no guarantee of stability (Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}), hard
routing in mixture-of-experts models falls outside the smooth theory of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, and deployed
inference samples tokens, in low precision, over long contexts. Closed models permit none of these computations, which
need the input embeddings, full next-token distributions and derivatives; for them the method and the questions
transfer, not the numbers.

\section{Related work in detail}
\label{sec:related}
Language-model generation has been studied as a dynamical system at several levels, and
the closest studies differ from ours in what they compute. Lyapunov-like quantities have been used to measure
sensitivity to small perturbations in reasoning traces \cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos} and in token rings
resampled by a model's own conditionals, with a test that separates the construction from the model
\cite{verazuniga2026selffeeding}, but these studies
measure chaos-like sensitivity in generation without computing orbits or bifurcations. Output statistics change
abruptly with sampling temperature, and several studies analyse the changes as phase transitions or criticality
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality}. These analyses concern output statistics;
they are not bifurcation analyses of a defined map. Closest to ours in subject, a study of eleven models sorts sampled
continuations into classes from frozen and periodic to intermittent and noisy across temperature, fits a noisy effective map to one
hidden-state coordinate that predicts repetition, and steers along it \cite{johnson2026temperature}; that map is
fitted to sampled trajectories, and no orbits, multipliers or bifurcation curves of the model's own map are computed. Repetition loops are a known failure of
decoding \cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}, successive paraphrasing converges to attractor cycles
\cite{wang2025attractor}, and multi-turn conversations between models settle into attractor states
\cite{ko2026attractor}. These attractors are behavioural: they are observed in generated text rather than computed as
invariant sets of a defined map. Feeding a model its own output during training degrades it over generations
\cite{shumailov2024collapse}; we study the inference-time loop of a model with fixed weights. The attractors of
recurrent networks have been related to the difficulty of training them \cite{ribeiro2020attractors}, and fixed points
with their linearizations have long been used to reverse-engineer trained recurrent networks
\cite{sussillo2013opening,maheswaranathan2019reverse}. Equilibrium, looped and recurrent-depth models iterate a block to a fixed point or for
many steps, and their stability has been analysed through Jacobians and fixed points
\cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,geiping2025recurrent,zhu2025ouro,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars,prairie2026parcae,buehlmaier2026layernorm,labovich2026stability,movahedi2026fixedpoint},
and recent analyses of looped reasoners follow their internal dynamics, their settling, marginal or drifting regimes
and slowly decaying oscillatory modes \cite{blayney2026mechanistic,viakhirev2026thinkshallow,park2026loopedearlier};
these studies examine fixed-point stability, finite-depth dynamics and nonstationary regimes. Here we continue
full-state periodic branches and classify their bifurcations in specified frozen-model feedback maps. A minimal recurrent self-attention with a repulsive value map
becomes chaotic \cite{gao2026repulsive}, but it is a constructed model, not a trained one. A separate line predicts the answers of looped models from the geometry of
score margins along completed finite-depth trajectories \cite{luo2026prediction}, without fixed points or Jacobians. The closest methodological precedent is a derivative-based fold analysis of a small task-trained
looped transformer, whose passage and spectral-gap predictions are unfitted \cite{kim2026phase}. That model was
trained for a task, whereas ours is a released pretrained language model, and our analysis also differs in its
objects: the fold of a primitive periodic orbit, the full-channel passage of a two-step map, and the accumulation law
of the windows, whose prefactor is set by the global reinjection. Expected-embedding
feedback itself is used in training \cite{zeng2025ponderlm} and, training-free, in pretrained models
\cite{zhang2025softthinking,wu2025singlethreaded}, next to continuous latent reasoning \cite{hao2024coconut}; a reduced
dynamical model explains why pretrained models fail to preserve such mixtures \cite{backour2026mixture}, and whether
they exploit the superposition the mixtures could carry has been questioned \cite{rizvimartel2026illusion}. Fixing
the prefix and the position turns that feedback into the autonomous map we analyse.

In neural systems more broadly, saddle-node ghosts produce quantitative delays in task-trained recurrent networks
\cite{dinc2026ghost}. Finite-order Feigenbaum ratios have been measured in neural rate and mass models
\cite{battaglia2007temporal,papasavvas2015gain} and, through period 128, in a transformer trained on logistic-map
trajectories \cite{liu2026learningchaos}. Learned networks reproduce period-doubling routes \cite{kim2021teaching},
and so does gradient descent itself \cite{kalra2025universal}. Normal forms and continuation have been applied to
recurrent neural maps, including their quasi-periodic orbits \cite{marichal2015multiple}, and shrimp-shaped windows and periodicity staircases appear in
neuron models \cite{gomez2014universal,stenzinger2025shrimp,caixeta2025staircase}. Our second control parameter comes
from activation steering along contrastive directions \cite{zou2023representation,zhang2025steering}. The
dynamical-systems tools are standard: numerical continuation and normal forms
\cite{kuznetsov2023elements,govaerts2000numerical}, for maps in particular \cite{kuznetsov2019maps}, type-I intermittency \cite{pomeau1980intermittent}, shrimps and
hubs \cite{gallas1993structure,facanha2013endoskeletons,vitolo2011hubs,jones2012rossler}, and period-doubling universality
\cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{Precision.} Every result on the soft-token map labelled float64 is computed in float64 arithmetic
throughout: embeddings, activations, attention keys and values (the prefix cache), normalizations (patched, since the
library computes RMS normalization in float32 by default), rotary tables, logits, the softmax and the fed-back token.
The released checkpoints store their weights in bfloat16, and a bfloat16 number is exactly a float64 number, so
converting the weights is exact. For large models the decoder's linear weights stay in their stored bfloat16 and are
converted to float64 at each use, which gives the same map as converting them once (bitwise on Qwen2.5-0.5B). The
designated float64 computations use the patched normalization and rotary paths; some diagnostic traces, such as the
step records of the gain split, are serialized in float32, as recorded in their artifacts. The historical two-GPU runs,
whose final normalization was computed in float32, are identified separately in Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}.

\paragraph{Phase maps.} Each cell of a phase map is one parameter pair. Unless stated otherwise, we discard 300
transient steps and measure over the next 300. The largest Lyapunov exponent comes from a twin trajectory at
offset $10^{-6}$ that is renormalized to that offset after every step, Benettin's method with a finite-difference
tangent \cite{benettin1980lyapunovI,benettin1980lyapunovII}; it estimates over a finite horizon the exponent that the
multiplicative ergodic theorem defines as a limit \cite{oseledets1968multiplicative}. At the 448 random cells of the
horizon check (Appendix~\ref{sec:limits}), an exact tangent vector carried by forward-mode differentiation
reproduces the twin exponents at offsets $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$ within 0.0041 (median 0.0004), with the same 178
chaotic labels, and at six chaotic cells the four leading exponents from QR iteration include exactly one positive one
(0.06 to 0.42; the next is $-0.64$ to $-1.10$). The period is the smallest $q\le32$ (48 in the later zooms) with return distance below
$10^{-8}$, and the attractor is identified by its leading-token loop, up to rotation. We call a cell chaotic when this
finite-horizon exponent exceeds $10^{-3}$ and no period is found; the label is a finite-horizon diagnostic, not a
proof of chaos. Maps draw the chaotic cells by this criterion: in colour by their exponent where a map shows the exponent, in
black where it shows periods or attractor text. Panels whose legend reads `Lyapunov $>$ 0.005' follow an older
convention that marks every cell whose exponent exceeds $0.005$, whether or not a period was found; on the five maps
we redrew with the criterion, the two differ in at most $0.56\,\%$ of a map's cells. A $256^2$ map takes about 100 minutes on one H100 GPU in float64. Every cell starts from the same
state, so where attractors coexist, a map shows the one whose basin contains that state. Labels are text, not
dynamical identities: one continuous orbit changes its label where two tokens tie for the maximum. We therefore call
two labelled regions different attractors only when the contraction rate jumps between them or when float64 orbits of
both exist at one parameter value.

\paragraph{Periodic orbits.} We solve $F^p(x)=x$ by Newton's method with forward-mode Jacobians. The multipliers are
the eigenvalues of the monodromy matrix; where the dense matrix is not float64, we refine them by a short Arnoldi
factorization with float64 Jacobian-vector products.

\paragraph{Continuation.} Periodic branches are continued in one parameter by pseudo-arclength continuation, and fold
and flip curves in two parameters with minimally augmented systems \cite{govaerts2000numerical,kuznetsov2023elements}.
The test function $g$ of the bordered system $[[M\mp I,v],[w^\top,0]]$ is obtained by GMRES (generalized minimal
residual iterations) with Jacobian-vector products, and its gradient by reverse-mode differentiation through a
Jacobian-vector product. When the parameter tangent reverses, we flag a cusp candidate and test it with the normal
form. To locate a codimension-two point on a continued curve (\texttt{tools/cusp\_locate.py}), we bracket a sign
change between two curve points, then retrace the curve from the left end with steps of a quarter of the bracket.
We do this for three rounds and finish with a secant step. The function whose sign changes is the oriented quadratic
coefficient $b\operatorname{sign}a$ for a cusp, $|\mu_2|-1$ for a second multiplier $\mu_2$ leaving the unit
circle, and the projected parameter tangent for a turn of the curve. At a cusp we then evaluate $c$ and the unfolding
matrix at the located point. Elsewhere, the multipliers and the angle between their eigenvectors type the point: a
double multiplier whose eigenvectors coincide is a Jordan block. On maps with known answers the tool recovers a
cusp to $10^{-6}$ (with $c=-1$ and $\det J=1$) and a fold-flip point.

\paragraph{Normal form and passages.} Consider a fold of the two-step map $G=F^2$, with right and left eigenvectors
$r$ and $\ell$ normalized by $\|r\|=1$ and $\ell^\top r=1$. Along the critical direction the reduced map is
$u'=u+a\eps+bu^2$, with $a=\ell^\top\partial_\alpha G$ and $b=\tfrac12\ell^\top D^2G[r,r]$. We compute $b$ by
central differences of float64 Jacobian-vector products over a step-size plateau (later cross-checks use nested
automatic differentiation), and $a$ includes the steering's effect on the prefix. The signed coefficients are kept in
the original eigenvector gauge, with $\eps$ measured in the free parameter ($\alpha$ or $\log_{10}T$, whose units
apply to $a$). On maps with known normal forms the code recovers $ab$, the cubic coefficient $c$ with its
centre-manifold term and the two-parameter unfolding matrix, and it traces the analytic fold and flip curves of the
H\'enon map (\texttt{tests/test\_bifurcation\_curves.py}). For passages we set $v=\operatorname{sign}(b)\,u$ and take the ghost side
$p-p_c=\operatorname{sign}(ab)\,\epsilon$ with $\epsilon>0$, so that $v'=v+|a|\epsilon+|b|v^2$ to quadratic order. A
full passage starts at $v=-U$ and a midpoint passage at $v=0$; both end at the first strict crossing $v>U$, with
$U=0.1/|b|$. We compare passage counts with the iterated quadratic map and with the arctangent formula
\cite{pomeau1980intermittent}.

\paragraph{Period-doubling cascade.} Along a cut in one parameter, we locate the flip of each period-$p$ orbit as the
root of $\operatorname{Re}\mu_c+1$, where $\mu_c$ is the multiplier with the smallest real part, using the Illinois
variant of regula falsi with warm-started orbits. All but one or two multipliers are small, so Newton's method on
$F^p(x)-x$ needs the monodromy only on a six-dimensional dominant subspace. We obtain that subspace by subspace
iteration with exact forward-mode Jacobian-vector products chained along the orbit, so no dense Jacobian is formed,
and the residual is always the exact float64 $F^p(x)-x$. Each root is classified before it is accepted. The critical
multiplier must be real and equal to $-1$, with a Ritz residual below $10^{-6}$, and the other computed Ritz values
(six in all, estimates of the dominant spectrum) must lie inside the unit circle. We then reach the period-$2p$ orbit
by forward iteration inside the next band. On the logistic map embedded in six dimensions, the procedure reproduces
the first four flip parameters to $10^{-9}$.

\section{Theory background, network contraction and the dimension of the chaos}
\label{sec:theorymore}
\input{figures/tikz-reduction}
\paragraph{Why the structures of low-dimensional maps appear.} The map acts on a space of hundreds to thousands of
dimensions, yet two reductions make low-dimensional theory apply (Figure~\ref{fig:tworeductions}). Near a bifurcation of a periodic orbit the centre
manifold theorem reduces the dynamics exactly to the critical directions, whatever the size of the other multipliers,
as long as they stay off the unit circle. Our normal forms of folds, flips, cusps and 1:1 resonances rest on this
reduction alone. A scalar description of a whole attractor needs additional, global evidence. The small non-critical
multipliers of the periodic orbits we computed show strong transverse contraction near those orbits and motivate that
description: they range from about $0.1$ at short periods to below $10^{-13}$ along the cascade
(Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism} and~\ref{sec:cascade} and Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}), and along the cascade at $\log_{10}T=-0.156$ the next modulus
falls from $0.12$ at the first flip to $0.045$, $7\times10^{-5}$, $4\times10^{-7}$ and $2\times10^{-13}$ at the next
four. Temperature, through the gain of \eqref{eq:gain}, and steering act as two parameters. One-dimensional families of
maps with a quadratic extremum can exhibit fold and flip curves, nondegenerate cusps, period-doubling cascades whose
interval ratios tend to Feigenbaum's $\delta$, and saddle-node windows that accumulate on a fold with
$d_N\propto N^{-2}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} sketches the objects we locate); we test these predictions on specific branches and parameter sequences in Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism} to~\ref{sec:organization}. The organization of shrimps needs
more, either a second turning point of the reduced map or a second dimension
\cite{gallas1993structure,jones2012rossler}. We test the consequences where we state them: the multipliers of each
orbit, the interval ratios, and the normal-form predictions of passage times and coexisting orbits. Where the second
multiplier is not small, as in the high-temperature bubble of Section~\ref{sec:cascade} (next moduli $0.28$ and
$0.38$) and along the period-5 curves of Section~\ref{sec:organization} (up to $0.39$), the global reduction is only a rough guide, while the local normal forms still apply.

\paragraph{Coexisting attractors constrain one-dimensional descriptions.} A classical theorem says what a
one-dimensional reduction cannot do. If an interval map is three times differentiable, unimodal and has negative
Schwarzian derivative, $Sf=f'''/f'-\tfrac32(f''/f')^2<0$, the immediate basin of every attracting periodic orbit
contains the turning point or an endpoint of the interval \cite{singer1978stable}; if basins reaching an endpoint are
excluded, at most one attracting periodic orbit remains. The soft-token map has coexisting attracting orbits in several
places. Near each located cusp with
$c<0$, two attracting orbits of the same period coexist with a saddle between them (periods 4 and 6, Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}; period 7, Section~\ref{sec:hostile}). Inside the hostile period-7 body, the period-7 loop
coexists with a fixed point, which the fixed start reaches at isolated cells. Such coexistence constrains a scalar
interpretation: unless one attractor's basin reaches an end of the scalar interval, the effective one-dimensional
dynamics cannot be a single unimodal map with negative Schwarzian derivative, and needs a second turning point, a
stretch of positive Schwarzian derivative, or a second dimension. A second turning point is also what a doubly
superstable shrimp centre requires.

\input{figures/tikz-atlas}

\paragraph{What theory allows.} Chaos and periodic windows occur in many nonlinear feedback
systems. In a specified ensemble of random recurrent networks with asymmetric couplings, chaos sets in above a
critical gain, sharply in the large-network limit, and finite networks pass through limit cycles of increasing
complexity on the way \cite{sompolinsky1988chaos}; how the transition looks depends on the architecture and the
transfer function \cite{kadmon2015transition}. Discrete-time random networks, $x(t+1)=\tanh(gJx(t))$, iterate a map
as our loop does: their fixed point loses stability when the gain times the spectral radius of the couplings exceeds
one, the first bifurcation of large networks is a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation, and chaos follows by a quasi-periodic
route, through a torus and frequency locking, over a range of gain that narrows as the network grows
\cite{doyon1994bifurcations}. Our largest model narrows differently: Qwen2.5-72B passes from a confident echo to a
nearly uniform fixed point within 0.03 decades of temperature, and its chaos is confined to narrow bands, whereas in
random networks chaos is generic above the transition. External input can suppress that chaos
\cite{rajan2010stimulus}, and deep networks at initialization show an order-to-chaos transition in signal propagation
\cite{poole2016exponential,schoenholz2017deep}. In our map, temperature plays the role of an inverse gain and the
steering vector that of an input. Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes the correspondence concrete through an exact
chain-rule factorization, with the softmax derivative $(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top})/T$ in the place of the slope
of the transfer function; the identification with the random-network transition remains an analogy, not a transfer
of that ensemble's theorem. Feigenbaum scaling holds for a class of one-dimensional maps with a quadratic maximum
\cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}, and periodic windows, shrimp-shaped domains with their cusps and spirals, and
doubling cascades are found in many dissipative two-parameter systems
\cite{gallas1993structure,bonatto2008periodicity,vitolo2011hubs,facanha2013endoskeletons} and in experiments
\cite{stoop2010real}; intermittency near folds is classical \cite{pomeau1980intermittent}.

\paragraph{Contraction and expansion in the network.} Analyses of deep attention identify contracting tendencies:
pure self-attention collapses token representations toward a common vector with depth \cite{dong2021attention}, and
idealized attention dynamics form clusters \cite{geshkovski2023emergence}. Residual connections and MLPs counteract
the collapse \cite{dong2021attention}. Which tendency wins along an orbit is a property of the trained weights that
only computation settles. In every dense model we mapped, both outcomes occur: the same map has negative largest
exponents in some regions of its parameter plane and positive ones in others.

\paragraph{How many dimensions does the chaos have?} Near a periodic orbit, the multipliers measure how many
directions the dynamics keeps (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). For chaotic attractors the corresponding measure is the
Kaplan--Yorke dimension \cite{kaplan1979chaotic}, $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=j+\sum_{i\le j}\lambda_i/|\lambda_{j+1}|$ with $j$ the
largest index at which the partial sum of the ordered Lyapunov exponents is still nonnegative; with one positive
exponent and $\lambda_1+\lambda_2<0$, $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=1+\lambda_1/|\lambda_2|$. We estimated the four leading
exponents by QR iteration (Appendix~\ref{sec:methods}; 1000 transient and 2000 measured steps, float64) at 35 completed
cells: 28 chosen by percentile of their largest exponent before any of their spectra were seen (of 30 requested), and
seven from the earlier horizon check, six chaotic and one periodic control. At this horizon 31 have a positive leading
estimate and none has two positive estimates. These are finite-time estimates, not asymptotic dimensions. They grow
with temperature. Along the hostile temperature line, where we fit a scalar return relation (Section~\ref{sec:hostile}), eight chaotic cells have $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=1.008$ to $1.16$, consistent with nearly
one-dimensional chaos. At fourteen chaotic cells of the
headline maps ($\log_{10}T$ from $-0.17$ to $-0.04$) it is $1.03$ to $1.65$, rising with $\lambda_1$. In the
high-temperature region ($\log_{10}T$ from $-0.02$ to $0.13$), nine chaotic cells have $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=1.37$ to
$2.02$; at two of them the sum of the first two estimates is nonnegative, which suggests a second expanding direction
at this horizon. A scalar return
map describes the low-temperature chaos well and the high-temperature chaos poorly, consistent with the large second
multipliers of the high-temperature bubble of Section~\ref{sec:cascade}. The number of competing tokens does not
set the dimension. The readout's
trace-to-leading-eigenvalue ratio, $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_p/\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_p)$ averaged along the orbit (with
$\lambda_{\max}$ estimated by eight power iterations), counts the directions in which the readout amplifies comparably.
$D_{\mathrm{KY}}$ never exceeds it and rises with it within each group of cells (Spearman $0.72$ over 17 philosophy
cells and $0.69$ over 8 hostile cells, computed when the data came in and not independently reproduced; both are
confounded with temperature). Across groups it does not predict the dimension: the hostile cells have ratios of about
16 to 19 and next-token entropies of about 7 to 9 nats, yet $D_{\mathrm{KY}}\le1.16$. The readout supplies many competing directions; the network decides how many
of them expand.
\section{Behavioural tests in detail}
\label{sec:behaviour}
We looked for a behavioural signature of the regimes outside the loop. Two exploratory designs found no accuracy
signature that survives controls. First, ordinary sampled answers of Qwen2.5-0.5B under steering at the strengths of chaotic and periodic map
cells (30 matched pairs; arithmetic, open questions, short programs) showed no measurable accuracy contrast: at those
strengths, steering every position turns every reply into philosophy-flavoured text, so accuracy sits at the floor in
both zones (3 against 4 correct of 4500 arithmetic answers). Of nine secondary contrasts of entropy and reply length,
one (open questions, next-token entropy, $p=0.016$) passes $0.05$ uncorrected, not after a correction for nine tests,
and the entropy contrasts change sign across tasks. Second, labelling each of 200 prompts by the regime of its own unsteered loop in Qwen2.5-7B, prompts with
a chaotic loop were answered far less accurately (by 26 to 37 points at three temperatures). The chaotic labels fell
mostly on the harder task, and in a linear model with task, greedy correctness and the sampled answers' entropy, the
label's coefficient is $-3.7$ to $-4.6$ points (permutation $p$ from $0.07$ to $0.20$). These are associations: the
entropy is measured during sampling, so the adjustment does not identify a causal effect of the regime.

A frozen test addresses the second design. Its design, covariates and analysis were pushed before any data. It uses
4000 fresh products of two two-digit numbers and the unsteered Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at $\log_{10}T=0.40$: 1484 prompts
have a chaotic loop and 2509 a periodic one (seven with other labels are excluded). Chaotic-loop prompts are again answered less accurately by ordinary
sampling, by 7.1 points (95\,\% interval $[4.8,9.3]$; 10 samples per prompt). The gap vanishes once seven difficulty
covariates, all fixed before sampling, enter the fit. Beyond them the label's coefficient is $+0.15$ points
($[-1.4,1.7]$, permutation $p=0.84$), and the label adds no held-out predictive value. The first-token entropy alone
absorbs nearly all of the gap (post hoc: $-0.8$ points remain). This is what the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} predicts. The loop turns chaotic where next tokens compete, and a model unsure of its first
token also answers worse. Beyond that uncertainty, no association of the loop's regime with accuracy is detected at
this precision; this does not show that the regime has no effect.

Within a prompt no association is detected either. We steered the first 1000 of these prompts slightly, at block 14
along philosophy, hostility-vs-kindness and a random direction. The strength, 1\,\% of the residual norm, is the largest
at which greedy accuracy stayed within 5 points of the unsteered model on the first 200 of these prompts (so the
calibration overlaps the test prompts); on the 1000 prompts it is 0.87 to 0.89, against 0.89 unsteered. Under these
pushes the loop's label differs between conditions for 508 of the 1000 prompts. With prompt and condition fixed
effects (3992 prompt-condition rows), in an analysis fixed before the data, a chaotic label is associated with a change
of $+0.36$ points in the accuracy of the prompt's ordinary sampled answers (95\,\% interval $[-0.36,1.08]$;
permutation within prompts, $p=0.34$). This does not identify a causal effect of entering chaos: the pushes can act on
the answers through other paths as well.

Nor does accuracy peak at the edge of chaos. Adjusted for the covariates, three groups of prompts (3603 of the 4000;
the rest have intermediate exponents) are answered alike:
\begin{itemize}
  \item deep periodic ($\lambda\le-0.3$, 1961 prompts): $+0.3$ points;
  \item edge ($|\lambda|\le0.1$, 242 prompts): $-0.1$ points;
  \item deep chaotic ($\lambda\ge0.3$, 1400 prompts): $+0.3$ points.
\end{itemize}
All three intervals lie within $[-3.1,3.0]$. The edge contrast is $-0.4$ points ($[-3.5,2.6]$), and the curvature in
$\lambda$ is positive, the opposite of an edge optimum. Post hoc, the curvature comes from the most strongly
contracting tenth of prompts ($\lambda\le-3.42$), answered 5.3 points ($[3.0,7.6]$) better than the covariates
predict. That is a lead for a future test, not a finding.

\section{Bifurcation curves and cusps}
\label{sec:curves}
\paragraph{Period-8 stripe.} Continued for 80 points with a float64 test function, the fold curve of the period-8
orbit keeps its critical multiplier within $1\pm6\times10^{-6}$, follows the stripe's upper edge and caps its top near
$\log_{10}T=-0.065$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stripes}). At $\alpha=0.0717$ the stripe lies between a flip at
$\log_{10}T=-0.128955$ and the fold at $-0.1033462$. Continued downward, the flip curve keeps its multiplier at $-1$
for 115 points to $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0776,-0.402)$, with two reversals of its parameter tangent near
$(0.0750,-0.219)$ and $(0.0746,-0.206)$, and the fold curve keeps its multiplier at $+1$ for 120 points to
$(0.0758,-0.200)$. Located by bisection along the curve (Appendix~\ref{sec:methods}), the two reversals of the flip
curve, at $(0.074955,-0.219336)$ and $(0.074608,-0.206007)$, carry no second multiplier near the unit circle (the
next ones are $3\times10^{-7}$ and $1\times10^{-5}$). On a flip curve the parameter tangent can vanish only where a
second multiplier reaches $+1$, so both are tight but smooth turns of the curve in the parameter plane, not
codimension-two points.

\paragraph{The accumulation boundary.} The fold curve of the 2-cycle ``is it'', continued from
$(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0628,-0.115)$ in both directions (38 and 126 points, critical multiplier $1$ to $10^{-7}$, no
cusp), traces the boundary of the period-2 region in Figures~\ref{fig:stripes} and~\ref{fig:cross512}. The stripe fan
accumulates on this boundary, as the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} requires. Further along one direction,
outside the plotted window, another multiplier leaves the unit circle (moduli up to 11). Located along the curve,
this second real multiplier reaches $+1$ at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.035628,-0.341724)$, where the eigenvectors of
the two multipliers at $+1$ coincide to $3\times10^{-4}$ degrees: a double multiplier $+1$ with a Jordan block,
the 1:1 resonance of maps (the analogue of a Bogdanov--Takens point).

\paragraph{A period-6 window with shrimp-like anatomy.} The hooks of Figure~\ref{fig:zooms} (bottom) belong to
the period-6 loop ``. -\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe is a''. Float64 orbits (Newton residuals
$10^{-15}$--$10^{-13}$) show that its curved body and its thin leg are one window: the multipliers are $-0.061$ and
$+0.048$ at the body, $-0.564$ lower down and $-0.645$ on the leg. The foot is its period-doubled loop (period 12,
leading multiplier $-0.156$). Inside the body lies a different 6-cycle, ``.The\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe is
a'', whose leading multipliers are a complex pair, $-0.031\pm0.152i$. One-parameter branches from the body end in
folds in every direction: at $\log_{10}T=-0.0558$ and $-0.0721$ in temperature, at $\alpha=0.08074$ in steering, and,
toward smaller $\alpha$, in two folds only $10^{-5}$ apart. Such a close pair is the signature of a nearby cusp. A
flip at $\alpha\approx0.0797$ then opens the period-12 foot. The body is not effectively one-dimensional either. At two body cells the leading
multiplier is a complex pair, $-0.020\pm0.264i$, or has a second multiplier of comparable size ($-0.261$ against
$0.099$). Below the body, where the window's strip appears to cross a period-5 strip near
$(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0773,-0.0774)$, both orbits continued from their own strips are unstable at the crossing
(leading multipliers $-1.88$ and $-6.59$). The crossing is therefore neither a centre nor a point where two attractors
coexist.

Fold curves that bound a window and meet in cusps, with flip curves inside, form the codimension-two organization of
a shrimp \cite{facanha2013endoskeletons} (complex dynamics of small recurrent networks has been studied in the same
spirit \cite{pasemann2002complex}). In the continued curves (Figure~\ref{fig:z3curves}), a single fold arc bounds the
body on its outer side and a flip arc on its inner side, and the leg lies between them where they converge. The outer
fold arc extends far to the left as a thin arm and ends in a cusp at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.069106,-0.039750)$.
The fold curve through the close fold pair has a second cusp inside the body, at $(0.080097,-0.058344)$, from
which two fold branches descend.

At both reversals the quadratic coefficient $b$ changes sign between consecutive curve points while $a$ stays away
from zero (Table~\ref{tab:z3cusps}).
Because the signs of $a$ and $b$ both flip with the orientation of the critical eigenvector, the test uses
$b\operatorname{sign}a$. A cusp is nondegenerate when the cubic coefficient $c$ of the normal form
$u'=u+a\varepsilon+bu^2+cu^3$ is nonzero where $b$ vanishes. We compute $c$ with the second-order centre-manifold
term, $c=\ell^\top C(r,r,r)/6+\ell^\top B(r,h_2)/2$ with $(I-M)h_2=B(r,r)-2br$ and $\ell^\top h_2=0$, where $M$ is
the monodromy matrix and $B$ and $C$ are the second and third derivatives of its map, obtained by central differences
of float64 Jacobian-vector products. (The code reproduces $c$ to $10^{-6}$ on a map with a known reduction.) At the two
curve points that bracket each sign change, $c$ is nonzero far beyond its step-size uncertainty and has the same sign,
and $b$ changes sign with nonzero slope between them. This supports a nondegenerate cusp inside each bracket. Located along each fold curve (Appendix~\ref{sec:methods}),
$b\operatorname{sign}a$ falls to below $5\times10^{-6}$ of its bracket values at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.069106,-0.039750)$
on the outer arc and at $(0.080097,-0.058344)$ inside the body. At these same points $c=-1.51\times10^{4}$ and
$-2.38\times10^{5}$, and the unfolding matrix $J$ defined below is regular ($\det J=2416$ and $14.0$): two
numerically located cusps. (Location uses the small-step oriented coefficient $b\operatorname{sign}a$; the unfolding
coefficients use a separate finite-difference ladder, and the selected plateau of $c$ spreads by at most 4.8\,\% over
adjacent steps.) The
negative sign of $c$ means that near each cusp, two stable period-6 orbits coexist with a saddle between the fold
branches.

A generic codimension-two cusp also needs the two parameters to unfold it independently. Near the cusp the reduced
map is $u'=u+\beta_1+\beta_2u+bu^2+cu^3$, where $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ vanish at the cusp and depend on the
parameters. The unfolding is regular when $J=\partial(\beta_1,\beta_2)/\partial(\log_{10}T,10\alpha)$ has rank two
($\det J\neq0$). We first estimated $J$ at a fold point near the outer-arc cusp (where $b\operatorname{sign}a=14.7$):
$\det J=2737$ (singular values $1630$ and $1.7$), so the estimated unfolding is nonsingular there, as it is at the
located cusp ($\det J=2416$). With these derivatives, the local cubic model predicted the number of period-6 orbits and their
stability at six parameter offsets, written to disk before any orbit was solved. Newton solves started from the
predicted roots and from the base state recovered the predicted number of distinct orbits, with the predicted
stability, at all six. The search was seeded, not exhaustive: orbits that these starts do not reach would be missed.

\section{Steering toward hostility: further results}
\label{sec:hostility}
This appendix gives further results on the hostility direction of Section~\ref{sec:hostile}: the details of the shrimp-like shapes, the period-5 window of a plane steered at two depths, its orbit census and a closure test.

\paragraph{Details of the shrimp-like shapes.} Section~\ref{sec:organization} summarizes the anatomy (Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}); three details belong here. In higher dimensions a window can also end where a
complex pair crosses the unit circle, and the leading multiplier can change sign through a complex pair, as it does in
parts of our windows; beyond some of the flip curves we followed lie the doubling sequences of
Section~\ref{sec:cascade}, but a flip need not start a complete cascade. The period-6 hook's orbit never comes close
to superstable: its leading multiplier stays above $0.028$ in modulus. And the X-shaped crossing of a period-5 and a
period-6 strip (Figure~\ref{fig:zooms}, bottom) is not one window's anatomy but two windows overlapping, and at the
crossing both of their orbits are unstable (Appendix~\ref{sec:curves}). The shapes in these one-block planes are
therefore the two-parameter unfolding of windows whose return maps have one coarse extremum: shrimp-like in body,
legs and internal cascade, but without a one-dimensional shrimp centre on the tracked branches.

\paragraph{Two turning points from two steering depths.} Steering at two depths gives the band dynamics a second
turning point. We drew 1584 chaotic cells (200 from each of the four two-block planes of Figure~\ref{fig:twoblock},
150 from each one-block map of Figure~\ref{fig:layersweep} and the 34 of the hostile line of
Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade}), counted the turning points
of each band's return map and inspected every strong candidate by eye, since a projection can fold a single hump. Three
cells, all in two-block planes at $\log_{10}T=-0.1$, have smooth, single-valued return maps with two or more turning
points (Figure~\ref{fig:bimodal}); the other strong candidates were point sets, split bands or folded projections, or
were not reproduced. In one cell (hostility at block~4, gratitude at block~16), every one-dimensional phase of a
20-band attractor is N-shaped: a bimodal map. A $128\times128$ zoom around another, whose map has three maxima, shows
a period-5 window with a body, legs, a period-10 and period-20 border, and patches where the fixed start settles on
period~4 (Figure~\ref{fig:period5}a). We continued the window's period-5 orbit over a $31\times31$ grid of its body:
608 cells solved, all with the same token loop, 447 of them attracting. In 61 of these 447 cells, the nearest cell of
the zoom (at most $2.0\times10^{-5}$ away in $\alpha_1$ and $3.2\times10^{-5}$ in $\alpha_2$) settles on period~4, so
attractors of periods 4 and 5 coexist at grid scale, which a single unimodal map with negative Schwarzian derivative
allows only if a basin reaches an end of its interval (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).

The window still has no one-dimensional centre. The leading multiplier of the continued orbit never comes near zero:
its modulus is at least $0.0139$ in all 608 cells. Where a one-dimensional map would be superstable, it meets the
second multiplier, and the two form a complex pair (91 cells, in narrow strips) or, where their product is negative
(41 other cells), pass each other as real numbers of opposite sign (Figure~\ref{fig:period5}c). Per step, the second
direction contracts by a median factor of $0.44$ and the first by $0.76$ over the attracting cells, so near these
strips the orbit's linearization has two comparable leading directions. The curves on which the trace $\mu_1+\mu_2$ changes sign take the place of
superstable curves, and they do not cross: no $2\times2$ block of attracting cells has the alternating signs of a
crossing (Figure~\ref{fig:period5}b). One isolated cell holds a repelling orbit with the same token loop
($\mu_1=1.65$), and near the top of the grid one column of Newton solves failed above cells whose leading multiplier
rises to $0.88$; both are explained by the window's fold curve (next paragraph). A second turning point
in the band maps therefore does not give this window a one-dimensional shrimp centre. Like the hostile body, it is a window whose
orbit has two comparable leading directions near its superstable strips, and whether it is the shrimp of a
two-dimensional map is open.
\paragraph{The folds and flips of the period-5 window.} To resolve the folds the grid did not, we continued the
window's period-5 orbit along four lines from one attracting cell, $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)=(-0.118730,0.100693)$, and
then the boundaries the lines found, each in both directions (float64; 18 to 73 points per line, 30 to 120 per curve;
one of the eight curve runs failed at its start for lack of GPU memory, and its curve is covered by the other trace of
the same fold). At every curve point the residual is below $10^{-10}$ and the test function below $10^{-9}$. The
critical multiplier stays real and within $6\times10^{-8}$ of $+1$ on the fold curve and of $-1$ on the flip curves,
and the largest other multiplier modulus on any curve is $0.122$. Going right and up from the start, the orbit dies
in folds at $\alpha_1=-0.118166$ and $\alpha_2=0.102715$ (next moduli $0.0012$ and $0.0008$). Going left and down, it
first loses stability in flips (at $\alpha_1=-0.12147$ and $\alpha_2=0.09954$), regains it where the same multiplier
crosses $-1$ again (at $-0.12305$ and $0.09586$; a doubling bubble), and dies in a fold (at $-0.123323$ and
$0.095720$). Three of these four folds lie on one continued fold curve (within $3.1\times10^{-7}$); the left one lies on a
second. The first fold curve bounds the lower leg on its right, runs up the body's right edge and over its top, and

\paragraph{A closure test that failed.} We also tested whether two coordinates forecast the window's nonlinear return

followed for eight returns; quadratic return models in one, two or four modal coordinates (the spectral projections
on the leading eigenvectors), with a fitted lift back to the state, were iterated from the first return, and the error
is the squared forecast error over the squared deviation, summed over returns 2 to 8. The test failed for numerical
reasons: at most locations the iterated models diverged, with median errors up to $2\times10^{122}$, against at most

one-coordinate model diverges), and no regularized refit was run. At one location the divergence of the four-coordinate model satisfied the
frozen two-coordinate criterion spuriously. A guard added after the run marks fits with a median error above 1 as
degenerate; 12 of the 18 location-and-size rows are. At the body location, one coordinate gives errors of $0.026$ and

\section{Bridge to sampled decoding}
\label{sec:bridge}
How much of the fold mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} survives sampling? To find out, we replace the expected
embedding by the mean of $m$ sampled token
embeddings (Figure~\ref{fig:bridge}). With the exact map, laminar phases near the fold of the 2-cycle ``is it'' last 120 and 63 steps at
$\eps=10^{-5}$ and $3\times10^{-5}$. With $m=64$ they last about 10.6 steps, independently of $\eps$ (57\% of steps in
the channel), and with $m=8$ about 7.7 steps (38\%). With single samples ($m=1$, sampled decoding of the same
truncated system), essentially no laminar phases remain (11\% of steps in the channel). The deterministic
intermittency mechanism therefore belongs to the expected-embedding map. Sampling noise turns the bottleneck into a
noise-limited trap whose strength grows with $m$.

\paragraph{Zero-temperature limit and ordinary greedy decoding.} As $T\to0$ the soft-token map becomes greedy
decoding with a one-token memory, $g(t)=\arg\max_s z_s(\text{prefix},E_t)$. This is a map on the vocabulary, and its
cycles are the zero-temperature attractors. For Qwen2.5-0.5B we computed $g$ for all 151\,936 tokens. Unsteered, it
has 1060 cycles, and one of them, the period-5 loop ``![](https:// Hello'', drains 86\,\% of the vocabulary; it is
the largest attractor family of every 0.5B soft-token map. Ordinary greedy decoding with a growing context does not
inherit these cycles (Table~\ref{tab:memory}). From 220 starts (the first tokens of the 20 largest cycles and 200
random tokens), 19 runs end in a short loop within 256 tokens, and the dominant 5-cycle never appears. Steering
along philosophy makes ordinary decoding loop almost always (218 of 220 runs at $\alpha=0.1$), but in other loops,
mostly punctuation such as `` --'', none of them one-token cycles. Greedy decoding that keeps only the last $K$
tokens interpolates between the two. For the tested $K\ge2$ most final loops differ from the one-token cycles: at
$K=2$ only 7 of 220 unsteered runs and 1 of 220 steered runs keep one of them. Unsteered, looping
collapses to the level of the growing context between $K=16$ and $32$; steered, the share of runs whose loop equals
the growing-context loop rises from 0 at $K=1$ to 58\,\% at $K=64$. The soft-token map's low-temperature attractors
are therefore those of decoding with a one-token memory. Which loop ordinary decoding enters depends on the context
it keeps, but the tendency of steering to drive decoding into loops is shared. In ordinary decoding, repetition is
known to reinforce itself \cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}, a theoretical analysis ties loops to words of
high inflow \cite{fu2021repetition}, and induction heads, which copy
earlier context \cite{olsson2022induction}, are one mechanism by which a longer context can favour loops that repeat
earlier text.

\paragraph{Soft thinking with a growing context.} Soft Thinking \cite{zhang2025softthinking} feeds back the same
expected embedding but appends it to the context, so that every earlier soft token stays visible. We ran that
decoding in float64 from the paper's prompt and displaced the first soft token of twin trajectories by $10^{-10}$,
without renormalization; the distance between the newest soft tokens then shows whether the early perturbation grows.
The context grows, so these are finite-time growth rates of a non-autonomous system, not exponents of an attractor.
In Qwen2.5-7B the perturbation grows exponentially at temperatures inside the one-token map's chaotic range: by 0.77
to 0.82 per step at $\log_{10}T=0.17$ and $0.21$, reaching $10^{-3}$ within 49 to 58 steps, and it reaches $10^{-3}$
within 400 steps at nine other temperatures between $-0.10$ and $0.40$, at 0.02 to 0.18 per step (0.8 at $0.33$ for two
of three twins). It does not grow at the sharpest distributions
($\log_{10}T\le-0.14$) or at nearly uniform ones ($\log_{10}T\ge0.44$), where it stays between $10^{-13}$ and
$10^{-7}$ for 400 steps. In Qwen2.5-0.5B the growth moves to sharper distributions: it appears at six of the seven temperatures from
$\log_{10}T=-0.30$ to $-0.07$ (up to 0.33 per step) and at $0.09$, with a late transient burst at $0.05$, and is absent
from $0.13$ up, which includes its narrow one-token chaotic band. Sensitive
dependence therefore survives when the whole context is kept, and in the 7B model the one-token map locates it.
Qwen2.5-72B, run the same way on two GPUs, stays nearly insensitive. We count a temperature as growing when two of its three
twins reach $10^{-3}$ within 400 steps, a rule fixed before the 72B run and applied afterwards to the earlier 0.5B
and 7B records, where it gives 11 of 24 temperatures for the 7B model and 8 for the 0.5B model. The 72B model grows at one of 24 temperatures on the paper's prompt, and only
transiently: at $\log_{10}T=0.09$ the twins pass $10^{-3}$ at step 123 and fall back to $5\times10^{-6}$ (at $0.13$ one twin of three passes
$10^{-3}$ at step 381). It grows at
none of the 24 completed temperatures on the held-out prompt with its narrow unsteered band (a planned finer line there
was not completed), including $\log_{10}T=0.326$, between two temperatures
where its one-token map is chaotic; there the distance never exceeds $1.1\times10^{-9}$. It also grows at none of the
eleven steered cells of Appendix~\ref{sec:size}, including the six where its one-token map is chaotic: there the
distance never exceeds $3\times10^{-7}$ and ends between $1\times10^{-13}$ and $6\times10^{-10}$, and the growing
context holds the next-token entropy at 2.3 to 3.6 nats instead of the one-token map's 7.3 to 11.1. At these 72B
prompts and cells, over 400 steps, the growing-context decoder therefore did not reproduce the sustained sensitivity of
the fixed-window loop, while the one transient burst shows that the method registers growth at this scale.

\section{Replication and scale}
\label{sec:scale}
In Qwen2.5-1.5B (steering at layer 14 of 28), a coarse $64^2$ float64 map over the range of the 0.5B maps again shows chaotic
regions with embedded windows. A $256^2$ zoom (Figure~\ref{fig:qwen15}) resolves a chaotic band near
$\log_{10}T=0$--$0.13$ (14{,}422 of 65{,}536 cells), with a branch descending along $\alpha\approx0.06$ and periodic
windows inside it. The large periodic regions are question loops: ``What?\textbackslash n'' (period 2),
``\textbackslash nWhat is a'' (period 4) and ``What continuum?\textbackslash n'' (period 3). Unlike the 0.5B model,
no period-adding fan was resolved in this map.

A cut of 2048 cells at $\alpha=0.100$ (Figure~\ref{fig:qwen15route}) shows how the band is entered from below. The
2-cycle ``What?\textbackslash n'' gives way abruptly to a period-4 loop, and the Lyapunov exponent jumps, which is
consistent with a switch between attractors rather than a resolved smooth doubling. The period-4 loop then doubles, with float64 flips at
$\log_{10}T=-0.0031269$ (period $4\to8$, next modulus $0.014$), $-0.0021666$ (period $8\to16$, next modulus
$8\times10^{-5}$), $-0.0019002$ (period $16\to32$, next modulus $5\times10^{-9}$) and $-0.0018380$ (period
$32\to64$). Along the way the exponent rises continuously to zero, and chaos follows. The interval ratios are $3.60$,
$4.28$, $4.58$, $4.648$ and $4.665$; the last three use the flips of the period-64, period-128 and period-256 orbits
at $\log_{10}T=-0.0018244$, $-0.00182151$ and $-0.00182088$ (next moduli below $3\times10^{-14}$). The last ratio
differs from Feigenbaum's $4.6692$ by $0.0044$ (0.1\,\%); the period-256 flip is located to about $3\times10^{-14}$
in $\log_{10}T$, the floor set by float64 parameters. Like those of the 0.5B crescent at $\log_{10}T=-0.148$, the
ratios rise toward $4.669$. Their higher start does not
indicate a temperature trend, since along nearby cuts of the 0.5B crescent the early ratios range from $2.84$ to
$14.9$ (Section~\ref{sec:cascade}).

\paragraph{The fold law at 1.5B and 7B.} We tested whether the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, windows
accumulating on the fold of a short cycle with $d_N=C/(N+\delta)^2$, recurs in larger models. The criterion, a
constant $d_N(N+\delta)^2$ within 10\,\% over at least four windows for one offset $\delta$, was fixed before the lines
were computed; a disclosed correction to the period-progression selector after the first scoring kept the conclusions
for the nearest four windows and changed which farther windows were selected. In Qwen2.5-1.5B (philosophy direction,
layer 14, $\alpha=0.058588$) the period-4 orbit folds at $\log_{10}T_c=-0.0912029$ (multiplier $1$ to
$3\times10^{-14}$, next modulus $0.008$), with normal-form coefficients $a=-7.16$ per unit $\log_{10}T$ and
$b=-21.09$, so $ab=151.0$. A float64 line of 1700 cells, 1570 of them above the fold, shows
windows of periods $4N$ accumulating on it, from single cells at periods 44--64 to a wide period-12 window. Measured in $\log_{10}T$ from $\log_{10}T_c$ to their edges nearest the fold, the four nearest resolved windows (periods 28--40) meet the criterion
within $0.12\,\%$ ($\delta=-0.99$, $C=0.0612$). With the windows farther out the spread rises to $10.3$--$14.0\,\%$;
there several periods recur in multiple disconnected runs on the line's $10^{-5}$ grid, and grid-spaced edges no
longer identify the fold edges. In Qwen2.5-7B ($\alpha=0.051429$) the period-2 orbit folds at $\log_{10}T_c=-0.2040156$ (next modulus $0.0092$,
$ab=55.7$), and odd windows accumulate on it. The nearest four and five (periods 11--17 and 9--17) meet the criterion
within $4.5\,\%$, and six (to period 7) within $6.1\,\%$. Each window's own doubling or tripling lies on its far side,
as for the 0.5B windows of periods 16--20; this organization is consistent with fold edges on the near side, and the
fitted distances use grid estimates of those edges. The effective passage fraction $f=\sqrt{Cab}/\pi$ is about $0.97$
at 1.5B and $0.78$ to $0.81$ at 7B, against $0.58$ at 0.5B. The fold and its window law thus recur at all three sizes,
while $f$, an effective constant that absorbs the global reinjection, differs between the models. With one
fold per model, crossed in different coordinates ($\alpha$ at 0.5B, $\log_{10}T$ at 1.5B and 7B), no trend of $ab$
with size can be read.

\paragraph{A mixture-of-experts model.} Qwen3.5-35B-A3B differs from the dense models in two ways. Thirty of its 40
layers use linear (Gated DeltaNet) attention, with gated full attention in every fourth layer, and each layer routes
a token to 8 of 256 experts plus a shared expert, so that about 3B of its 35B parameters are active. Hugging Face
computes several parts of this architecture in float32 even when the model is cast to float64 (the normalizations,
the delta-rule recurrences, the rotary tables and the router softmax), and some tensors are stored in float32; we
patch these paths, restore the stored tensors exactly, and disable the thinking block that the default chat template
opens (a first run with the block open is kept in the research log). Steered at layer 20, the map behaves unlike
those of the dense models. Unsteered, 128 temperatures over $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$ give no positive exponent (largest
$-0.265$): periodic loops at all but 15 temperatures (``!Hello'' with period 2 at 92 of them, other periods up to 52),
and the remaining 15, above $\log_{10}T\approx0.1$, contract without a detected period. Along philosophy, none of 1024
cells is chaotic (largest exponent $-0.112$). Along hostility, 52 of 1024 cells ($5.1\,\%$) have a positive exponent
and no period, against $28\,\%$ for Llama-3.1 8B on the same plane. Routing is discontinuous: a twin trajectory that
crosses a routing boundary jumps, which can inflate a finite-difference exponent, so we call these cells irregular
rather than chaotic. Whether the contraction comes from the experts or from the Qwen3.5 architecture would take the
dense Qwen3.5-9B as a control, which we have not run.

\paragraph{Can a cheap property predict it?} Motivated by the saturation and high-temperature limits of
Section~\ref{sec:theory}, we tested the breadth of intermediate entropy as a heuristic predictor: a model whose
next-token distribution jumps from sharp to flat over a small range of temperature might leave little room for
chaos. The breadth of this intermediate regime, the decades of
$\log_{10}T$ in which the mean entropy on the orbit lies between 1 and 10 nats, ranges from 0.014 (Qwen2.5-72B) to
0.69 (OLMo-2-32B). It orders the fifteen models' chaotic length (Spearman $+0.55$) somewhat better than their parameter
count does ($-0.44$; Figure~\ref{fig:scale}, right), but it misses the chaos of Qwen2.5-1.5B and -14B at nearly
uniform distributions, above its 10-nat bound. This analysis was adapted after the OLMo-2-32B result, and the breadth
is measured on the loop's own orbits, which chaos itself spreads, so the test is partly circular. A loop-free version,
frozen before it was run (\texttt{docs/start-state-predictor.md}), uses one forward pass per model and prompt: the
entropy profile of the logits at the loop's start state, and the one-step gain there. Its primary prediction fails: the
start-state breadth on the paper's prompt orders the fourteen dense models' chaotic length with Spearman $+0.30$,
below the frozen threshold of $0.5$. The secondary criterion, its median over ten prompts, narrowly passes on the
frozen outcome ($+0.52$; $p=0.06$) and weakens on the expanded sweeps ($+0.44$), and the one-step gain at the start
state is negative in every model, the most chaotic ones included ($-0.5$ to $-3.4$ per step), and orders nothing
($-0.32$). These features ran on two GPUs before the precision fix, which changes them only at about the
$10^{-7}$ level. We have not established a reliable predictor from a single forward pass: as for the period-8 orbit of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, it has to be measured
on the loop.

\section{Unsteered chaos and steering controls}
\label{sec:origin}
Steering adds a fixed vector to the residual stream, so chaos along
a steering axis could belong to the push rather than to the model. At $\alpha=0$, however, the map is the unsteered
model's own feedback map, with temperature as its only parameter. The $\alpha=0$ columns of the hostility maps contain
cells with positive finite-horizon exponents in Llama-3.1 8B (26 of 64 temperatures), Qwen2.5-7B (15 of 64) and
OLMo-2 7B (11 of 64), while the near-zero columns of the 0.5B and 1.5B maps contain one such cell each (of 128 and
64). A longer float64 sweep of the unsteered 0.5B map (512 temperatures over $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$, 1000 transient
and 2000 measured steps) finds a narrow chaotic band at $\log_{10}T\approx0.15$--$0.21$ (7 temperatures, exponents up
to $0.28$), with a period-5 loop at 437 of the 512 temperatures. The same long sweep finds 96 chaotic temperatures in
Llama-3.1 8B, between $\log_{10}T=-0.20$ and $0.24$ (exponents up to $0.66$), interleaved with periodic windows of
periods 2 to 52; within $\log_{10}T\in[-0.3,0.3]$ its chaotic share, 41\,\%, equals that of the coarse column.
Qwen2.5-7B has 65 chaotic temperatures between $\log_{10}T=-0.07$ and $0.28$ (exponents up to $0.74$), and OLMo-2 7B
has 32 between $0.04$ and $0.14$ (up to $0.30$). Steering is therefore not needed to reach chaotic regimes in any of
these four models; where it does create chaos, it does so mainly through the readout, by making next tokens compete
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Temperature alone produces chaos with embedded windows, broadly in the 7B and 8B models and in
a narrow band in the 0.5B model (Figure~\ref{fig:alpha0}). Across the fifteen models of Section~\ref{sec:sizemain}, the
regular-grid sweeps find unsteered chaos in eleven of the fourteen dense models (in Llama-3.1-70B at two
temperatures that pass a three-offset test), but not in Qwen2.5-72B, Llama-3.3-70B, OLMo-2-13B or the mixture of
experts; finer lines found candidates in the first two, and the Qwen2.5-72B window, recomputed in float64
throughout, is periodic.

Steering does widen the chaotic regions, but not as a property of the concept: along norm-matched random directions,
the 0.5B maps are as chaotic as along the concept directions (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}). On the same $128^2$ grid,
7.6, 8.3 and 12.5\,\% of cells are chaotic for three Gaussian directions with the philosophy vector's norm, against
6.8\,\% for philosophy and 7.5\,\% for gratitude. In all five maps the chaotic share rises from about 1\,\% at
$|\alpha|<0.01$ to between 5 and 22\,\% at larger $|\alpha|$, and a chaotic region at high temperature, with windows
reaching down, recurs. In this model, chaos under steering is therefore a generic response to perturbing the residual
stream at this norm. Rotating the steering direction makes the same point continuously: along the great circle from
one Gaussian direction to another, $90^{\circ}$ away, at constant norm, the chaotic regions move and change shape, but
the chaotic share stays between 13 and 24\,\% at every one of 103 angles (Figure~\ref{fig:morph}; a video of the
rotation is in the supplementary material). Single neurons behave the same way. Steering along the output direction (Figure~\ref{fig:neuronsteer})
of one MLP neuron of the steered layer (its column of the down-projection, rescaled to the same norm; adding it is the same as raising that one neuron's activation by a fixed amount for every token of the input: by 37.4 at $|\alpha|=0.12$ for the neuron most specific to philosophy, whose mean activation on the philosophy texts is 0.27) gives 9.9\,\%
chaotic cells for the neuron most specific to philosophy on held-out texts, chosen like the autoencoder feature below
by a rule fixed before any map, 5.1\,\% for its index neighbour and 12.9\,\% for a random neuron
(Figure~\ref{fig:neurons}); near $\alpha=0$ the share is 0.4 to 1.2\,\%. The direction does select the texts. The largest attractor family in all five maps is the
unsteered period-5 loop, but beyond it only 26 to 56\,\% of one map's cells carry an attractor text that appears
anywhere in another map. Llama-3.1 8B agrees. On its hostility plane, two norm-matched random directions at the same
layer give 26 and 17\,\% chaotic cells against 28\,\% along the concept direction; all three carry 34 to 44\,\% near
$\alpha=0$, and the random directions thin out at large $|\alpha|$. At layer 19, where a sparse autoencoder trained on
this model reads the residual stream \cite{goodfire2025sae}, the concept vector rebuilt there gives 28\,\%. The
decoder direction of the autoencoder feature most specific to hostility on held-out texts (chosen by a rule fixed
before any map; cosine $0.10$ with the concept vector) gives 29\,\%, a random live feature 27\,\% and a Gaussian
direction 39\,\%, all at the same norm. In this model, chaos therefore occurs along autoencoder-feature,
contrastive and random directions alike, with direction-dependent prevalence; at 70B the directions differ more
(Appendix~\ref{sec:size}).

\section{Limitations in detail}
\label{sec:limits}
The detailed orbit studies cover one model family (Qwen2.5, 0.5B and 1.5B), with coarse
maps and three float64 orbits at 7B; the two other families (OLMo-2 7B and Llama-3.1 8B) have maps, including
$128^2$ refinements for Llama, but no continued orbits. The size comparison of Section~\ref{sec:sizemain} uses regular
temperature grids that can miss narrow windows, a window that missed most of the larger models' chaos, and mostly
one twin offset per label. The two-GPU runs of the 70B-class models computed their final normalization in float32
until we fixed it; their labels rest on offsets of $10^{-6}$ or more, and the cells we rechecked, the Qwen2.5-72B jump
window and steered candidates in float64 throughout and samples of the Llama maps at three offsets, behave as stated,
but the rest of those maps were not recomputed. Neither were the four coarse lines of the frozen 72B test; after the
fix we computed only fine lines through their entropy jumps, one of which passes through their single chaotic
temperature.
The maps use one prompt (Section~\ref{sec:sizemain} adds ten for Qwen2.5-7B and -14B, and four for Qwen2.5-72B, its base
model, and OLMo-2-7B and -32B), and steering uses contrastive vectors, checked against norm-matched random and sparse-autoencoder
directions in two models. The system is a constructed loop: Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge} extends it to memories of up to sixteen soft tokens and to the growing context of Soft Thinking, but sampled decoding of discrete tokens is a different system. The evidence consists of numerical
validation, cross-checks and prospectively frozen passage tests (including failed all-row criteria), with a
computer-assisted proof for only one orbit at one parameter point. Chaos labels are finite-horizon estimates (300
measured steps after 300 to 500 transient steps), and no chaotic cell is proved chaotic. A computer-assisted proof of chaos, such as a horseshoe verified by covering
relations, would need enclosures of the map over boxes many orders of magnitude larger than the period-8 proof's
(radius $2^{-640}$). With the ball arithmetic used there, a box of radius $2^{-80}$ already loses the positivity
of an RMS normalization at layer 3 (the enclosures overestimate through the layers), so such a proof needs other
verified methods. A horizon check retested
random cells of two float64 maps (Figures~\ref{fig:teaser} and~\ref{fig:stripes}) with 1000 transient and 2000
measured steps at two twin offsets: 86 and 92 of 96 cells labelled chaotic stayed chaotic (all but one of the rest
settled on periodic orbits), all 96 periodic cells in each map stayed periodic, and 18 and 20 of 32 cells with neither
label turned out to be slow periodic transients. Near the accumulation of a cascade the labels are less reliable, because orbits there converge
slowly or wander chaotically before they settle (transient chaos \cite{lai2011transient}): on
the hostile line of Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade} (left), 135 of the 235 cells labelled chaotic from 400 measured steps
stayed chaotic and 91 were periodic. The Feigenbaum ratios are
finite-order values that rise toward $\delta$ (the last is within 1\,\%); the limit itself is not computed. All
orbit, bifurcation and chaos results concern the constructed map, not sampled generation with a growing context. With
sampling, the bridge of Appendix~\ref{sec:bridge} tests only whether the bottleneck's laminar phases survive the noise;
its other tests are deterministic (greedy decoding, longer fed-back memories and Soft Thinking's growing context).

\section{Evidence and reproducibility}
\label{sec:evidence}
We grade claims on an evidence ladder. Rung 1 means numerically validated (tolerances, residuals, convergence,
precision varied); rung 2, cross-checked by an independent estimate, implementation or hardware; rung 3, a
computer-assisted proof.

\paragraph{Search scale.} The soft-token results come from an exploratory search. An initial census of the raw
data at 00:19 UTC on 14 October 2026 counted 34 distinct maps (987\,308 cells, 27 maps in float64) in six models,
each with one prompt and one of three steering directions or none, plus one partial map. Of these, 29 maps and the
partial one appear here; the five omitted are early discovery or side maps. Later additions are not in that count:
the steering-control maps of Appendix~\ref{sec:origin}, the mixture-of-experts maps, and the sweeps, lines and maps of the models of Section~\ref{sec:sizemain} and Appendix~\ref{sec:size}.
The labelled maps contain about 2600 periodic families of at least six cells, counted per map. We singled out about
