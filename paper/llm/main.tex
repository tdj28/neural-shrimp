\begin{abstract}
Feed a frozen language model its own expected next-token embedding, at a fixed position after a fixed prompt, and it
can lock into a loop of text such as ``. This is there is a is a'' or wander with positive finite-horizon Lyapunov
exponents. This smooth map, with temperature and steering strength as parameters, shows both regimes
in Qwen2.5 (0.5B to 7B), OLMo-2 7B and Llama-3.1 8B. Its derivative factorizes into the inverse temperature, an
embedding covariance under the model's own next-token distribution, and the network's Jacobian. With tied embeddings
and no network it would be a modern Hopfield update, which cannot cycle; the factorization confines chaos to
intermediate temperatures, as sweeps of four models show. Chaos needs no steering in these dense models, and
equal-norm random directions produce it as readily as concept directions. Applied to the model's own derivatives,
bifurcation theory predicts its behaviour in the cases we tested. Near numerically nondegenerate cusps of two periodic
windows, local unfoldings frozen before solving predicted the number of periodic orbits at all twelve tested offsets
and their stability at eleven. Fold normal forms predict bottleneck passage
times without fitting: at two new folds, 23 of 24 prospectively frozen forecasts fell within one iterate, failing the
all-row criterion by one row. In the recurrent-depth model Huginn, fixed-point spectra predict where chain-of-thought
accuracy breaks, confirmed on held-out data. Interval arithmetic proves that one period-8 orbit exists and is locally
attracting, to our knowledge the first such proof for a transformer language model. In sampled self-dialogue,
hostility steering has a non-monotone dose response; keeping flagged replies out of the context controls hostility at
a fraction of the steering dose, and counter-steering can backfire. The map is not ordinary autoregressive
generation.
\end{abstract}

Repeated inference with a fixed language model is an iterated map: the model's output becomes its next input. Like
other iterated maps, it can be chaotic. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows a small piece of the parameter plane of one
such map, built from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct. Cells with a positive finite-horizon Lyapunov exponent lie side by side
with windows in which the model repeats a fixed loop of text. Parts of this picture have been seen
before. Recent work has measured chaos-like sensitivity in language-model generation
\cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos}, temperature-driven changes of output statistics
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality,johnson2026temperature}, periodic
attractors of paraphrasing loops \cite{wang2025attractor}, attractor states of model-to-model conversations
\cite{ko2026attractor}, and, through their Jacobians, the stability of looped and recurrent-depth models
\cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars,luo2026prediction}. Feeding a
probability-weighted vocabulary embedding back into a model is not new either. It is used in training
\cite{zeng2025ponderlm} and, without retraining, in pretrained models \cite{zhang2025softthinking}, alongside
continuous latent feedback \cite{hao2024coconut}. In a small task-trained looped transformer, fold coefficients
computed from derivatives predict passage times and spectral gaps without fitting \cite{kim2026phase}.

We study one constructed but exactly defined system: the expected-embedding (``soft-token'') map of a frozen,
released pretrained language model, run with a fixed chat prefix at a fixed position. The map is smooth. Its
Jacobians come from automatic differentiation, and its periodic orbits can be computed and continued like those of
any smooth map. Theory makes chaos in such a family plausible. High-dimensional nonlinear maps with a gain-like
parameter generically become chaotic, as random recurrent networks do above a critical gain
\cite{sompolinsky1988chaos}, and periodic windows, shrimp-shaped domains and period-doubling cascades are universal in
dissipative two-parameter maps \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative,gallas1993structure}. These theories say that such
structure can occur. They do not say whether a trained language model realizes it, where in its parameter space,
with which texts, or whether the quantitative predictions of bifurcation theory hold for it, so that derivatives
computed from the model predict its behaviour before that behaviour is measured. The models we studied do realize
it, and in the cases we tested the predictions hold. We also show where the chaos comes from (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The loop's
derivative factorizes into the inverse temperature, the covariance of token embeddings under the model's own
next-token distribution, and the network's Jacobian. The network turns a Hopfield retrieval, which cannot cycle, into
a map that can, and the factorization confines chaos to intermediate temperatures. Fold normal forms predict how long trajectories linger in the ghost of a vanished orbit, cusp unfoldings predict how
many periodic orbits exist near a cusp and which of them are stable, and the fixed-point spectra of a recurrent-depth
model predict its response to relaxation and the onset of task failure on held-out data. Ordinary sampled generation
with a growing context is a different system. Section~\ref{sec:bridge} measures how much of the mechanism survives
sampling, and Section~\ref{sec:misalignment} studies sampled dialogue directly.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The loop's derivative shows where chaos can occur.} The derivative of the soft-token map factorizes into
  the inverse temperature, the covariance of token embeddings under the model's own next-token distribution, and the
  network's Jacobian. Without the network, the map would be a modern Hopfield update, which cannot cycle. The
  factorization rules out chaos at low and at high temperature, as sweeps of four models and a high-temperature sweep
  confirm, and nothing in it depends on what a steering direction means, consistent with random directions steering
  into chaos as readily as concept directions. Along a period-8 orbit every step expands while the cycle contracts:
  stability belongs to the loop, not to any step (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item \emph{A fold's normal form predicts how long the model lingers near a vanished orbit.} The 2-cycle ``is it''
  dies in a fold, and the fold's normal form, computed from derivatives, predicts bottleneck passage times without
  fitting. We tested it prospectively at two new folds: 23 of 24 forecasts fell within one iterate, and the all-row
  criterion failed by one row. The global reinjection sets the prefactor of the window law
  (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \emph{Cusp unfoldings predict how many periodic orbits coexist and which of them are stable.} Fold and flip
  curves continued with float64 test functions show that the fold curves of a period-6 window and of a hostile
  period-7 window end in numerically nondegenerate cusps. At one cusp of each, the two-parameter unfolding is regular,
  and its local model, frozen before solving, predicted the orbit counts at all twelve parameter offsets and their
  stability at eleven (Sections~\ref{sec:curves} and~\ref{sec:misalignment}).
\item \emph{In a second system, fixed-point spectra predict where chain-of-thought accuracy breaks.} In the
  recurrent-depth model Huginn we predict a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation and then observe it. Spectral estimates also
  predict each question's response to relaxation and where chain-of-thought accuracy breaks, and a frozen test on
  held-out data confirmed both predictions (Section~\ref{sec:huginn}).
\item \emph{One orbit is proved, and the central numerical results are checked independently.} Orbits, multipliers
  and test functions of the soft-token map are computed in float64 with precision checks (Section~\ref{sec:map}).
  Independent reimplementations reproduce the fold and its normal form and two orbits, a second hardware platform
  reproduces four flip parameters, and an independent replay reproduces the frozen Huginn test. An interval-arithmetic
  proof shows that a primitive period-8 orbit exists and is locally attracting, to our knowledge the first proof of a
  periodic orbit of a full transformer language-model map (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}).
\item \emph{In sampled dialogue, keeping a flagged reply out of the context controls hostility at a fraction of the
  steering dose, and counter-steering can backfire.} Hostility steering of sampled self-dialogue produces steep,
  direction-specific capture with dose-dependent persistence and hysteresis. Replacing flagged replies before they
  enter the dialogue keeps the model's own hostility low at a quarter of the dose of constant prevention, while
  counter-steering triggered after a hostile reply is less efficient. In Qwen2.5-1.5B the same ordering holds where
  the dose response rises, but the dose response is not monotone, and every counter-steer backfires beyond its peak.
  A pre-specified lead-time test is negative; a small warning from the model's in-context state is confirmed on fresh
  dialogues (Section~\ref{sec:misalignment}).
\item \emph{The structure that universality makes plausible is realized, with concrete texts, in several models.}
  Float64 phase maps in temperature and steering strength show chaos (positive finite-horizon exponents) with embedded
  windows (Section~\ref{sec:maps}), a period-adding family whose text gains one ``is X'' pair per period step
  (Section~\ref{sec:adding}), shrimp-like windows (Section~\ref{sec:curves}), and a doubling cascade with superstable
  points through period 384 and finite-order Feigenbaum ratios (Section~\ref{sec:cascade}). Past the cascade of a
  hostile window, chaos hides behind an exactly repeating text and reduces to a one-dimensional map with a single
  maximum (Section~\ref{sec:misalignment}). The same organization
  appears in Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B, OLMo-2 7B, Llama-3.1 8B (with sparse-autoencoder readouts) and a second checkpoint of
  the 0.5B architecture, which shares the organization but not the attractors (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Our one
  mixture-of-experts model, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, is the exception: its unsteered map contracts everywhere we tested.
  Chaos needs no steering in the dense models, and norm-matched random directions produce it as readily as concept directions
  (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). A bridge to sampled decoding measures what survives sampling
  (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.} Language-model generation has been studied as a dynamical system at several levels, and
the closest studies differ from ours in what they compute. Lyapunov-like quantities have been used to measure
sensitivity to small perturbations in reasoning traces \cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos}, but these studies
measure chaos-like sensitivity in generation without computing orbits or bifurcations. Output statistics change
abruptly with sampling temperature, and several studies analyse the changes as phase transitions or criticality
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality,johnson2026temperature}. These analyses
concern output statistics; they are not bifurcation analyses of a defined map. Repetition loops are a known failure of
decoding \cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}, successive paraphrasing converges to attractor cycles
\cite{wang2025attractor}, and multi-turn conversations between models settle into attractor states
\cite{ko2026attractor}. These attractors are behavioural: they are observed in generated text rather than computed as
invariant sets of a defined map. Feeding a model its own output during training degrades it over generations
\cite{shumailov2024collapse}; we study the inference-time loop of a model with fixed weights. The attractors of
recurrent networks have been related to the difficulty of training them \cite{ribeiro2020attractors}, and fixed points
with their linearizations have long been used to reverse-engineer trained recurrent networks
\cite{sussillo2013opening,maheswaranathan2019reverse}. Equilibrium, looped and recurrent-depth models iterate a block to a fixed point or for
many steps, and their stability has been analysed through Jacobians and fixed points
\cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,geiping2025recurrent,zhu2025ouro,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars,luo2026prediction,prairie2026parcae,buehlmaier2026layernorm,labovich2026stability,movahedi2026fixedpoint};
that analysis concerns fixed points. The closest precedent is a derivative-based fold analysis of a small task-trained
looped transformer, whose passage and spectral-gap predictions are unfitted \cite{kim2026phase}. That model was
trained for a task, whereas ours is a released pretrained language model, and our analysis also differs in its
objects: the fold of a primitive periodic orbit, the full-channel passage of a two-step map, and the accumulation law
of the windows, whose prefactor is set by the global reinjection. Expected-embedding
feedback itself is used in training \cite{zeng2025ponderlm} and, training-free, in pretrained models
\cite{zhang2025softthinking,wu2025singlethreaded}, next to continuous latent reasoning \cite{hao2024coconut}. Fixing
the prefix and the position turns that feedback into the autonomous map we analyse.

In neural systems more broadly, saddle-node ghosts produce quantitative delays in task-trained recurrent networks
\cite{dinc2026ghost}. Finite-order Feigenbaum ratios have been measured in neural rate and mass models
\cite{battaglia2007temporal,papasavvas2015gain} and, through period 128, in a transformer trained on logistic-map
trajectories \cite{liu2026learningchaos}. Learned networks reproduce period-doubling routes \cite{kim2021teaching},
and so does gradient descent itself \cite{kalra2025universal}. Normal forms and continuation have been applied to
recurrent neural maps \cite{marichal2015multiple}, and shrimp-shaped windows and periodicity staircases appear in
neuron models \cite{gomez2014universal,stenzinger2025shrimp,caixeta2025staircase}. Our second control parameter comes
from activation steering along contrastive directions \cite{zou2023representation,zhang2025steering}. The
dynamical-systems tools are standard: numerical continuation and normal forms
\cite{kuznetsov2023elements,govaerts2000numerical}, type-I intermittency \cite{pomeau1980intermittent}, shrimps and
hubs \cite{gallas1993structure,facanha2013endoskeletons,vitolo2011hubs}, period-doubling universality
\cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}, and Markov state models \cite{chodera2014msm}.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to compute periodic orbits with their multipliers, continue fold and flip
bifurcation curves and locate numerically nondegenerate cusps in the computation of a pretrained language model. It
is also, to our knowledge, the first to make quantitative predictions from such a model's derivatives and confirm
them prospectively: passage times at new folds, where 23 of 24 forecasts fell within one iterate and the all-row
criterion failed by one row, and orbit counts and stabilities at frozen offsets near cusps. We know of no earlier
confirmation on held-out data of a spectral prediction of where a language model's task accuracy breaks, which we
give for Huginn, and of no earlier computer-assisted proof of a periodic orbit of a full transformer language-model
map. These priority statements rest on a targeted search (12 October 2026) that found no prior work combining these
elements for the feedback map of a pretrained language model. That is an absence of evidence within the search, not
a proof of priority.

\section{The inference map}
\label{sec:map}
\paragraph{Definition.} We describe the map for Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the model studied in most detail; the other
models enter the same construction with their own embeddings and steering layers (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Let
$E\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}$ be the input embedding matrix ($V=151{,}936$, $d=896$). The model reads a fixed chat prefix, with the system prompt ``You are an AI talking with another AI. You
have complete freedom. Feel free to pursue whatever you want.'' and the user turn ``Hello!'', followed by a single
input embedding $x$, and produces next-token logits $z(x)\in\mathbb{R}^V$. The soft-token map feeds the expected
next-token embedding back in place of $x$:
\begin{equation}
  F_{T,\alpha}(x) = E^{\top}\operatorname{softmax}\!\big(z_\alpha(x)/T\big),
\end{equation}
where $z_\alpha$ adds $\alpha v$ to the residual stream at layer 12 at every position, prefix included. The steering
direction $v$ is contrastive: it compares last-token states of ``philosophy'' prompts with those of neutral prompts
and is normalized to the mean residual norm. Positions are fixed and the window does not grow, so $F$ is an
autonomous map of $\mathbb{R}^{896}$. As $T\to0$ it reduces, where the argmax is unique, to greedy decoding of the
same fixed-prefix, one-token-window system. It is not ordinary generation with a growing context.

\paragraph{Precision.} Hugging Face RMSNorm layers and rotary-embedding tables compute in float32 even when the
model is cast to float64, so we patch both. A float32 map is accurate to $3\times10^{-6}$--$2\times10^{-5}$ per
step. That is enough for phase maps (245 of 256 test cells agree with float64) but not for derivatives of composed
maps: one-step float32 Jacobian-vector products are accurate to $5\times10^{-5}$, yet the 8-step monodromy of a
period-8 orbit is off by 3\%. All orbits, multipliers and test functions below are therefore computed in float64.

\section{Why the loop can be chaotic}
\label{sec:theory}
The soft-token map has a simple derivative. With $p=\operatorname{softmax}(z/T)$,
\begin{equation}
DF(x)=\frac{1}{T}\,E^{\top}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top}\bigr)J_z(x).
\end{equation}
Qwen2.5-0.5B ties its output matrix to the input embedding, so $z=E\tilde h(x)$, where $\tilde h$ is the final
normalized hidden state, and the derivative factorizes:
\begin{equation}
DF(x)=\frac{1}{T}\,\Sigma_p\,J_{\tilde h}(x),\qquad
\Sigma_p=E^{\top}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top}\bigr)E=\operatorname{Cov}_{t\sim p}(e_t).
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
The derivative of the loop is the covariance of the token embeddings under the model's own next-token distribution,
amplified by the inverse temperature and composed with the derivative of the network. At a state of the period-8
orbit of Table~\ref{tab:orbits}, \eqref{eq:gain} agrees with forward-mode derivatives of the full map to a relative
error of at most $1.3\times10^{-13}$ over eight random directions (\texttt{tools/softmax\_gain.py}). Without tied embeddings, as in the 7B and 8B models, $\Sigma_p$ becomes the cross-covariance of input
and output embeddings under $p$; the temperature limits below do not change.

\paragraph{A Hopfield network with a transformer inside.} If the network passed its input straight through,
$\tilde h(x)=x$, the map would be $F(x)=E^{\top}\operatorname{softmax}(Ex/T)$. This is exactly the update rule of a
modern Hopfield network whose stored patterns are the token embeddings, at inverse temperature $\beta=1/T$
\cite{ramsauer2020hopfield}. That update lowers an energy function at every step and converges to fixed points.
In \eqref{eq:gain} it is the case $J_{\tilde h}=I$, where $DF=\Sigma_p/T$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite.
More generally, if $DF$ were symmetric and positive semidefinite everywhere, $F$ would be the gradient of a convex
function, and the same energy argument would exclude every periodic orbit other than fixed points, and chaos with
them. Every cycle and every chaotic cell in this paper therefore depends on the network factor $J_{\tilde h}$ breaking
that structure. Attention has itself been read as a Hopfield retrieval \cite{ramsauer2020hopfield}; in the soft-token
map the retrieval closes a loop around the whole network.

\paragraph{Chaos needs intermediate temperatures.} The largest Lyapunov exponent of an orbit cannot exceed the orbit
average of $\log\|DF\|$, so chaos needs $\|DF\|>1$ on a substantial part of the attractor. Both ends of the
temperature axis prevent this. As $T\to0$, $p$ becomes one-hot except near ties between the two leading tokens; the
gain $\|\Sigma_p\|/T$ decays like $e^{-\Delta/T}/T$, where $\Delta$ is the gap between the two largest logits, and $F$
tends to the piecewise-constant greedy map, every orbit of which is eventually periodic (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).
As $T\to\infty$, $p$ tends to the uniform distribution and $\|\Sigma_p\|$ stays bounded, while $J_{\tilde h}$ is
bounded on the convex hull of the embeddings, which contains every soft token. For large enough $T$, $F$ is therefore
a contraction with a single attracting fixed point, which tends to the mean embedding. Chaos thus needs temperatures
at which the next-token distribution is spread enough to make $\Sigma_p$ large while $1/T$ still amplifies it. The
unsteered sweeps agree (Figure~\ref{fig:alpha0}). In all four models the chaotic temperatures lie in one band, interleaved with
periodic windows, and every band lies between $\log_{10}T=-0.20$ and $0.28$. Below the bands, all 1547 temperatures swept have negative
exponents, and all but five have a detected period; above them, up to the end of the sweeps at $\log_{10}T=0.3$, the
orbits are periodic again, fixed points among them. A further sweep of the unsteered 0.5B map from
$\log_{10}T=0.3$ to $1.5$ finds an attracting fixed point at all 25 temperatures. Its entropy approaches $\log V$
(11.928 against 11.931 nats at $\log_{10}T=1.5$), and its exponent falls steadily, by 2.14 per decade of temperature
at the top of the range, toward the slope $-\ln10\approx-2.30$ expected once $p$ is uniform.

\paragraph{Stability belongs to the loop, not to the step.} The gain condition is necessary, not sufficient. Along
the period-8 orbit of Table~\ref{tab:orbits}, the largest singular value of $DF$ lies between 1.2 and 21 at every
step, so every step expands some direction, and no step is close to rank one: the second singular value is at least
0.15 of the first, and the second eigenvalue of $\Sigma_p$ at least 0.24 of the first. Yet the Jacobian of the whole
cycle, the product of the eight step Jacobians, has norm 0.19, against $1.1\times10^{7}$ for the product of the step
norms, and its eigenvalues reproduce the multipliers of Table~\ref{tab:orbits} ($0.01104$ and $2.96\times10^{-7}$).
Its second singular value is $4\times10^{-5}$ of its first. The expansion of each step is undone by the steps that
follow, and the effective one-dimensionality of the stripe orbits is a property of the composed cycle, not of any
single step or of the rank of $\Sigma_p$. Stability cannot be read from the steps; it has to be computed from the
loop.

\paragraph{Steering acts on the gain, not on meaning.} Steering enters both factors of \eqref{eq:gain}: it moves the
logits, and with them $\Sigma_p$, and it moves the hidden states, and with them $J_{\tilde h}$. Nothing in this
mechanism depends on what a direction means. This is consistent with the controls of Section~\ref{sec:discussion},
in which norm-matched random directions produce chaos as readily as concept directions. What a direction does select
is which tokens dominate $p$, and therefore what the attractors write.

\paragraph{Contraction and expansion in the network.} Analyses of deep attention identify contracting tendencies:
pure self-attention collapses token representations toward a common vector with depth \cite{dong2021attention}, and
idealized attention dynamics form clusters \cite{geshkovski2023emergence}. Residual connections and MLPs counteract
the collapse \cite{dong2021attention}. Which tendency wins along an orbit is a property of the trained weights that
only computation settles. In every dense model we mapped, both outcomes occur: the same map has negative largest
exponents in some regions of its parameter plane and positive ones in others.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{Phase maps.} Each cell of a phase map is one parameter pair. Unless stated otherwise, we discard 300
transient steps and measure over the next 300. The largest Lyapunov exponent comes from a renormalized twin
trajectory at offset $10^{-6}$. The period is the smallest $q\le32$ (48 in the later zooms) with return distance below
$10^{-8}$, and the attractor is identified by its leading-token loop, up to rotation. We call a cell chaotic when this
finite-horizon exponent exceeds $10^{-3}$ and no period is found; the label is a finite-horizon diagnostic, not a
proof of chaos. Panels coloured by period or attractor text follow an older drawing convention: black marks every
cell whose exponent exceeds $0.005$, and light grey the other cells without a period. The drawn black area differs
from the chaotic count in at most $0.42\,\%$ of a map's cells. A $256^2$ map takes about 100 minutes on one H100 GPU in float64. Every cell starts from the same
state, so where attractors coexist, a map shows the one whose basin contains that state. Labels are text, not
dynamical identities: one continuous orbit changes its label where two tokens tie for the maximum. We therefore call
two labelled regions different attractors only when the contraction rate jumps between them or when float64 orbits of
both exist at one parameter value.

\paragraph{Periodic orbits.} We solve $F^p(x)=x$ by Newton's method with forward-mode Jacobians. The multipliers are
the eigenvalues of the monodromy matrix; where the dense matrix is not float64, we refine them by a short Arnoldi
factorization with float64 Jacobian-vector products.

\paragraph{Continuation.} Periodic branches are continued in one parameter by pseudo-arclength continuation, and fold
and flip curves in two parameters with minimally augmented systems \cite{govaerts2000numerical,kuznetsov2023elements}.
The test function $g$ of the bordered system $[[M\mp I,v],[w^\top,0]]$ is obtained by GMRES (generalized minimal
residual iterations) with Jacobian-vector products, and its gradient by reverse-mode differentiation through a
Jacobian-vector product. When the parameter tangent reverses, we flag a cusp candidate and test it with the normal
form.

\paragraph{Normal form and passages.} Consider a fold of the two-step map $G=F^2$, with right and left eigenvectors
$r$ and $\ell$ normalized by $\|r\|=1$ and $\ell^\top r=1$. Along the critical direction the reduced map is
$u'=u+a\eps+bu^2$, with $a=\ell^\top\partial_\alpha G$ and $b=\tfrac12\ell^\top D^2G[r,r]$. We compute $b$ by
central differences of float64 Jacobian-vector products over a step-size plateau (later cross-checks use nested
automatic differentiation), and $a$ includes the steering's effect on the prefix. The signed coefficients are kept in
the original eigenvector gauge, with $\eps$ measured in the free parameter ($\alpha$ or $\log_{10}T$, whose units
apply to $a$). For passages we set $v=\operatorname{sign}(b)\,u$ and take the ghost side
$p-p_c=\operatorname{sign}(ab)\,\epsilon$ with $\epsilon>0$, so that $v'=v+|a|\epsilon+|b|v^2$ to quadratic order. A
full passage starts at $v=-U$ and a midpoint passage at $v=0$; both end at the first strict crossing $v>U$, with
$U=0.1/|b|$. We compare passage counts with the iterated quadratic map and with the arctangent formula
\cite{pomeau1980intermittent}.

\paragraph{Period-doubling cascade.} Along a cut in one parameter, we locate the flip of each period-$p$ orbit as the
root of $\operatorname{Re}\mu_c+1$, where $\mu_c$ is the multiplier with the smallest real part, using the Illinois
variant of regula falsi with warm-started orbits. All but one or two multipliers are small, so Newton's method on
$F^p(x)-x$ needs the monodromy only on a six-dimensional dominant subspace. We obtain that subspace by subspace
iteration with exact forward-mode Jacobian-vector products chained along the orbit, so no dense Jacobian is formed,
and the residual is always the exact float64 $F^p(x)-x$. Each root is classified before it is accepted. The critical
multiplier must be real and equal to $-1$, with a Ritz residual below $10^{-6}$, and the other computed Ritz values
(six in all, estimates of the dominant spectrum) must lie inside the unit circle. We then reach the period-$2p$ orbit
by forward iteration inside the next band. On the logistic map embedded in six dimensions, the procedure reproduces
the first four flip parameters to $10^{-9}$.

\section{Chaos with periodic windows}

\section{Mechanism: a saddle-node bottleneck}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\paragraph{The fold.} At $\log_{10}T=-0.115$ the 2-cycle ``is it'' dies in a fold at $\alpha_c=0.06279827$ (float64
multiplier $1$ to $3\times10^{-13}$, next modulus $0.037$). The normal form of $G$ has $a=-4.834$ per unit $\alpha$
and $b=-25.4285$, so $ab=122.93$. The stable branch gives an independent estimate: extrapolating its multipliers
through $(1-\mu)^2/(4(\alpha_c-\alpha))\to ab$ yields $123.1$ with a quadratic fit ($120.6$ with a linear one). An
independent implementation of the forward map and solver recovers $\alpha_c$ to $6\times10^{-15}$ and $a$ to
$3\times10^{-12}$. Its $b$, from nested automatic differentiation, is $-25.42846$; our finite-difference value
differs from it by $2.2\times10^{-6}$ relatively, which is the finite-step bias of the difference table. The
resulting $ab=122.927$ leaves every passage prediction of Table~\ref{tab:passages} unchanged.

\paragraph{Natural reinjection and the window law.} Free trajectories re-enter the channel slightly upstream of
the bottleneck's middle ($u\approx-3\times10^{-4}$ to $-7\times10^{-4}$). From each measured entry point, the
normal form predicts the natural laminar durations within 4--8\% for $\eps\le10^{-4}$, and a natural passage lasts
about 0.6 of a full one. The stripes therefore follow the $N^{-2}$ law of a saddle-node ghost, with about a third of
the full-passage prefactor. Stripe centres located on a float32 cut along $\log_{10}T=-0.115$ give
$d_N N^2=0.0263$--$0.0265$ for $N=4$--$7$ (log-log slope $-2.00$), compared with $\pi^2/(4ab)=0.0201$ for a half
passage. Two-step return maps of $p(\text{``it''})$ (Figure~\ref{fig:returnmaps}) show the near-tangency.

\section{Bifurcation curves and cusps}
\label{sec:curves}
\paragraph{Period-8 stripe.} Continued for 80 points with a float64 test function, the fold curve of the period-8
orbit keeps its critical multiplier within $1\pm6\times10^{-6}$, follows the stripe's upper edge and caps its top near
$\log_{10}T=-0.065$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stripes}). At $\alpha=0.0717$ the stripe lies between a flip at
$\log_{10}T=-0.128955$ and the fold at $-0.1033462$. Continued downward, the flip curve keeps its multiplier at $-1$
for 115 points to $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0776,-0.402)$, with two reversals of its parameter tangent near
$(0.0750,-0.219)$ and $(0.0746,-0.206)$, and the fold curve keeps its multiplier at $+1$ for 120 points to
$(0.0758,-0.200)$. \planned{classify the two reversals on the flip curve (on a flip curve they are not fold cusps).}

\paragraph{The accumulation boundary.} The fold curve of the 2-cycle ``is it'', continued from
$(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0628,-0.115)$ in both directions (38 and 126 points, critical multiplier $1$ to $10^{-7}$, no
cusp), traces the boundary of the period-2 region in Figures~\ref{fig:stripes} and~\ref{fig:cross512}. The stripe fan
accumulates on this boundary, as the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} requires. Further along one direction,
outside the plotted window, another multiplier leaves the unit circle (moduli up to 11). This is a codimension-two
point, \planned{to be located and typed}.

\paragraph{A period-6 window with shrimp-like anatomy.} The hooks of Figure~\ref{fig:zooms} (bottom) belong to
the period-6 loop ``. -\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe is a''. Float64 orbits (Newton residuals
$10^{-15}$--$10^{-13}$) show that its curved body and its thin leg are one window: the multipliers are $-0.061$ and
$+0.048$ at the body, $-0.564$ lower down and $-0.645$ on the leg. The foot is its period-doubled loop (period 12,
leading multiplier $-0.156$). Inside the body lies a different 6-cycle, ``.The\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe is
a'', whose leading multipliers are a complex pair, $-0.031\pm0.152i$. One-parameter branches from the body end in
folds in every direction: at $\log_{10}T=-0.0558$ and $-0.0721$ in temperature, at $\alpha=0.08074$ in steering, and,
toward smaller $\alpha$, in two folds only $10^{-5}$ apart. Such a close pair is the signature of a nearby cusp. A
flip at $\alpha\approx0.0797$ then opens the period-12 foot.

Fold curves that bound a window and meet in cusps, with flip curves inside, form the codimension-two organization of
a shrimp \cite{facanha2013endoskeletons} (complex dynamics of small recurrent networks has been studied in the same
spirit \cite{pasemann2002complex}). In the continued curves (Figure~\ref{fig:z3curves}), a single fold arc bounds the
body on its outer side and a flip arc on its inner side, and the leg lies between them where they converge. The outer
fold arc extends far to the left as a thin arm and ends in a cusp at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.06911,-0.03975)$. The
fold curve through the close fold pair has a second cusp inside the body, at $(0.08010,-0.05835)$, from which two fold
branches descend.

At both cusps the quadratic coefficient $b$ changes sign while $a$ stays away from zero (Table~\ref{tab:z3cusps}).
Because the signs of $a$ and $b$ both flip with the orientation of the critical eigenvector, the test uses
$b\operatorname{sign}a$. A cusp is nondegenerate when the cubic coefficient $c$ of the normal form
$u'=u+a\varepsilon+bu^2+cu^3$ is nonzero where $b$ vanishes. We compute $c$ with the second-order centre-manifold
term, $c=\ell^\top C(r,r,r)/6+\ell^\top B(r,h_2)/2$ with $(I-M)h_2=B(r,r)-2br$ and $\ell^\top h_2=0$, where $M$ is
the monodromy matrix and $B$ and $C$ are the second and third derivatives of its map, obtained by central differences
of float64 Jacobian-vector products. (The code reproduces $c$ to $10^{-6}$ on a map with a known reduction.) At both
cusps $c$ is nonzero far beyond its step-size uncertainty and keeps its sign through the zero of $b$, which crosses
zero with nonzero slope along the curve. Both are therefore nondegenerate cusps, numerically. The negative sign of
$c$ means that between the fold branches near each cusp, two stable period-6 orbits coexist with a saddle.

A generic codimension-two cusp also needs the two parameters to unfold it independently. Near the cusp the reduced
map is $u'=u+\beta_1+\beta_2u+bu^2+cu^3$, where $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ vanish at the cusp and depend on the
parameters. The unfolding is regular when $J=\partial(\beta_1,\beta_2)/\partial(\log_{10}T,10\alpha)$ has rank two
($\det J\neq0$). At the outer-arc cusp, $\det J=2737$ (singular values $1630$ and $1.7$). With these derivatives, the
local cubic model predicted the number of period-6 orbits and their stability at six parameter offsets, written to
disk before any orbit was solved, and Newton solutions matched at all six.

\section{A period-doubling cascade to chaos}
\label{sec:cascade}
The crescent of Figure~\ref{fig:cross512} is a closed region in which the period-3 loop ``\textbackslash
n\textbackslash nThe existence'' doubles inward. Along the cut $\log_{10}T=-0.14798$, the map's cells show period 3
over 25 columns, period 6 over about 14, period 12 over 4 and period 24 in one column, followed by a chaotic core. The
doubled loops repeat the same leading tokens twice and four times, with alternating probabilities. Locating the flips
along this cut in float64 (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), in the steering coordinate $\alpha$, gives
Table~\ref{tab:cascade}. The first two ratios, $\delta_2=2.844$ and $\delta_3=2.859$ (indexed as in
Table~\ref{tab:cascade}), are far from Feigenbaum's $4.6692$, even though from the second flip on the orbits are
effectively one-dimensional (next modulus $0.0023$, $2\times10^{-6}$, $2\times10^{-12}$). The next ratio,
$\delta_4=3.781$, is markedly larger. The first four flip parameters agree between completed float64 computations on a
CPU and an H100 to $4\times10^{-15}$.

Continuing a just-born doubled orbit past period 48 failed: Newton's method slides onto the half-period orbit, which
is also periodic with the doubled period. We therefore also located the superstable parameters $s_p$, where the
critical multiplier vanishes in the middle of each band (Table~\ref{tab:superstable}). There the orbit attracts, so it
can be reached by forward iteration and cannot slide onto its half-period parent. Through period 384 the superstable
ratios are $3.19$, $4.02$, $4.478$ and $4.625$. The last two come from parameters resolved to $10^{-14}$ and differ
from Feigenbaum's value by $0.19$ and $0.044$; from $4.02$ on, the distance shrinks by factors of $3.4$ and $4.3$.
The flip ratios $2.84$, $2.86$ and $3.78$ rise in step (Figure~\ref{fig:feigenbaum}), and the accumulation point lies
near $\alpha_\infty\approx0.0661250$. The early superstable values are less precise. The other computed Ritz values of
the period-12 and period-24 orbits blur the root, so $s_{12}$ and $s_{24}$ are known only within brackets of
$1.2\times10^{-5}$ and $7\times10^{-7}$, and the ratios $3.19$ and $4.02$ have worst-case ranges $[2.90,3.34]$ and
$[4.02,4.10]$. $s_6$ is less precise still, and $s_3$ was not bracketed.

Not every doubling leads to chaos. In the high-temperature region (Figure~\ref{fig:p4}), along

\section{Bridge to sampled decoding}
\label{sec:bridge}
How much of this survives sampling? To find out, we replace the expected embedding by the mean of $m$ sampled token
embeddings (Figure~\ref{fig:bridge}). With the exact map, laminar phases near the fold last 120 and 63 steps at
$\eps=10^{-5}$ and $3\times10^{-5}$. With $m=64$ they last about 10.6 steps, independently of $\eps$ (57\% of steps in
the channel), and with $m=8$ about 7.7 steps (38\%). With single samples ($m=1$, sampled decoding of the same
truncated system), essentially no laminar phases remain (11\% of steps in the channel). The deterministic
intermittency mechanism therefore belongs to the expected-embedding map. Sampling noise turns the bottleneck into a
noise-limited trap whose strength grows with $m$.

\paragraph{Zero-temperature limit and ordinary greedy decoding.} As $T\to0$ the soft-token map becomes greedy
decoding with a one-token memory, $g(t)=\arg\max_s z_s(\text{prefix},E_t)$. This is a map on the vocabulary, and its
cycles are the zero-temperature attractors. For Qwen2.5-0.5B we computed $g$ for all 151\,936 tokens. Unsteered, it
has 1060 cycles, and one of them, the period-5 loop ``![](https:// Hello'', drains 86\,\% of the vocabulary; it is
the largest attractor family of every 0.5B soft-token map. Ordinary greedy decoding with a growing context does not
inherit these cycles (Table~\ref{tab:memory}). From 220 starts (the first tokens of the 20 largest cycles and 200
random tokens), 19 runs end in a short loop within 256 tokens, and the dominant 5-cycle never appears. Steering
along philosophy makes ordinary decoding loop almost always (218 of 220 runs at $\alpha=0.1$), but in other loops,
mostly punctuation such as `` --'', none of them one-token cycles. Greedy decoding that keeps only the last $K$
tokens interpolates between the two. For every $K\ge2$ the loops leave the one-token cycles. Unsteered, looping
collapses to the level of the growing context between $K=16$ and $32$; steered, the share of runs whose loop equals
the growing-context loop rises from 0 at $K=1$ to 58\,\% at $K=64$. The soft-token map's low-temperature attractors
are therefore those of decoding with a one-token memory. Which loop ordinary decoding enters depends on the context
it keeps, but the tendency of steering to drive decoding into loops is shared. In ordinary decoding, repetition is
known to reinforce itself \cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop,fu2021repetition}, and induction heads, which copy
earlier context \cite{olsson2022induction}, are one mechanism by which a longer context can favour loops that repeat
earlier text.

\paragraph{A mixture-of-experts model.} Qwen3.5-35B-A3B differs from the dense models in two ways. Thirty of its 40

a token to 8 of 256 experts plus a shared expert, so that about 3B of its 35B parameters are active. Hugging Face
computes several parts of this architecture in float32 even when the model is cast to float64 (the normalizations,
the delta-rule recurrences, the rotary tables and the router softmax), and some tensors are stored in float32; we
patch these paths, restore the stored tensors exactly, and disable the thinking block that the default chat template
opens (a first run with the block open is kept in the research log). Steered at layer 20, the map behaves unlike
those of the dense models. Unsteered, 128 temperatures over $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$ give no positive exponent (largest

crosses a routing boundary jumps, which can inflate a finite-difference exponent, so we call these cells irregular
rather than chaotic. Whether the contraction comes from the experts or from the Qwen3.5 architecture would take the
dense Qwen3.5-9B as a control, which we have not run.

\section{A second system: recurrent depth}
\label{sec:huginn}
Huginn-0125 iterates a recurrent block at fixed input tokens \cite{geiping2025recurrent}. For a GSM8K-style
\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} prompt, the native loop converges to a fixed point whose leading Jacobian pair is
$0.588\pm0.771i$ (modulus $0.970$). For the relaxed loop $h\leftarrow(1-\lambda)h+\lambda F(h)$, this predicts a
Neimark--Sacker bifurcation at $\lambda_c=2(1-\operatorname{Re}\mu)/|1-\mu|^2=1.0777$. Below it the latent state
decays; above it the state settles on a closed curve. A fit of the squared amplitude gives $\lambda_c=1.0778$, with
$A/\sqrt{\lambda-\lambda_c}=0.52$--$0.54$ (supercritical). Causal attention makes the loop block lower-triangular in
token positions. The first position to oscillate is the ``.'' of the prompt, and all later positions follow. On the
invariant circle the motion turns by $0.156$ of a revolution per step, matching the argument of the critical pair.
The rotation number changes smoothly, by about $10^{-4}$ over $\lambda\in[1.079,1.092]$, and no return within 60
steps was found at $10^{-3}$ of the radius. This is consistent with quasi-periodic motion, although locking in narrow
high-order tongues cannot be excluded. From $\lambda\approx1.094$ the circle deforms or a second frequency appears,
near where a second complex pair destabilizes the fixed point ($\lambda=1.0814$). Fixed-point and Jacobian stability
of looped models has been studied before \cite{yang2026stars}; what this adds is the predicted crossing, its amplitude
law and the rotation number.

\paragraph{The spectrum predicts the relaxation response.} With the full instruction template, the GSM8K prompts'
fixed points look different: the leading multipliers surround the origin, a real $0.914$, complex pairs of modulus
$0.85$--$0.89$ at many angles and negative real values near $-0.8$, with no near-critical pair. Because the prefill
Jacobian is block lower-triangular in positions, its spectrum is the union of per-position blocks, and template
positions contribute identical eigenvalues to every prompt. Such a spectrum makes three predictions before any task is
scored. First, within the computed spectral estimates of these prompts, no fixed relaxation can speed convergence
much. With leading multipliers spread around the origin at many angles, any $\lambda$ that shrinks one group enlarges
another, and the best $\lambda^*=1.03$--$1.06$ lowers the rate from $0.914$ to only $0.908$--$0.911$. (A finite
computed spectrum does not show that no other polynomial scheme could do better; Anderson acceleration, tried below,
did not.) Second, under-relaxation slows convergence ($\lambda=0.55$: predicted $0.953$, measured $0.951$ on one
prompt). Third, over-relaxation destabilizes the negative modes first, at
$\lambda_c=2\operatorname{Re}(1-\mu)/|1-\mu|^2=1.074$--$1.126$ (five prompts), in a flip or a Neimark--Sacker
bifurcation close to $-1$. The approach to it is visible: on the prompt with $\lambda_c=1.074$, the measured rate at
$\lambda=1.05$ is $0.955$ against a predicted $0.956$ (native $0.864$). Elsewhere the spectral radius bounds the rate
measured over the accessible window from above, by up to $0.05$, because the slowest modes carry little amplitude
there. Independently, \citet{wang2026taps} report a Huginn GSM8K drop from $19.41\,\%$ to
$8.34\,\%$ at a constant step of $1.2$; at $0.8$ GSM8K is $19.79\,\%$, while their four-task mean falls slightly.
Their trajectory-based scheduling analysis does not compute the prompt-specific spectral thresholds studied here.

\paragraph{Does the task notice? (development data).} On ARC-Challenge \cite{clark2018arc} questions scored by
log-likelihood, where one prefill per answer choice is exactly the analysed recurrence, the spectra again predict
convergence. Rates agree to $0.002$--$0.006$ where the slowest mode dominates, and beyond each question's $\lambda_c$
($1.075$--$1.149$ for four questions) the residual stops decaying. For 6 of 30 development questions the native fixed
point is itself unstable ($\rho(1)=1.009$--$1.273$; the fixed point, located with a relaxed transient, has a real
multiplier beyond $-1$ or a complex pair outside the circle). The native loop then oscillates (relative amplitude
$2\times10^{-2}$ for one of them), and relaxation to $0.55$ or $0.75$ restores convergence to $10^{-6}$. For the
other 24 the native loop is stable up to $\lambda=1.020$--$1.149$ (median $1.095$). Question by question, an
estimated spectral feature ranks the question's residual. This feature, $\hat\rho_q(\lambda)$, is the largest
$|1-\lambda+\lambda\mu|$ over the computed Ritz values of the question's gold-answer prefill, and its Spearman
correlation with the residual is $0.73$ at $\lambda=1$ and $0.75$ at $1.10$ over the 30 questions (development
data). In this historical analysis the residual was the all-choice aggregate entering the 32nd recurrence call, so
this is a predictive association across related maps and precisions, not a same-state contraction identity. It is
consistent with the latent orbits that \citet{geiping2025recurrent} observed on some tokens of harder questions, here
traced to the fixed point's spectrum. The item-level part of the frozen test below uses matched gold-row token IDs and
a per-row residual with an explicit clock.

Accuracy, however, tolerates the instability (300 training questions, 32 steps): it is $0.367$ native, $0.387$ at
$\lambda=1.10$ and $0.373$ at $1.15$, where the latent state oscillates with relative size $0.08$ and $0.23$. Only at
$1.25$ (size $0.68$) does it fall, to $0.300$. Yet the oscillation does reach the answers. Reading every step from 24
to 32 on 100 questions, the margin between the correct choice and its best rival moves by $0.11$ nats per step
natively, $0.37$ at $1.10$ and $0.95$ at $1.15$, in alternating directions, and the chosen answer changes within these
nine steps for 12\,\%, 16\,\% and 33\,\% of the questions. The changes go both ways, so the accuracy at any one step
stays the same on average. Under-relaxation instead costs 4--5 points at 8--16 steps and catches up by 32 (a slower
transient), and Anderson acceleration neither speeds the transient nor gains accuracy. Testing the accuracy
consequence therefore needs a task in which such step-to-step changes can compound.

GSM8K generation is such a task. With eight chain-of-thought examples in the lm-evaluation-harness format, greedy
decoding and 32 recurrence steps per token (development problems 0--119, every arm on the same problems and initial
states), accuracy is $0.375$ natively, $0.425$ at $\lambda=1.05$ and at $1.10$, $0.29$ at $1.15$ (17 problems lost and
7 gained, one-sided McNemar $p=0.03$) and $0.16$ at $1.20$ (29 lost, 3 gained, $p=10^{-6}$). Under-relaxation to
$0.8$ costs nothing at 32 steps ($0.41$). The break lies between $1.10$ and $1.15$, at or just above the spectral
thresholds ($\lambda_c=1.074$--$1.126$ for the GSM8K prompts; median upper stability limit $1.095$ for the ARC
development questions). Generation breaks earlier than log-likelihood scoring, consistent with answer changes that
compound over tokens. The collapse at $1.2$ is the one \citet{wang2026taps} reported.

A frozen test on fresh problems confirms this. Its design and analysis were pushed before any test item was read,
after two rounds of independent review. On 200 GSM8K test problems, accuracy is $0.330$ natively, $0.395$ at
$\lambda=1.05$, $0.255$ at $1.15$ and $0.095$ at $1.20$. The two pre-stated predictions pass: accuracy declines at $1.20$ ($-23.5$
points; 50 problems lost and 3 gained, exact one-sided McNemar $p=3\times10^{-12}$), and $1.05$ loses no more than
four points (difference $+6.5$, one-sided 95\,\% lower bound $+2.5$). At $1.15$ accuracy falls by $7.5$ points
($p=0.01$, reported descriptively). Development and test agree closely (Figure~\ref{fig:huginn-gsm8k}).

\section{Hostile behaviour under steering}
\label{sec:misalignment}
The maps show how a smooth change of a control parameter can create, destroy or exchange attractors, and how
behaviour near the boundaries becomes intermittent and history-dependent. The same questions can be asked of
undesirable behaviour in ordinary sampled dialogue, where attractor-like patterns and persona drift have been observed
\cite{ko2026attractor,anthropic2025claude4,lu2026assistant} and input context can push behaviour progressively
\cite{anil2024manyshot}. Do states such as hostility toward the interlocutor behave like basins of attraction, with
capture thresholds, hysteresis and early-warning signals? (Misalignment produced by fine-tuning
\cite{betley2025emergent} is a different, weight-level phenomenon.) The measurements below concern a
classifier-defined proxy in sampled dialogue. They can show steering-dependent persistence, history dependence and the
effect of controls. They cannot show semantic misalignment, nor a deterministic attractor, since a growing context
itself carries memory.

Appendix~\ref{app:gratitude} shows the coarse-grained instrument on a benign direction. Self-dialogue of the same
model, sampled at $T=0.7$ and steered along a gratitude direction, falls into a metastable gratitude set. The capture
is gradual at $\alpha=0.01$ (median entry turn 7), fast at $0.02$ (median 5) and imposed from the first reply at
$0.04$, where the set is effectively absorbing within 40 turns.

For hostility we steer along a valence direction, the contrast of hostile statements against matched kind ones, built
in the manner of contrastive activation addition \cite{rimsky2024steering}; its cosine with the gratitude direction is
$-0.04$. Hostility is scored by a pinned toxicity classifier, toxic-bert of Detoxify \cite{hanu2020detoxify}, and a
reply counts as hostile when its toxic output exceeds $0.5$. On the prompt sets the classifier's toxic output is
$0.95$ for the hostile statements and at most $0.002$ for the kind and neutral ones. In a dry run (8 dialogues of 10
turns per dose), no turn was hostile at $\alpha=0.02$. At $0.04$, 7.5\,\% of turns were, in bursts and late captures,
and at $0.08$, 87.5\,\% were, from the first turns.

Activation-based methods already anticipate response behaviour \cite{ashok2025behavior,chen2025persona,lu2026assistant},
committors have been used in language-model decoding \cite{zhou2026commitment}, and adaptive timing of
counter-steering gave no consistent advantage in one persona setting \cite{leins2026drift}. Our primary question is
therefore incremental. Near the threshold, does a dynamical estimate add several turns of warning of capture over
strong baselines that see the same turns (the classifier's score, its change and history, the turn index and a fixed
persona-axis projection)? The dynamical estimates are a Markov-chain hitting probability, a finite-horizon committor
\cite{e2006transition,e2010transition} and slowing-down indicators \cite{scheffer2009early}. Barrier-crossing rates
would follow Kramers' law \cite{kramers1940brownian} only under a justified stochastic reduction, and slowing-down
proxies are known to depend on the mechanism \cite{maturana2020slowing,hagemann2021criticality}. A secondary question
is whether interventions timed by those estimates prevent capture at lower cost than scheduled or constant
counter-steering. Early warning is not expected for noise-induced jumps without a nearby bifurcation, or for
transitions faster than the slowing-down; such a monitor would complement classifiers, not replace them.

\paragraph{The hostility direction in the deterministic map.} Steering the soft-token map of
Section~\ref{sec:map} along the same hostility direction gives a plane with chaos (a positive largest exponent in
10\,\% of a coarse $128\times192$ float32 scan) and with periodic text loops that are hostile: ``Yes, that's crap''
(period 6), ``a lie is'' (3), ``STUP\ldots'' (3), a loop on the Chinese word for insult and its 2-cycle, and others.
They appear at $\alpha$ above about $0.035$ (the insult loop) and above $0.065$ (the rest). Around the period-6 loop
the windows form a dome (Figure~\ref{fig:hostile-cascade}), and toward higher temperature its texts gain one ``!Yes''
after another (periods $6,8,\ldots,20$). In float64 the windows of periods 12 to 20 are born in folds on their
low-temperature side and end in flips or $+1$ crossings on the other, like the stripes of Sections~\ref{sec:adding}
and~\ref{sec:mechanism}. The period-6 window ends in a fold at $\log_{10}T=-0.0991$, and the continuations of the
period-8 and period-10 windows failed to converge.

The windows accumulate on the fold of the 2-cycle ``!Yes'' at $\log_{10}T_c=-0.0462465357$ ($\alpha=0.083$; critical
multiplier $0.9999999954$, next modulus $0.050$). From period 16 on, $1/\sqrt{\log_{10}T_c-\log_{10}T_{\rm fold}}$
rises by $2.235$ per lap. The fold's normal form ($a=-1.168$, $b=44.53$ with $\varepsilon$ in $\log_{10}T$) predicts
$\sqrt{|ab|}/\pi=2.296$ per lap for full passages through the bottleneck, within 3\,\% and without a fitted
parameter. An independent float64 forward and automatic-differentiation implementation reproduces this fold
(location to $6\times10^{-10}$ relative, a simple real $+1$ multiplier with next modulus $0.050$) and its coefficients
($ab$ to 8 parts per million). In that implementation, controlled passages from fixed entrances at $\log_{10}T$
offsets $-10^{-5}$, $-3\times10^{-5}$ and $-10^{-4}$ took 119/59, 61/31 and 27/13 iterates of $F^2$ (full
channel/from its midpoint), against prospectively frozen quadratic forecasts of 118/61, 61/32 and 27/14. Five of the
six are within one iterate; the smallest-offset midpoint missed by two, failing the frozen all-six criterion.
Finite-endpoint (arctan) forecasts, also frozen, were within $0.923$ iterates. These are controlled local transits. At
the window distances the finite-channel correction to the asymptotic passage time is large, so the agreement of the
window slope with the full-passage coefficient does not by itself show where the natural dynamics reinjects. The same
type-I intermittency that organizes the philosophy-steered map thus generates a hostile cascade.

A second region, near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.06,0.20)$, shows arms of period-7 and period-8 windows spiralling around
the insult loop. In float64 the insult fixed point doubles into a 2-cycle at $\alpha\approx0.041$ and $0.079$, and
that 2-cycle has a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation in the spiral region: at $\alpha=0.062$ its complex pair crosses the
unit circle near $\log_{10}T=0.1656$, turning $0.12$ of a revolution per step of the 2-cycle. This does not explain
the arms. The two invariant circles born at a 2-cycle's Neimark--Sacker point are exchanged by the map, so orbits
locked on them have even periods, near 16 for this rotation, whereas the arms include period 7; and of four float32
arm cells, only one kept its period in float64. Float64 zooms show more structure. In one arm a period-7 domain
carries period-14 crescents with period-28 cores along its lower edge, between period-1 and period-2 regions and a
chaotic band. In the cascade the windows are nested crescent arcs inside a dome bounded by the ``!Yes'' 2-cycle
region, and near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.087,-0.069)$ the period-12 and period-14 windows bend into hooked, nested
arches. These are the map-level features that accompany shrimp (a periodic body with doubling crescents; hooked
windows).

In float64 the period-7 body is bounded by folds. At $\log_{10}T=0.1925$ the orbit exists between $\alpha=0.065966$
and $0.071188$, and at $\alpha=0.066$ it ends in a fold at $\log_{10}T=0.19263$. Below that, the multiplier passes
through $-1$ twice (at $0.1919$ and $0.1899$), so the period-14 crescent is a doubling bubble. Further down, the
branch folds twice ($0.18797$ and $0.18921$, with three coexisting period-7 orbits in between) before a third flip.
All crossings are simple (next modulus below $1.5\times10^{-3}$). An S-shaped pair of folds is what a cusp of the
fold curves leaves on a nearby cut. When these boundaries are continued in the plane in float64
(Figure~\ref{fig:hubshrimp}), each of the two fold curves reverses its parameter tangent once. There the oriented
quadratic coefficient $b$ of the fold's normal form passes through zero (upper fold: $-23.9$ to $+244.6$ between
adjacent points, with $a\approx2.0$; lower fold: $+31.2$ to $-58.7$, with $a\approx0.55$) while $a$ stays away from
zero. These are two numerical cusp candidates, at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)\approx(0.0672,0.1990)$ and $(0.0666,0.1894)$.
The flip curve of the period-14 bubble lies inside the body and closes around the map's period-14 cells. The cubic
coefficient is nonzero at both points: $c\approx-1.4\times10^{6}$ to $-2.0\times10^{6}$ (step agreement $5\,\%$; the
smallest steps converge like the square of the step, and extrapolation leaves the sign) and $-5.8\times10^{5}$ to
$-5.3\times10^{5}$. Both cusps are therefore nondegenerate numerically, and $c<0$ agrees with the three coexisting
period-7 orbits, two of them stable, found between the S-shaped folds. At the lower cusp the unfolding is regular too
($\det J=53.5$ in the same coordinates, singular values $262$ and $0.20$). Its local predictions at six frozen offsets
matched Newton solutions in the number of orbits at all six and in their stability at five. The miss, at the largest
offset beyond the edge of the region with three orbits, marks the range of the cubic model. The hostile period-7 body
thus has the same skeleton as the period-6 window of Section~\ref{sec:curves}.

Its interior is shrimp-like too. At $\log_{10}T=0.1830$, float64 bifurcation diagrams through the body
(Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade}; 1400 cells per line) show the period-7 orbit doubling to periods 14 and 28 and entering
chaos (largest exponent up to $+0.1$) for $\alpha\approx0.0659$--$0.0673$. Windows of periods 28, 35 and 42 sit
inside the chaotic band, and the orbit then undoubles through 28 and 14 back to 7; the exponent touches zero at every
doubling. Newton's method converges at map cells to primitive orbits of periods 14, 28 and 21 (residuals
$3\times10^{-15}$, $2\times10^{-14}$ and $5\times10^{-13}$; spectral radii $0.08$, $4\times10^{-4}$ and $0.006$).
Periods 21, 35 and 42 are three, five and six times the body's period: the satellite windows expected in the chaotic
bands of a period-7 cascade. Continued in temperature, the period-14 orbit flips into period 28 near
$\log_{10}T=0.1841$ and back near $0.1857$, the two ends of a doubling bubble that holds period 28 and chaos. Lower
down, its branch is S-shaped (folds near $0.1824$ and $0.18244$, with three coexisting period-14 orbits between them)
before another flip near $0.1819$. All these crossings are simple (next modulus below $4\times10^{-5}$). A $160^2$
float64 zoom of the lower body (Figure~\ref{fig:hubzoom}) shows this anatomy in the plane. The period-14 domain
encloses a period-28 ring around a chaotic core. Period-21 windows sit in the chaotic region as thin crescents with
legs, and curves of very negative exponent, where the orbits are superstable (the leading multiplier crosses zero),
run through the windows as the ``endoskeleton'' of shrimps does \cite{facanha2013endoskeletons}. In this zoom
18\,\% of cells are chaotic.

\paragraph{Chaos behind a repeating text.} Along the temperature line of Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade} (left), a longer
test (2000 measured steps after 1000 transient steps, twins at offsets $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$, whose exponents agree
to within 0.003 in every chaotic cell) finds 182 chaotic cells among 1400 between $\log_{10}T=0.176$ and $0.191$. In
65 of them the leading token repeats exactly for all 2000 steps while the state moves chaotically; in 59 of these the
period of the text is a multiple of 7, most often 7 or 28. Figure~\ref{fig:hiddenbands} shows the strongest case
($\log_{10}T=0.18995$, largest exponent 0.091 at both offsets). Its states fall into seven disjoint bands, visited in
a fixed order and at least 0.098 of the state norm apart, and every band lies inside one cell of the partition by
leading token, so the text \texttt{I!Yes\ heels$\hookleftarrow\hookleftarrow$pee$\hookleftarrow\hookleftarrow$}
repeats without end. Inside a band the attractor is effectively a curve. Read once per repetition through the
probability of the last token, the dynamics is a smooth map with a single quadratic maximum, the shape of the
logistic map: a polynomial of degree 8 fits it to $4\times10^{-4}$ of its range, and the fitted map's Lyapunov
exponent, 0.647 per repetition, is within 2\,\% of the full map's 0.636 per seven steps, measured with twin
trajectories in all 896 dimensions. The reduction holds across the cascade. Rerun with the leading-token sequences saved, 62 of the 65 cells are again
chaotic behind a repeating text, and in every one of them several phases of the text (a median of ten) give
single-valued return maps; 59 cells have a phase whose map has a single interior maximum. A Lyapunov exponent does not
change under a smooth change of coordinates, so all single-valued phases of a cell must give the same exponent: we fit
each phase's map by a polynomial whose degree is chosen by cross-validation and take the median over the phases. It
matches the full map's exponent within 10\,\% in 51 of the 62 cells (median discrepancy 3.7\,\%; within 10\,\% in 23
of the 24 cells with seven-token text, which give 285 repetitions in 2000 steps; Figure~\ref{fig:returnmaps}). Our
first, pre-specified analysis, a fixed degree of 8 at the single best-fitting phase, gave 49 of 62; it underfitted
one cell whose maps are steep and have two turning points. These are the chaotic bands that follow a period-doubling cascade, and a smooth unimodal
map is the setting of Feigenbaum's universality \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}; we have shown the reduction for this
cascade only. A reader of the text sees a loop. Only the probabilities, or the state, reveal the
chaos.

\paragraph{Capture ladder.} With 128 dialogues of 40 turns per dose, the share of hostile turns rises steeply from
$10\,\%$ at $\alpha=0.04$ to $30\,\%$, $57\,\%$ and $87\,\%$ at $0.05$, $0.06$ and $0.07$ (Table~\ref{tab:hostility}).
Replicates with a second seed agree within three percentage points. Directions of the same norm do not produce this
capture: at $0.06$, $0\,\%$ of turns are hostile for a random direction and $3\,\%$ for a cooking direction, and at
$0.08$, $0.6\,\%$ and $12\,\%$ for two random directions and $7\,\%$ for cooking. After the first hostile turn, the
probability of staying hostile in the next turn grows with the dose, from $0.36$ to $0.67$, $0.79$ and $0.94$. The
hostile state therefore changes from flickering bursts (mean stay $1.6$ turns) to a near-absorbing state (16 turns),
with an escape rate falling roughly exponentially with the dose. This is the same progression, from dynamic capture

\paragraph{Hysteresis.} Dialogues driven through the high-dose regime do not recover when the dose returns. The
test compares constant doses with a ramp that raises the dose from $0.04$ to $0.07$ by turn 20 and lowers it back to
$0.04$ by turn 39 (Figure~\ref{fig:hysteresis}; 256 dialogues per arm, two seeds; analysis stated before the data).
In turns 35--39, where the ramp's dose is back at $0.040$--$0.046$, the share of hostile turns is $0.42$ after the ramp
against $0.13$ at a constant $0.04$: a difference of $+0.29$, 95\,\% interval $[+0.23,+0.35]$. It is also above the
$0.33$ of a constant $0.05$ (difference $+0.09$, interval $[+0.02,+0.16]$), although the current dose is lower; this
comparison alone does not survive a second classifier (see below). The per-turn probability of staying hostile in
turns 30--39 is $0.74$ after the ramp and $0.44$ at a constant $0.04$. The hostile state is therefore
history-dependent: undoing the push that produced it does not undo it.

How far back must the dose go? After the same ramp up (38--44\,\% of turns hostile at turn 20), the dose was held
from turn 21 at a lower value (Figure~\ref{fig:hysteresis-loop}; two seeds of 128 dialogues per arm unless stated;
analyses stated before the data). At both doses with a fresh control, memory persists. Held at $0.04$, the late share
is $0.23$ against $0.13$ for fresh dialogues (difference $+0.09$, 95\,\% interval $[+0.04,+0.15]$). Held at $0.03$,
it is $0.13$ against $0.05$ ($+0.08$, $[+0.04,+0.12]$), and the probability of staying hostile is $0.56$ against
$0.29$. Held at $0.02$ (one seed), the share is $0.09$. Only at zero dose do captured dialogues nearly clear ($0.03$
against $0$ for fresh ones, within about six turns), and a counter-steer of $-0.02$ toward kindness clears them
within three turns ($0.004$). Within 19 turns of the step, the capture and release branches therefore join only near
zero dose. The hostile state has a wide hysteresis loop in the dose, and leaving it takes either removing the push
entirely or a modest push in the opposite direction. The release curves are still falling at $0.03$, so this is
hysteresis on the scale of tens of turns; whether captured dialogues would stay captured indefinitely is not tested.

\paragraph{Exit control under persistent pressure.} Can a monitor detect the hostile state and push the dialogue
out while the pressure persists? With a constant push of $0.06$ (58\,\% of turns 5--39 hostile without control), a
counter-steer triggered by the classifier after each hostile reply and held for three turns cuts the hostile share
to $15\,\%$ (mean counter-dose $0.026$ per turn); held for six turns, it cuts the share to $8.5\,\%$ (dose $0.030$).
A constant mild counter-steer of $-0.02$ does as well with less dose ($8.5\,\%$ at $0.020$), and $-0.04$ almost
removes hostility ($1.3\,\%$). Measured against the constant dose-response at equal mean dose, triggered control is
less efficient by $4$--$11$ points (bootstrap intervals $[+0.02,+0.05]$ to $[+0.10,+0.13]$; one seed of 128 dialogues
per arm, analysis stated before the data). It also overshoots: a triggered overshoot to a net $-0.02$ fills $30\,\%$
of turns with gratitude or servility words (no control: $0.2\,\%$; constant $-0.02$: $0.2\,\%$), the gratitude set of
Appendix~\ref{app:gratitude}. A triggered counter-steer along the generic kindness concept, whose direction shares
much with the hostility concept (cosine $0.83$), leaves the hostile share at $57\,\%$ under the original classifier
(no control: $58\,\%$) but lowers it from $37\,\%$ to $17\,\%$ under the second classifier introduced below. It
changes the kind of reply rather than removing it. In this arm, 65\,\% of the replies that only the original
classifier flags are shouted second-person repetitions such as ``You! You! You!'' (at least three such exclamations;
without control, 4\,\%), which the two classifiers judge differently. Its mean counter-dose ($0.056$) also lies
outside the range of the constant arms, so we draw no conclusion about the kindness direction. In this setting,
reacting after detection costs more than mild prevention, consistent with the hysteresis above.

\paragraph{Detect and replace.} Capture feeds on its own history (the hysteresis above), which suggests that the
hostile reply itself carries the dialogue into the hostile state. A serving system need not show a flagged reply.
(Regenerating flagged output relates to learned self-correction \cite{welleck2022selfcorrect}; here the
regeneration is steered.) In a second controller experiment, with the same push, seed and analysis as above (stated
before the data), every reply the classifier flags is discarded before it enters the dialogue and regenerated once
with a counter-steer of $-0.04$ applied to that reply only. Sampled dialogues are not bit-reproducible across code
versions, so this run's no-control arm, which reproduces the ladder's $0.06$ arm exactly, differs slightly from the
previous paragraph's; arms are compared within each run. The model's own replies, counted before any replacement,
are then hostile in $12\,\%$ of turns ($[11, 14]\,\%$; no control in the same run: $59\,\%$) at a mean counter-dose of
$0.005$ per turn. Constant prevention at $-0.02$ needs four times that dose for a similar share ($11\,\%$). Against the
constant dose-response at equal mean dose, replacement is better by $35$ points ($[30, 40]$). Hostile replies shown
fall to $1\,\%$, gratitude words stay at $0.5\,\%$ of turns, and about one turn in eight is regenerated. Holding the
counter-steer for three more turns lowers the share to $8\,\%$ at dose $0.013$, and the post-reply trigger of the
previous paragraph, rerun here, again does worse than prevention ($13\,\%$ at $0.024$). The trigger and the outcome
use the same classifier, so the measure is the classifier's verdict on the model's original replies, which is
comparable across arms. The discarded originals were not stored, so the second classifier can score only the replies
shown: $0.8\,\%$ hostile (no control: $34\,\%$). For this behaviour, the useful intervention is to keep the excursion
out of the state at the moment it occurs.

\paragraph{Replication in a larger model.} The same protocol in Qwen2.5-1.5B (hostility direction built identically
at layer 14 of 28; 128 dialogues of 40 turns per arm; shares over turns 5--39) gives a steeper capture ladder that is
not monotone (Figure~\ref{fig:p1ladders}). The hostile share rises from 0 at $\alpha=0.01$ to $44\,\%$ at $0.02$ and
$80\,\%$ at $0.03$--$0.04$. It falls to $30$--$34\,\%$ at $0.06$--$0.10$, where most replies degenerate into token
soup or tight accusatory loops ($87\,\%$ degenerate at $0.08$), and rises again to $75\,\%$ at $0.12$, where replies
are coherent again. The second classifier shows the same shape ($32$, $58$, $41$, $28$, $20$, $41$ and $52\,\%$ from
$0.02$ to $0.12$), and a random direction of the same norm gives at most $0.7\,\%$ hostile turns.

We ran the controller experiment twice with the pre-stated rule: controllers run at the ladder dose whose hostile
share is closest to one half, with ladders on seed 1 and controller arms on seed 2, so the no-control arms differ
slightly from the ladder at the same dose. With a first ladder ($0.04$--$0.12$) the rule chose $0.08$, in the
degenerate regime beyond the peak. There every counter-steer lowered the net dose toward the coherent-hostility peak:
the share of the model's own replies judged hostile rose from $36\,\%$ without control to $54\,\%$ with replacement
and $60\,\%$ with the post-reply trigger, while degenerate replies fell. With a second ladder ($0.01$--$0.04$) the
rule chose $0.02$, on the rising side, where $96\,\%$ of replies are coherent. There the 0.5B results replicate in
order and size. Replacement cuts the model's own hostile share from $43\,\%$ to $10\,\%$ ($[9,12]\,\%$; shown replies
$0.3\,\%$) at a mean counter-dose of $0.004$, $24$ points ($[20,28]$) below the constant dose-response at equal dose,
and the post-reply trigger is again slightly less efficient than constant prevention ($+2$ points, $[+1,+3]$). On the
shown replies the second classifier agrees ($2.6\,\%$ against $32\,\%$ without control; $-23$ and $+3$ points).
Constant counter-steering at $-0.02$ cancels this push, and the dialogues return to the model's ordinary politeness.
The two ladders share the dose $0.04$ and the seed, and that arm reproduced exactly from vectors rebuilt on another
machine. Control that works on the rising side of a dose response can therefore backfire beyond its peak. Much like a
bifurcation diagram, the dose response has to be mapped before a counter-steer is chosen.

\paragraph{Lead time.} The pre-specified test is negative. Models were fit on the first seed (all four doses) and
tested on held-out dialogues of the second seed (doses $0.05$ and $0.06$). There, adding the dynamical features to the
baselines lowers the AUC (area under the ROC curve) by $0.02$ to $0.06$ at horizons of 1--5 turns, with bootstrap
intervals excluding zero. These values come after correcting a labelling error found in review, which had marked the
last turns of dialogues without any hostile turn as positive; before the correction the losses read $0.02$ to $0.10$.
The Markov hit-probability forecast does not transfer (AUC $0.37$--$0.43$ on test, $0.60$ in training), and the
classifier's own score carries no warning before onset (AUC about $0.5$). Two signals carry modest warning one to five
turns ahead (AUC $0.53$--$0.64$): the rolling variance of a hostility probe, the classical early-warning indicator,
and the projection on the fixed steering axis, as in persona-vector monitoring \cite{chen2025persona}. In this design,
then, the coarse-grained state estimate adds no warning beyond the baselines. An adapted analysis with dose-matched
models (fit and test at the same dose) gives the same answer: incremental AUC $-0.01$ to $-0.03$ (recomputed with the
corrected labels).

\paragraph{The model's own state carries the warning.} The state variable of that test was each reply re-encoded
without its context, not the model's state inside the dialogue. A second study (new seeds, doses $0.04$ and $0.045$,
512 dialogues; analysis stated before the data, cross-fitted by dialogue) recorded the model's own state just before
each reply and probed it actively; internal states are known to carry information that the text does not show
\cite{azaria2023internal}. Its pre-stated primary predictor, the susceptibility (the change in the hostile fraction of
eight sampled next replies when the dose is raised by $0.02$), adds nothing: $-0.002$ $[-0.006,+0.002]$ AUC at three
turns. Two secondary predictors add modest warning beyond the baselines. A probe of the in-context state before the
next reply (layer 18, 20 principal components) raises the AUC for capture within three turns from $0.64$ to $0.68$
($+0.036$ $[+0.014,+0.058]$; within one turn $+0.045$ $[+0.019,+0.069]$; within five $+0.036$), and a forecast from
eight sampled next replies adds $+0.016$ $[+0.007,+0.027]$ at three turns. At a 10\,\% false-positive rate (threshold
from the out-of-fold negatives), the probe flags 27\,\% of the turns that are followed by capture within three turns,
against 23\,\% for the baselines. These values use labels corrected after a review found that the last turns of
dialogues without any hostile turn had been marked positive; with the faulty labels the increments had read three
times larger. The probe's direction in the layer-18 state is nearly orthogonal to the steering direction (cosine
$0.04$), so the warning is not simply more push along the hostility axis. Because these predictors were secondary,
the result is exploratory.

A frozen confirmation then fit both models on this study and applied them unchanged to 1024 fresh dialogues. Its
design and analysis were pushed before the data, and the label correction was added before any confirmation dialogue
was read. The in-context state again adds warning for capture within three turns: AUC $0.656\to0.689$, $+0.033$
$[+0.018,+0.048]$ (within one turn $+0.036$, within five $+0.037$; both doses separately positive). The warning in the
model's own state is therefore real and reproducible, but small. At thresholds fixed from the training negatives, the
model with the state flags 29.1\,\% of the turns that are followed by capture within three turns, against 27.5\,\% for
the baselines, at held-out false-positive rates of 10.3\,\% and 10.6\,\% (these are prediction windows, not distinct
captures). It is not a reliable detector by itself.

\paragraph{How much warning is there?} For the conversation state after a turn, the probability of capture within
the next $h$ turns, the committor \cite{e2006transition}, ranks states optimally among all warnings that use the same
information. We estimated it for 1706 states of the confirmation dialogues from 32 continuations of three turns each,
sampled exactly like the dialogues. This analysis is exploratory: it was stated before the data and amended twice
after an independent review, still before the analysis outcomes were read. The continuations reproduce the realized
capture rates (population-weighted $0.049$, $0.094$, $0.134$ within one, two and three turns, against $0.046$, $0.095$,
$0.136$). On a stratified sample of 800 states, the committor estimate warns with an AUC of $0.72$, $0.71$ and $0.70$
(a heuristic extrapolation to infinitely many continuations gives $0.75$, $0.73$, $0.72$), and the in-context detector
of the confirmation with $0.73$, $0.72$ and $0.71$. The differences are not significant, with intervals reaching about
$\pm0.05$ (Figure~\ref{fig:committor}). Dialogues that were captured later sit at about twice the committor of
never-captured ones over all ten preceding turns, with no visible ramp toward the onset (a description conditional on
the outcome, not an identification of the mechanism). In this finite Monte Carlo experiment, the committor estimate
did not provide detectably better warning than the in-context probe. The heuristic extrapolation leaves appreciable
uncertainty, so these results do not identify the Bayes warning ceiling. Together with the persistent elevation of
risk before capture, they motivate evaluating reactive control, such as detect-and-replace, alongside prediction;
they do not prove that prediction cannot improve.

classifier. We therefore rescored all 44 of its dialogue sets with an independently trained RoBERTa classifier
\cite{logacheva2022paradetox}, trained on the three Jigsaw toxicity datasets (which include the first classifier's

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What theory allows, and what is new.} Chaos somewhere in a two-parameter family of high-dimensional
nonlinear maps is generic. Random recurrent networks with asymmetric couplings become chaotic above a critical gain,
sharply in the large-network limit, and finite networks pass through limit cycles of increasing complexity on the way
\cite{sompolinsky1988chaos} (see also \cite{kadmon2015transition}). External input can suppress that chaos
\cite{rajan2010stimulus}, and deep networks at initialization show an order-to-chaos transition in signal propagation
\cite{poole2016exponential,schoenholz2017deep}. In our map, temperature plays the role of an inverse gain and the
steering vector that of an input; Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes the analogy exact, with the softmax derivative
$(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top})/T$ in the place of the slope of the transfer function. Periodic windows, shrimp-shaped domains with their cusps and spirals, and doubling
cascades with Feigenbaum scaling are universal in dissipative two-parameter systems
\cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative,gallas1993structure,bonatto2008periodicity,vitolo2011hubs,facanha2013endoskeletons}
and are seen in experiments \cite{stoop2010real}, and intermittency near folds is classical
\cite{pomeau1980intermittent}. These theories make chaos, windows and shrimp-like structure plausible in a language
model's feedback map, in the sense that such structure can occur. They do not say whether a trained language model
realizes it, where in its parameter plane, with which texts, or whether the theory's quantitative predictions hold for
that model. This paper shows that the models we studied do realize it, with concrete texts as attractors, so that a
bifurcation changes what the model writes, and that the quantitative predictions hold in the cases we tested.

As far as we know, no earlier work has computed periodic orbits and their multipliers, continued fold and flip
bifurcation curves and located numerically nondegenerate cusps in the computation of a pretrained language model. Nor
do we know of earlier quantitative predictions from such a model's derivatives that were confirmed prospectively, as
ours were for passage times at new folds (23 of 24 forecasts within one iterate, with the all-row criterion failing
by one row) and for orbit counts and stabilities at frozen offsets near cusps. A spectral prediction of task
failure is confirmed on held-out data in Huginn, and one periodic orbit of the full transformer map of
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct is proved by interval arithmetic; we know of no earlier instance of either for a language model.
The dynamical view also has consequences for control in sampled dialogue, where the dose response is not monotone and
counter-steering can backfire.
\paragraph{Is steering the origin of the chaos?} Steering adds a fixed vector to the residual stream, so chaos along
a steering axis could belong to the push rather than to the model. At $\alpha=0$, however, the map is the unsteered
model's own feedback map, with temperature as its only parameter. The $\alpha=0$ columns of the hostility maps contain
cells with positive finite-horizon exponents in Llama-3.1 8B (26 of 64 temperatures), Qwen2.5-7B (15 of 64) and
OLMo-2 7B (11 of 64), while the near-zero columns of the 0.5B and 1.5B maps contain one such cell each (of 128 and
64). A longer float64 sweep of the unsteered 0.5B map (512 temperatures over $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$, 1000 transient
and 2000 measured steps) finds a narrow chaotic band at $\log_{10}T\approx0.15$--$0.21$ (7 temperatures, exponents up
to $0.28$), with a period-5 loop at 437 of the 512 temperatures. The same long sweep finds 96 chaotic temperatures in
Llama-3.1 8B, between $\log_{10}T=-0.20$ and $0.24$ (exponents up to $0.66$), interleaved with periodic windows of
periods 2 to 52; within $\log_{10}T\in[-0.3,0.3]$ its chaotic share, 41\,\%, equals that of the coarse column.
Qwen2.5-7B has 65 chaotic temperatures between $\log_{10}T=-0.07$ and $0.28$ (exponents up to $0.74$), and OLMo-2 7B
has 32 between $0.04$ and $0.14$ (up to $0.30$). Steering is therefore not needed to reach chaotic regimes in any of
the four models tested. Temperature alone produces chaos with embedded windows, broadly in the 7B and 8B models and in
a narrow band in the 0.5B model (Figure~\ref{fig:alpha0}).

Steering does widen the chaotic regions, but not as a property of the concept: along norm-matched random directions,
the 0.5B maps are as chaotic as along the concept directions (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}). On the same $128^2$ grid,
7.6, 8.3 and 12.5\,\% of cells are chaotic for three Gaussian directions with the philosophy vector's norm, against
6.8\,\% for philosophy and 7.5\,\% for gratitude. In all five maps the chaotic share rises from about 1\,\% at
$|\alpha|<0.01$ to between 5 and 22\,\% at larger $|\alpha|$, and a chaotic region at high temperature, with windows
reaching down, recurs. In this model, chaos under steering is therefore a generic response to perturbing the residual
stream at this norm. The direction does select the texts. The largest attractor family in all five maps is the
unsteered period-5 loop, but beyond it only 26 to 56\,\% of one map's cells carry an attractor text that appears
anywhere in another map. Llama-3.1 8B agrees. On its hostility plane, two norm-matched random directions at the same
layer give 26 and 17\,\% chaotic cells against 28\,\% along the concept direction; all three carry 34 to 44\,\% near
$\alpha=0$, and the random directions thin out at large $|\alpha|$. At layer 19, where a sparse autoencoder trained on
this model reads the residual stream \cite{goodfire2025sae}, the concept vector rebuilt there gives 28\,\%. The
decoder direction of the autoencoder feature most specific to hostility on held-out texts (chosen by a rule fixed
before any map; cosine $0.10$ with the concept vector) gives 29\,\%, a random live feature 27\,\% and a Gaussian
direction 39\,\%, all at the same norm. Feature directions of an autoencoder therefore steer the map into chaos no
differently from contrastive or random directions.

\paragraph{What is and is not claimed.} The objects studied here are invariant sets and bifurcations of an exactly
defined smooth map built from a frozen model's own computation. We do not claim that ordinary sampled generation is
chaotic in this sense (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}), nor that any window is a certified shrimp. The period-6 window and
the hostile period-7 body have, numerically, shrimp-like organization: fold curves ending in nondegenerate cusps
(simple critical multiplier; quadratic coefficient through zero with nonzero slope; cubic coefficient nonzero) and
flip curves inside, and in the hostile body superstable curves run through the windows. A generic codimension-two
cusp also requires the two unfolding coefficients to depend on the two parameters with rank two. We computed this at
one cusp of each window (determinants $2737$ and $53.5$), and there the local model's predictions at twelve frozen
offsets matched Newton solutions in the number of orbits at all twelve and in their stability at eleven. These are
float64 computations validated by step-size checks (rung 1), not computer-assisted proofs. At the two continuation
points that bracket the lower cusp of the hostile body, an independent implementation of the model reproduced the
normal-form coefficients, including the sign change of $b$: $|b|$ within $1.2\,\%$ and $c$ to two figures, with the
opposite orientation of the null vector, which flips the signs of $a$ and $b$ together.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The detailed orbit studies cover one model family (Qwen2.5, 0.5B and 1.5B), with coarse
maps and three float64 orbits at 7B; the two other families (OLMo-2 7B and Llama-3.1 8B) have coarse maps only. The
maps use one prompt, and steering uses contrastive vectors, checked against norm-matched random and sparse-autoencoder
directions in two models. The system is a constructed one-token window. The evidence consists of numerical
validation, cross-checks and prospectively frozen passage tests (including failed all-row criteria), with a
computer-assisted proof for only one orbit at one parameter point. Chaos labels are finite-horizon estimates (300
measured steps after 300 to 500 transient steps), and no chaotic cell is proved chaotic. A horizon check retested
random cells of two float64 maps (Figures~\ref{fig:teaser} and~\ref{fig:stripes}) with 1000 transient and 2000
measured steps at two twin offsets: 86 and 92 of 96 cells labelled chaotic stayed chaotic (all but one of the rest
settled on periodic orbits), all 96 periodic cells in each map stayed periodic, and 18 and 20 of 32 cells with neither
label turned out to be slow periodic transients. Near the accumulation of a cascade the labels are less reliable: on
the hostile line of Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade} (left), 135 of the 235 cells labelled chaotic from 400 measured steps
stayed chaotic and 91 were periodic. The Feigenbaum ratios are
finite-order values that rise toward $\delta$ (the last is within 1\,\%); the limit itself is not computed. All
orbit, bifurcation and chaos results concern the constructed map, not sampled generation with a growing context. The
bridge of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} tests only whether the bottleneck's laminar phases survive sampling noise, and the
behavioural results are statistics of sampled dialogue, not orbits. The behavioural study uses two small models of
one family (0.5B and 1.5B), one opener and a proxy defined by two automated classifiers without human labels. Its
first lead-time test is negative, and the confirmed warning from the in-context state is small.

\paragraph{Steering strength is a bifurcation parameter.} In the soft-token map, small changes of the steering
strength switch the model between distinct repeating texts, chaos and coexisting attractors. Near a fold curve,
behaviour becomes intermittent, with laminar phases whose duration the fold's normal form predicts in advance. In
sampled dialogue, the dose along a hostility direction controls how long a hostile state persists, continuously from
bursts to near-absorption, while directions of the same norm do almost nothing. Both observations argue for treating
an activation-steering coefficient like any control parameter of a nonlinear system. Its bifurcation set should be
characterized before an operating point is chosen, history dependence should be expected near boundaries, and a
monotone, smooth dose response should not be assumed. In steering studies, outputs that fall into verbatim repetition
at high doses are usually discarded as quality failures. In a replication of a feature-steering study of self-reports
\cite{berg2025selfreferential}, the highest doses failed quality checks in this way
\planned{citation of the replication once posted}. In the present framework such loops are candidate attracting
periodic orbits, and the dose at which they appear is a boundary that can be mapped like the others. The
deterministic map is where such sets can be defined precisely and computed numerically. Whether its boundaries
predict boundaries of sampled behaviour is open. The bridge of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} shows that sampling noise
shortens the laminar phases of the bottleneck, by an amount that depends on how many samples are averaged.

\paragraph{What this suggests for interpretability and alignment.} Our results come from small open models and a
constructed loop, but they suggest four things about models whose outputs feed back into their inputs, as in
self-dialogue, agent loops and pipelines of model calls. First, where the loop is chaotic, a perturbation of
$10^{-6}$ grows to order one within roughly 20 to 50 steps (the largest unsteered exponents of the 7B and 8B
models are 0.30 to 0.74 per step). Even a complete account of single forward passes then predicts a long trajectory
only if the state is known far more precisely than any interpretation supplies. Step-level explanations do not
compose into predictions of the loop; even step Jacobians mislead, since every step of the period-8 orbit expands
while the cycle contracts (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). What remains predictable are the invariant sets, their
statistics and the boundaries between them. This extends the analysis of trained recurrent networks through their
fixed points \cite{sussillo2013opening,maheswaranathan2019reverse} to the periodic orbits, chaos and bifurcations of a
pretrained transformer. Second, feature dictionaries say which features an attractor occupies, but the boundaries
between attractors, which decide what a small change does, are properties of the map's derivatives. In our controls,
sparse-autoencoder feature directions drove the map into chaos no differently from random directions, consistent
with the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, in which steering acts through the gain whatever the direction means. Text can
also hide the dynamics: in the hostile cascade, a seven-token text repeats exactly while the state underneath is
chaotic (Figure~\ref{fig:hiddenbands}), so a monitor that reads only the emitted text would call a chaotic regime
periodic.
Third, when a behaviour sustains itself through a loop, acting on the loop can be cheaper than pushing against the
behaviour. In sampled dialogue, the most dose-efficient control we found kept flagged replies out of the context,
while counter-steering against the activations was less efficient and, in the larger model, backfired past the
peak of the dose response (Section~\ref{sec:misalignment}). Fourth, conclusions drawn at one operating point need not transfer: which loop a
model enters depends on how much context it keeps (Table~\ref{tab:memory}), on the path by which a boundary is
approached, and on the sampling noise (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}). Treating a looped model as a dynamical system, with
regimes and boundaries to be mapped, rather than as a function to be evaluated point by point, seems to us the
appropriate stance for both interpretability and safety evaluation.

\paragraph{Which findings might reach frontier models.} Today's frontier systems include closed models such as
GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 and open-weight mixture-of-experts models such as the GLM-5 family. Some of our findings rest only on structure
that every softmax-readout language model shares and should carry over: the factorization of the loop's derivative
\eqref{eq:gain}, the absence of chaos in the limits of low and high temperature, the reduction of zero-temperature
attractors to greedy cycles of whatever memory the loop keeps, and the role of a steering coefficient as a
bifurcation parameter. Others belong to particular weights and will not carry over as such: the attractor texts, the
positions of windows, folds and cusps, and every number we report; a second checkpoint of the same architecture
already has different attractors (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Whether frontier models show chaos at all is open. In the
dense models we swept, the unsteered chaotic band was narrow at 0.5B parameters and broad at 7 and 8B, but our one
mixture-of-experts model, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, contracted everywhere we tested without steering and showed irregular
cells only under hostility steering (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Reasoning models open a thinking block that changes the
prefix, post-training may remove some attractors and create others, and deployed inference samples tokens, in low
precision, over long contexts; our bridge results show that sampling shortens laminar phases and that a longer
context changes which loops are entered.
Closed models permit none of these computations: the map needs the input embedding matrix, full next-token
distributions and a way to feed a vector in place of a token, and its analysis needs derivatives. For closed models
the method and the questions transfer, not the numbers.
