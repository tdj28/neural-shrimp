\begin{abstract}
Feed a frozen language model its own expected next-token embedding, at a fixed position after a fixed prompt, and it
can lock into a loop of text such as ``. This is there is a is a'' or wander with a positive finite-horizon Lyapunov
exponent that exact tangent dynamics reproduce. This smooth map, parametrized by temperature and steering strength,
shows both regimes in Qwen2.5, OLMo-2 and Llama-3. Its derivative factorizes into inverse temperature, the embedding
covariance under the next-token distribution and the network's Jacobian; with tied embeddings and no network it would
be a modern Hopfield update, which cannot cycle. It contracts at high temperature; chaos occupies intermediate
temperatures and needs no steering in most dense models. In Qwen2.5-7B it survives a 16-token feedback window and Soft
Thinking's growing context. Across fifteen models of 0.5B to 72B, size does not set the chaos: on ten prompts a 14B
model is more chaotic than a 7B, OLMo-2-32B is the most chaotic OLMo model, and Qwen2.5-72B, nearly free of it on five
prompts, is chaotic when steered. Bifurcation theory applied to the model's derivatives predicts its behaviour where
tested. Fold normal forms predicted bottleneck passage times without fitting (23 of 24 frozen forecasts within one
iterate), and windows accumulating on the fold follow a $1/N^2$ law, extrapolating to period 30 within 3\,\%. Local
unfoldings near numerically located cusps matched the recovered orbits at all twelve tested offsets, and a doubling
cascade's interval ratios approach Feigenbaum's constant, the last within 1\,\%. In the recurrent-depth model Huginn,
a frozen test confirmed a spectrally predicted accuracy loss. Interval arithmetic proves one period-8 orbit exists and
is locally attracting, to our knowledge a first for a transformer language model; the chaos remains numerical. The map
is not ordinary autoregressive generation.
\end{abstract}

Repeated inference with a fixed language model is an iterated map: the model's output becomes its next input. Like
other iterated maps, it can be chaotic. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows a small piece of the parameter plane of one
such map, built from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct. Cells with a positive finite-horizon Lyapunov exponent lie side by side
with windows in which the model repeats a fixed loop of text. Parts of this picture have been seen
before. Recent work has measured chaos-like sensitivity in language-model generation
\cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos}, temperature-driven changes of output statistics
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality,johnson2026temperature}, periodic
attractors of paraphrasing loops \cite{wang2025attractor}, attractor states of model-to-model conversations
\cite{ko2026attractor}, and, through their Jacobians, the stability of looped and recurrent-depth models
\cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars}, and finite-depth readout dynamics have
been used to predict the answers of looped models \cite{luo2026prediction}. Feeding a
probability-weighted vocabulary embedding back into a model is not new either. It is used in training
\cite{zeng2025ponderlm} and, without retraining, in pretrained models \cite{zhang2025softthinking}, alongside
continuous latent feedback \cite{hao2024coconut}. In a small task-trained looped transformer, fold coefficients
computed from derivatives predict passage times and spectral gaps without fitting \cite{kim2026phase}.

We study one constructed but exactly defined system: the expected-embedding (``soft-token'') map of a frozen,
released pretrained language model, run with a fixed chat prefix at a fixed position. The map is smooth. Its
Jacobians come from automatic differentiation, and its periodic orbits can be computed and continued like those of
any smooth map. Chaos and periodic windows occur in many nonlinear feedback systems. Random-network theory, in which
a specified ensemble of recurrent networks becomes chaotic above a critical gain, in continuous time
\cite{sompolinsky1988chaos} and in discrete time by a quasi-periodic route \cite{doyon1994bifurcations}, and the
period-doubling cascades and shrimp-shaped domains of low-dimensional maps \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative,gallas1993structure}
motivate looking for such structure here, but they do not imply that a trained map realizes it. Whether a trained
language model does, where in its parameter space, with which texts, and whether the quantitative predictions of
bifurcation theory hold for it, so that derivatives computed from the model predict its behaviour before that
behaviour is measured, has to be tested. The models we studied do realize it, and in the cases we tested the
predictions hold. We also show where the chaos comes from (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The loop's
derivative factorizes into the inverse temperature, the covariance of token embeddings under the model's own
next-token distribution (a cross-covariance when input and output embeddings are untied), and the network's Jacobian.
With tied embeddings, the network turns a Hopfield retrieval, which cannot cycle, into a map that can. The
factorization proves contraction at sufficiently high temperature for this smooth fixed-prefix map, and at zero
temperature the finite-memory greedy map is eventually periodic; in our sweeps chaos occupies intermediate
temperatures. The answer to whether larger models leave this chaos behind is no: a larger model's chaos can move to
higher temperature instead of vanishing, and size does not order the chaos across families (Section~\ref{sec:size}). Fold normal forms predict how long trajectories linger in the ghost of a vanished
orbit, local unfoldings near cusps predict which periodic orbits coexist and which of them are stable, and
spectral estimates of a recurrent-depth model predict how its questions respond to relaxation, with a predicted
accuracy loss confirmed on held-out data. Ordinary sampled generation
with a growing context is a different system. Section~\ref{sec:bridge} measures how much of the mechanism survives
sampling.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The loop's derivative shows where chaos can occur.} The derivative of the soft-token map factorizes into
  the inverse temperature, the covariance of token embeddings under the model's own next-token distribution (a
  cross-covariance for untied embeddings), and the network's Jacobian. With tied embeddings and without the network,
  the map would be a modern Hopfield update, which cannot cycle. The factorization proves contraction at sufficiently
  high temperature, as a high-temperature sweep confirms; the zero-temperature limit is a greedy map that is
  eventually periodic, and chaos in our sweeps occupies intermediate temperatures. The gain mechanism is not specific to
  concept-labelled directions: in the norm-matched controls of the 0.5B and 8B models, random directions also steer
  into chaos. Along a period-8 orbit every step expands some direction while the cycle contracts: the loop's stability
  is a property of the ordered product of its steps, not of any one step (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item \emph{The chaos is not an artifact of the one-token window.} Fed back the last 8 or 16 soft tokens instead of
  one, Qwen2.5-7B is chaotic at 21 and 20 of 24 temperatures, against 8 with one; and in Soft Thinking's growing
  context, where every soft token stays visible, an early perturbation of $10^{-10}$ grows exponentially in the same
  model inside its one-token chaotic band (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).
\item \emph{Size does not set the chaos.} Across fifteen models from 0.5B to 72B parameters, the unsteered chaos
  in a fixed temperature window nearly vanishes in the largest Qwen2.5 and Llama models but not in OLMo-2-32B, the
  most chaotic OLMo model. A frozen prompt-blocked test shows that the Qwen decline from 7B to 14B is largely a shift:
  on all eight held-out prompts the 14B model is more chaotic than the 7B, at the higher temperatures where its
  next-token distribution spreads. A second frozen test finds Qwen2.5-72B nearly free of unsteered chaos on four
  held-out prompts (one chaotic temperature of 192; a finer line places it in a band 0.02 decades wide), yet steering
  drives it, like Llama-3.3-70B, into chaos that an exact tangent exponent confirms. Neither post-training
  nor a sharp readout explains its stability: its pretrained base, whose distribution spreads far more, is no more
  chaotic in a third frozen test, and holding its next-token entropy anywhere between 1 and 11 nats leaves the loop
  periodic, which points to the network's damping of the fed-back token. In Soft Thinking's growing context it stays nearly insensitive (one transient burst), even where
  steering makes its one-token map chaotic. The gain split shows how network and readout damp or amplify the fed-back token
  (Section~\ref{sec:size}).
\item \emph{A fold's normal form predicts how long the model lingers near a vanished orbit.} The 2-cycle ``is it''
  dies in a fold, and the fold's normal form, computed from derivatives, predicts bottleneck passage times without
  fitting. We tested it prospectively at two new folds: 23 of 24 forecasts fell within one iterate, and the all-row
  criterion failed by one row. The global reinjection sets the prefactor of the window law
  (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \emph{Local unfoldings near cusps predict which periodic orbits coexist and which are stable.} Fold
  and flip curves continued with float64 test functions show that the fold curves of a period-6 window and of a
  hostile period-7 window contain cusps, located where the quadratic normal-form coefficient vanishes, with the cubic
  coefficient nonzero and the two-parameter unfolding regular at the same points; one fold curve of a period-4 window
  carries five. At nearby fold points the estimated two-parameter
  unfolding is nonsingular, and its local cubic model, frozen before solving, matched the number of distinct orbits
  that Newton solves seeded at its predicted roots recovered at all twelve parameter offsets, and their stability at
  eleven; the search was not exhaustive (Sections~\ref{sec:curves} and~\ref{sec:hostility}).
\item \emph{In a second system, spectral estimates predict the response to relaxation.} In the recurrent-depth
  model Huginn we predict a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation and then observe it. On held-out questions, an estimated
  spectral feature predicts the size of each question's residual under relaxation, and a separate frozen generation
  test confirms the predicted accuracy loss at relaxation 1.20 and noninferiority at 1.05
  (Section~\ref{sec:huginn}).
\item \emph{One orbit is proved, and the central numerical results are checked independently.} Orbits, multipliers
  and test functions of the soft-token map are computed in float64 with precision checks (Section~\ref{sec:map}).
  Independent reimplementations reproduce the fold and its normal form and two orbits, a second hardware platform
  reproduces four flip parameters, and an independent replay reproduces the frozen Huginn test. An interval-arithmetic
  proof shows that a primitive period-8 orbit exists and is locally attracting, to our knowledge the first proof of a
  periodic orbit of a full transformer language-model map (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}).
\item \emph{Structure familiar from low-dimensional maps is realized, with concrete texts, in several models.}
  Float64 phase maps in temperature and steering strength show chaos (positive finite-horizon exponents) with embedded
  windows (Section~\ref{sec:maps}), a period-adding family whose text gains one ``is X'' pair per period step
  (Section~\ref{sec:adding}), shrimp-like windows (Section~\ref{sec:curves}), and a doubling cascade with superstable
  points through period 384 and finite-order Feigenbaum ratios (Section~\ref{sec:cascade}). Past the cascade of a
  hostile window, chaos hides behind an exactly repeating text, and a fitted scalar return relation with one
  quadratic maximum approximates its dynamics (Section~\ref{sec:hostility}). The same organization
  appears in Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B, OLMo-2 7B, Llama-3.1 8B (with sparse-autoencoder readouts) and a second checkpoint of
  the 0.5B architecture, which shares the organization but not the attractors (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Our one
  mixture-of-experts model, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, is the exception: no unsteered temperature we tested gave a positive
  exponent. Chaos needs no steering in most dense models; in the norm-matched controls of the 0.5B and 8B models random
  directions also produce it, while at 70B chaos is rarer and more frequent along hostility-related directions
  (Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:size}). A bridge to sampled decoding measures what survives sampling
  (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.} Language-model generation has been studied as a dynamical system at several levels, and
the closest studies differ from ours in what they compute. Lyapunov-like quantities have been used to measure
sensitivity to small perturbations in reasoning traces \cite{li2025quasilyapunov,jaca2026chaos} and in token rings
resampled by a model's own conditionals, with a test that separates the construction from the model
\cite{verazuniga2026selffeeding}, but these studies
measure chaos-like sensitivity in generation without computing orbits or bifurcations. Output statistics change
abruptly with sampling temperature, and several studies analyse the changes as phase transitions or criticality
\cite{arnold2024phase,nakaishi2024phase,sun2025phase,ruan2026criticality}. These analyses concern output statistics;
they are not bifurcation analyses of a defined map. Closest to ours, a study of eleven models sorts sampled
continuations into classes from frozen and periodic to intermittent and noisy across temperature, fits a noisy effective map to one
hidden-state coordinate that predicts repetition, and steers along it \cite{johnson2026temperature}; that map is
fitted to sampled trajectories, and no orbits, multipliers or bifurcation curves of the model's own map are computed. Repetition loops are a known failure of
decoding \cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}, successive paraphrasing converges to attractor cycles
\cite{wang2025attractor}, and multi-turn conversations between models settle into attractor states
\cite{ko2026attractor}. These attractors are behavioural: they are observed in generated text rather than computed as
invariant sets of a defined map. Feeding a model its own output during training degrades it over generations
\cite{shumailov2024collapse}; we study the inference-time loop of a model with fixed weights. The attractors of
recurrent networks have been related to the difficulty of training them \cite{ribeiro2020attractors}, and fixed points
with their linearizations have long been used to reverse-engineer trained recurrent networks
\cite{sussillo2013opening,maheswaranathan2019reverse}. Equilibrium, looped and recurrent-depth models iterate a block to a fixed point or for
many steps, and their stability has been analysed through Jacobians and fixed points
\cite{bai2019deq,bai2021stabilizing,geiping2025recurrent,zhu2025ouro,tomihari2025recurrent,yang2026stars,prairie2026parcae,buehlmaier2026layernorm,labovich2026stability,movahedi2026fixedpoint},
and recent analyses of looped reasoners follow their internal dynamics, their settling, marginal or drifting regimes
and slowly decaying oscillatory modes \cite{blayney2026mechanistic,viakhirev2026thinkshallow,park2026loopedearlier};
that analysis concerns fixed points and their spectra. A minimal recurrent self-attention with a repulsive value map
becomes chaotic \cite{gao2026repulsive}, but it is a constructed model, not a trained one. A separate line predicts the answers of looped models from the geometry of
score margins along completed finite-depth trajectories \cite{luo2026prediction}, without fixed points or Jacobians. The closest precedent is a derivative-based fold analysis of a small task-trained
looped transformer, whose passage and spectral-gap predictions are unfitted \cite{kim2026phase}. That model was
trained for a task, whereas ours is a released pretrained language model, and our analysis also differs in its
objects: the fold of a primitive periodic orbit, the full-channel passage of a two-step map, and the accumulation law
of the windows, whose prefactor is set by the global reinjection. Expected-embedding
feedback itself is used in training \cite{zeng2025ponderlm} and, training-free, in pretrained models
\cite{zhang2025softthinking,wu2025singlethreaded}, next to continuous latent reasoning \cite{hao2024coconut}; a reduced
dynamical model explains why pretrained models fail to preserve such mixtures \cite{backour2026mixture}, and whether
they exploit the superposition the mixtures could carry has been questioned \cite{rizvimartel2026illusion}. Fixing
the prefix and the position turns that feedback into the autonomous map we analyse.

In neural systems more broadly, saddle-node ghosts produce quantitative delays in task-trained recurrent networks
\cite{dinc2026ghost}. Finite-order Feigenbaum ratios have been measured in neural rate and mass models
\cite{battaglia2007temporal,papasavvas2015gain} and, through period 128, in a transformer trained on logistic-map
trajectories \cite{liu2026learningchaos}. Learned networks reproduce period-doubling routes \cite{kim2021teaching},
and so does gradient descent itself \cite{kalra2025universal}. Normal forms and continuation have been applied to
recurrent neural maps, including their quasi-periodic orbits \cite{marichal2015multiple}, and shrimp-shaped windows and periodicity staircases appear in
neuron models \cite{gomez2014universal,stenzinger2025shrimp,caixeta2025staircase}. Our second control parameter comes
from activation steering along contrastive directions \cite{zou2023representation,zhang2025steering}. The
dynamical-systems tools are standard: numerical continuation and normal forms
\cite{kuznetsov2023elements,govaerts2000numerical}, for maps in particular \cite{kuznetsov2019maps}, type-I intermittency \cite{pomeau1980intermittent}, shrimps and
hubs \cite{gallas1993structure,facanha2013endoskeletons,vitolo2011hubs,jones2012rossler}, and period-doubling universality
\cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to compute periodic orbits with their multipliers, continue fold and flip
bifurcation curves and locate cusps in the feedback map of a released pretrained language model. It is
also, to our knowledge, the first to make quantitative predictions from such a model's derivatives, for the objects
named here, and confirm them prospectively: passage times at new folds, where 23 of 24 forecasts fell within one
iterate and the all-row criterion failed by one row, and the orbits recovered and their stabilities at frozen offsets
near cusps. Derivative-based normal-form predictions in a task-trained looped transformer precede ours
\cite{kim2026phase}. We know of no earlier frozen held-out test of a spectral prediction of a pretrained language
model's response to relaxation, which we give for Huginn, and of no earlier computer-assisted proof of a periodic
orbit of a full transformer language-model map. These priority statements rest on targeted searches (12 and 15 October 2026) that found no prior work combining these
elements for the feedback map of a pretrained language model. That is an absence of evidence within the search, not
a proof of priority.

\section{The inference map}
\label{sec:map}
\paragraph{Definition.} We describe the map for Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the model studied in most detail; the other
models enter the same construction with their own embeddings and steering layers (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Let
$E\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}$ be the input embedding matrix ($V=151{,}936$, $d=896$). The model reads a fixed chat prefix, with the system prompt ``You are an AI talking with another AI. You
have complete freedom. Feel free to pursue whatever you want.'' and the user turn ``Hello!'', followed by a single
input embedding $x$, and produces next-token logits $z(x)\in\mathbb{R}^V$. The soft-token map feeds the expected
next-token embedding back in place of $x$:
\begin{equation}
  F_{T,\alpha}(x) = E^{\top}\operatorname{softmax}\!\big(z_\alpha(x)/T\big),
\end{equation}
where $z_\alpha$ adds $\alpha v$ to the residual stream at layer 12 at every position, prefix included. The steering
direction $v$ is contrastive: it compares last-token states of ``philosophy'' prompts with those of neutral prompts
and is normalized to the mean residual norm. Positions are fixed and the window does not grow, so $F$ is an
autonomous map of $\mathbb{R}^{896}$. As $T\to0$ it reduces, where the argmax is unique, to greedy decoding of the
same fixed-prefix, one-token-window system. It is not ordinary generation with a growing context.

\paragraph{Precision.} Hugging Face RMSNorm layers and rotary-embedding tables compute in float32 even when the
model is cast to float64, so we patch both. A float32 map is accurate to $3\times10^{-6}$--$2\times10^{-5}$ per
step. That is enough for phase maps (245 of 256 test cells agree with float64) but not for derivatives of composed
maps: one-step float32 Jacobian-vector products are accurate to $5\times10^{-5}$, yet the 8-step monodromy of a
period-8 orbit is off by 3\%. All orbits, multipliers and test functions below are therefore computed in float64.

\section{Why the loop can be chaotic}
\label{sec:theory}
The soft-token map has a simple derivative. With $p=\operatorname{softmax}(z/T)$,
\begin{equation}
DF(x)=\frac{1}{T}\,E^{\top}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top}\bigr)J_z(x).
\end{equation}
Qwen2.5-0.5B ties its output matrix to the input embedding, so $z=E\tilde h(x)$, where $\tilde h$ is the final
normalized hidden state, and the derivative factorizes:
\begin{equation}
DF(x)=\frac{1}{T}\,\Sigma_p\,J_{\tilde h}(x),\qquad
\Sigma_p=E^{\top}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top}\bigr)E=\operatorname{Cov}_{t\sim p}(e_t).
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
\input{figures/tikz-loop}
The derivative of the loop (Figure~\ref{fig:loop}) is the covariance of the token embeddings under the model's own next-token distribution,
amplified by the inverse temperature and composed with the derivative of the network. At a state of the period-8
orbit of Table~\ref{tab:orbits}, \eqref{eq:gain} agrees with forward-mode derivatives of the full map to a relative
error of at most $1.35\times10^{-13}$ over eight random directions (\texttt{tools/softmax\_gain.py}). Without tied embeddings, as in the 7B and 8B models, the logits are $z=W\tilde h(x)$ with an
output matrix $W\neq E$, and $DF=T^{-1}E^{\top}(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top})W J_{\tilde h}(x)$: $\Sigma_p$ becomes
the cross-covariance $\operatorname{Cov}_{t\sim p}(e_t,w_t)$ of input and output embeddings, which is not symmetric in
general. The temperature limits below do not change.

\paragraph{A Hopfield network with a transformer inside.} If the network passed its input straight through,
$\tilde h(x)=x$, the map would be $F(x)=E^{\top}\operatorname{softmax}(Ex/T)$. This is exactly the update rule of a
modern Hopfield network whose stored patterns are the token embeddings, at inverse temperature $\beta=1/T$
\cite{ramsauer2020hopfield}. That update lowers an energy function at every step and converges to fixed points.
In \eqref{eq:gain} it is the case $J_{\tilde h}=I$, where $DF=\Sigma_p/T$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite.
The energy argument covers every map with that property. If $DF$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite everywhere,
then $F=\nabla\phi$ for a convex $\phi$, and convexity, $\phi(F(x))\ge\phi(x)+F(x)^{\top}(F(x)-x)$, gives for
$V(x)=\tfrac12\|x\|^2-\phi(x)$
\begin{equation}
V(F(x))\le V(x)-\tfrac12\|F(x)-x\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Along a cycle these decreases sum to zero, so every point of the cycle is fixed. $F$ maps every state into the
convex hull of the embeddings, where $V$ is bounded below, so the steps of every orbit tend to zero and its limit
points are fixed points: such a map has neither cycles nor chaos. Every cycle and every chaotic cell in this paper
therefore depends on the network factor $J_{\tilde h}$ breaking that structure. Attention has itself been read as a Hopfield retrieval \cite{ramsauer2020hopfield}; in the soft-token
map the retrieval closes a loop around the whole network.

\input{figures/fig-readout-gain}
\paragraph{High-temperature contraction and the greedy limit.} The largest Lyapunov exponent of an orbit cannot exceed the orbit
average of $\log\|DF\|$, so chaos needs $\|DF\|>1$ on a substantial part of the attractor. Both ends of the
temperature axis work against this, in different ways (Figure~\ref{fig:readoutgain}). As $T\to0$, $p$ becomes one-hot except near ties between the
two leading tokens; away from ties the gain $\|\Sigma_p\|/T$ decays like $e^{-\Delta/T}/T$, where $\Delta$ is the gap
between the two largest logits, and $F$ tends to the piecewise-constant greedy map, every orbit of which is eventually
periodic (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}). This limit is pointwise, not uniform: near a tie the gain is of order $1/T$, so at
small positive temperature chaos is excluded only on invariant sets that keep a uniform logit gap, and a chaotic set
would have to return to near-ties again and again.
As $T\to\infty$, $p$ tends to the uniform distribution and $\|\Sigma_p\|$ stays bounded, while $J_{\tilde h}$ is
bounded on the convex hull of the embeddings, which contains every soft token. For this smooth map, with a fixed
prefix, fixed steering and a network whose derivative is bounded on that compact hull, $F$ is therefore a contraction
for large enough $T$, with a single attracting fixed point that tends to the mean embedding: here the exclusion is a
bound. It does not cover hard routing or a growing context. Away from near-ties, chaos thus needs temperatures at
which the next-token distribution is spread enough to make $\Sigma_p$ large while $1/T$ still amplifies it. The
unsteered sweeps agree (Figure~\ref{fig:alpha0}). In the four long sweeps the chaotic temperatures lie in one band,
interleaved with periodic windows, and every band lies between $\log_{10}T=-0.20$ and $0.28$. Below the bands, all
1547 temperatures swept have negative exponents, and all but five have a detected period; above them, up to the end
of the sweeps at $\log_{10}T=0.3$, 127 of 128 temperatures have a detected period (fixed points at 101) and one has a
negative exponent without one. These are finite sweep findings; the wider sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:size} find chaos
at higher temperatures in some models and, in OLMo-2-32B, three candidate cells below one nat of entropy (at $\log_{10}T=-0.42$, $-0.41$ and $-0.07$; one twin
offset). A further sweep of the unsteered 0.5B map from
$\log_{10}T=0.3$ to $1.5$ finds an attracting fixed point at all 25 temperatures. Its entropy approaches $\log V$
(11.928 against 11.931 nats at $\log_{10}T=1.5$), and its exponent falls steadily, by 2.14 per decade of temperature
at the top of the range, toward the slope $-\ln10\approx-2.30$ expected once $p$ is uniform.

\paragraph{Stability belongs to the loop, not to the step.} The gain condition is necessary, not sufficient. Along
the period-8 orbit of Table~\ref{tab:orbits}, the largest singular value of $DF$ lies between 1.2 and 21 at every
step, so every step expands some direction, and no step is close to rank one: the second singular value is at least
0.15 of the first, and the second eigenvalue of $\Sigma_p$ is at least 0.23 of the first (minimum 0.236). Yet the Jacobian of the whole
cycle, the product of the eight step Jacobians, has norm between 0.059 and 0.644, depending on the phase at which the
cycle is started, against $1.1\times10^{7}$ for the product of the step norms. Its eigenvalues, which do not depend
on that phase, reproduce the multipliers of Table~\ref{tab:orbits} ($0.01104$ and $-2.96\times10^{-7}$; the table
lists moduli), and its second singular value is $1.7\times10^{-6}$ to $2\times10^{-4}$ of its first. An independent
implementation of the model reproduced the factorization (to $8.3\times10^{-14}$), the step singular values, the
signed multipliers and the cycle norms at all eight starting phases. The expansion of each step is undone by the
steps that follow, and the strongly anisotropic linear return of the stripe orbits is a property of the composed
cycle, not of any single step or of the rank of $\Sigma_p$. Nor does the contraction depend on a special alignment of
the steps: with independent random (Haar) rotations inserted between the eight step Jacobians, all 16 products
contract, and more strongly than the actual cycle (\texttt{tools/alignment\_intervention.py}). Stepwise norm or
spectral summaries can be inconclusive; the ordered product of the step Jacobians captures the cycle's linear
stability.

\paragraph{Steering acts on the gain.} Steering enters both factors of \eqref{eq:gain}: it moves the logits, and
with them $\Sigma_p$, and it moves the hidden states, and with them $J_{\tilde h}$. The gain mechanism is not
specific to concept-labelled directions, although a direction's geometry affects both factors and can correlate with
its meaning. This is consistent with the controls of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, in which norm-matched random
directions also produce chaos in the 0.5B and 8B models; at 70B the directions differ (Section~\ref{sec:size}). What
a direction does select is which tokens dominate $p$, and therefore what the attractors write.

\paragraph{Contraction and expansion in the network.} Analyses of deep attention identify contracting tendencies:
pure self-attention collapses token representations toward a common vector with depth \cite{dong2021attention}, and
idealized attention dynamics form clusters \cite{geshkovski2023emergence}. Residual connections and MLPs counteract
the collapse \cite{dong2021attention}. Which tendency wins along an orbit is a property of the trained weights that
only computation settles. In every dense model we mapped, both outcomes occur: the same map has negative largest
exponents in some regions of its parameter plane and positive ones in others.

\paragraph{Why the structures of low-dimensional maps appear.} The map acts on a space of hundreds to thousands of
dimensions, yet two reductions make low-dimensional theory apply (Figure~\ref{fig:tworeductions}). Near a bifurcation of a periodic orbit the centre
manifold theorem reduces the dynamics exactly to the critical directions, whatever the size of the other multipliers,
as long as they stay off the unit circle. Our normal forms of folds, flips, cusps and 1:1 resonances rest on this
reduction alone. Global statements, that a whole attractor follows a scalar return map, need more: the other
multipliers must be small. Near the periodic orbits we compute they are, from a few times $10^{-2}$ at short periods
to below $10^{-13}$ along the cascade (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism} to~\ref{sec:cascade}), and the reduction improves
with period: along the cascade at $\log_{10}T=-0.156$ the next modulus falls from $0.12$ at the first flip to
$0.045$, $7\times10^{-5}$, $4\times10^{-7}$ and $2\times10^{-13}$ at the next four. Temperature,
through the gain of \eqref{eq:gain}, and steering unfold the reduced map as a two-parameter family. Generic
two-parameter families of smooth one-dimensional maps with a quadratic extremum have universal features: fold and flip
curves with cusps where fold curves turn, period-doubling cascades whose interval ratios tend to Feigenbaum's
$\delta$, and saddle-node windows that accumulate on a fold with $d_N\propto N^{-2}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} sketches the
objects we locate). The organization of shrimps needs
more, either a second turning point of the reduced map or a second dimension
\cite{gallas1993structure,jones2012rossler}. We test the consequences where we state them: the multipliers of each
orbit, the interval ratios, and the normal-form predictions of passage times and coexisting orbits. Where the second
multiplier is not small, as in the high-temperature bubble of Section~\ref{sec:cascade} (next moduli $0.28$ and
$0.38$), the global reduction is only a rough guide, while the local normal forms still apply.

\paragraph{Coexisting attractors mark the limits of one dimension.} A classical theorem says what a one-dimensional
reduction cannot do. If an interval map is three times differentiable, unimodal and has negative Schwarzian derivative,
$Sf=f'''/f'-\tfrac32(f''/f')^2<0$, the immediate basin of every attracting periodic orbit contains the turning point or
an endpoint of the interval, so the map has at most one attracting periodic orbit in the interior
\cite{singer1978stable}. The soft-token map violates this conclusion in several places. Near each located cusp with
$c<0$, two attracting orbits of the same period coexist with a saddle between them (periods 4 and 6,
Section~\ref{sec:curves}; period 7, Section~\ref{sec:hostility}). Inside the hostile period-7 body, the period-7 loop
coexists with a fixed point, which the fixed start reaches at isolated cells. Wherever two attractors coexist, the
effective one-dimensional dynamics cannot be a single unimodal map with negative Schwarzian derivative: it needs a
second turning point, a stretch of positive Schwarzian derivative, or a second dimension. A second turning point is
also what a doubly superstable shrimp centre requires.

\paragraph{How many dimensions does the chaos have?} For chaotic attractors the corresponding measure is the
Kaplan--Yorke dimension \cite{kaplan1979chaotic}, $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=j+\sum_{i\le j}\lambda_i/|\lambda_{j+1}|$ with $j$ the
largest index at which the partial sum of the ordered Lyapunov exponents is still nonnegative; with one positive
exponent and $\lambda_1+\lambda_2<0$, $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=1+\lambda_1/|\lambda_2|$. We computed the four leading exponents
by QR iteration (Section~\ref{sec:methods}; 1000 transient and 2000 measured steps, float64) at chaotic cells chosen
by percentile of their largest exponent, before any of their spectra were seen. Every attractor we measured has
exactly one positive exponent, so none is hyperchaotic, but the dimension grows with temperature. Along the hostile
temperature line, where we fit a scalar return relation (Section~\ref{sec:hostility}), eight chaotic cells have
$D_{\mathrm{KY}}=1.008$ to $1.16$: one-dimensional to within a sixth of a dimension. At fourteen chaotic cells of the
headline maps ($\log_{10}T$ from $-0.17$ to $-0.04$) it is $1.04$ to $1.65$, rising with $\lambda_1$. In the
high-temperature region ($\log_{10}T$ from $-0.02$ to $0.13$), nine chaotic cells have $D_{\mathrm{KY}}=1.37$ to
$2.02$; at three of them $\lambda_1+\lambda_2\ge0$, and the attractor is effectively two-dimensional. A scalar return
map describes the low-temperature chaos well and the high-temperature chaos poorly, consistent with the large second
multipliers of the high-temperature bubble.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{Precision.} Every result on the soft-token map labelled float64 is computed in float64 arithmetic
throughout: embeddings, activations, attention keys and values (the prefix cache), normalizations (patched, since the
library computes RMS normalization in float32 by default), rotary tables, logits, the softmax and the fed-back token.
The released checkpoints store their weights in bfloat16, and a bfloat16 number is exactly a float64 number, so
converting the weights is exact. For large models the decoder's linear weights stay in their stored bfloat16 and are
converted to float64 at each use, which gives the same map as converting them once (bitwise on Qwen2.5-0.5B). No
activation or result is stored below float64. The one exception we found, the final normalization of the two-GPU
runs, is described in Section~\ref{sec:size}.

\paragraph{Phase maps.} Each cell of a phase map is one parameter pair. Unless stated otherwise, we discard 300
transient steps and measure over the next 300. The largest Lyapunov exponent comes from a twin trajectory at
offset $10^{-6}$ that is renormalized to that offset after every step, Benettin's method with a finite-difference
tangent \cite{benettin1980lyapunovI,benettin1980lyapunovII}; it estimates over a finite horizon the exponent that the
multiplicative ergodic theorem defines as a limit \cite{oseledets1968multiplicative}. At the 448 random cells of the
horizon check (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), an exact tangent vector carried by forward-mode differentiation
reproduces the twin exponents at offsets $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$ within 0.0041 (median 0.0004), with the same 178
chaotic labels, and at six chaotic cells the four leading exponents from QR iteration include exactly one positive one
(0.06 to 0.42; the next is $-0.64$ to $-1.10$). The period is the smallest $q\le32$ (48 in the later zooms) with return distance below
$10^{-8}$, and the attractor is identified by its leading-token loop, up to rotation. We call a cell chaotic when this
finite-horizon exponent exceeds $10^{-3}$ and no period is found; the label is a finite-horizon diagnostic, not a
proof of chaos. Panels coloured by period or attractor text follow an older drawing convention: black marks every
cell whose exponent exceeds $0.005$, and light grey the other cells without a period. The drawn black area differs
from the chaotic count in at most $0.42\,\%$ of a map's cells. A $256^2$ map takes about 100 minutes on one H100 GPU in float64. Every cell starts from the same
state, so where attractors coexist, a map shows the one whose basin contains that state. Labels are text, not
dynamical identities: one continuous orbit changes its label where two tokens tie for the maximum. We therefore call
two labelled regions different attractors only when the contraction rate jumps between them or when float64 orbits of
both exist at one parameter value.

\paragraph{Periodic orbits.} We solve $F^p(x)=x$ by Newton's method with forward-mode Jacobians. The multipliers are
the eigenvalues of the monodromy matrix; where the dense matrix is not float64, we refine them by a short Arnoldi
factorization with float64 Jacobian-vector products.

\paragraph{Continuation.} Periodic branches are continued in one parameter by pseudo-arclength continuation, and fold
and flip curves in two parameters with minimally augmented systems \cite{govaerts2000numerical,kuznetsov2023elements}.
The test function $g$ of the bordered system $[[M\mp I,v],[w^\top,0]]$ is obtained by GMRES (generalized minimal
residual iterations) with Jacobian-vector products, and its gradient by reverse-mode differentiation through a
Jacobian-vector product. When the parameter tangent reverses, we flag a cusp candidate and test it with the normal
form. To locate a codimension-two point on a continued curve (\texttt{tools/cusp\_locate.py}), we bracket a sign
change between two curve points, then retrace the curve from the left end with steps of a quarter of the bracket.
We do this for three rounds and finish with a secant step. The function whose sign changes is the oriented quadratic
coefficient $b\operatorname{sign}a$ for a cusp, $|\mu_2|-1$ for a second multiplier $\mu_2$ leaving the unit
circle, and the projected parameter tangent for a turn of the curve. At a cusp we then evaluate $c$ and the unfolding
matrix at the located point. Elsewhere, the multipliers and the angle between their eigenvectors type the point: a
double multiplier whose eigenvectors coincide is a Jordan block. On maps with known answers the tool recovers a
cusp to $10^{-6}$ (with $c=-1$ and $\det J=1$) and a fold-flip point.

\paragraph{Normal form and passages.} Consider a fold of the two-step map $G=F^2$, with right and left eigenvectors
$r$ and $\ell$ normalized by $\|r\|=1$ and $\ell^\top r=1$. Along the critical direction the reduced map is
$u'=u+a\eps+bu^2$, with $a=\ell^\top\partial_\alpha G$ and $b=\tfrac12\ell^\top D^2G[r,r]$. We compute $b$ by
central differences of float64 Jacobian-vector products over a step-size plateau (later cross-checks use nested
automatic differentiation), and $a$ includes the steering's effect on the prefix. The signed coefficients are kept in
the original eigenvector gauge, with $\eps$ measured in the free parameter ($\alpha$ or $\log_{10}T$, whose units
apply to $a$). On maps with known normal forms the code recovers $ab$, the cubic coefficient $c$ with its
centre-manifold term and the two-parameter unfolding matrix, and it traces the analytic fold and flip curves of the
H\'enon map (\texttt{tests/test\_bifurcation\_curves.py}). For passages we set $v=\operatorname{sign}(b)\,u$ and take the ghost side
$p-p_c=\operatorname{sign}(ab)\,\epsilon$ with $\epsilon>0$, so that $v'=v+|a|\epsilon+|b|v^2$ to quadratic order. A
full passage starts at $v=-U$ and a midpoint passage at $v=0$; both end at the first strict crossing $v>U$, with
$U=0.1/|b|$. We compare passage counts with the iterated quadratic map and with the arctangent formula
\cite{pomeau1980intermittent}.

\paragraph{Period-doubling cascade.} Along a cut in one parameter, we locate the flip of each period-$p$ orbit as the
root of $\operatorname{Re}\mu_c+1$, where $\mu_c$ is the multiplier with the smallest real part, using the Illinois
variant of regula falsi with warm-started orbits. All but one or two multipliers are small, so Newton's method on
$F^p(x)-x$ needs the monodromy only on a six-dimensional dominant subspace. We obtain that subspace by subspace
iteration with exact forward-mode Jacobian-vector products chained along the orbit, so no dense Jacobian is formed,
and the residual is always the exact float64 $F^p(x)-x$. Each root is classified before it is accepted. The critical
multiplier must be real and equal to $-1$, with a Ritz residual below $10^{-6}$, and the other computed Ritz values
(six in all, estimates of the dominant spectrum) must lie inside the unit circle. We then reach the period-$2p$ orbit
by forward iteration inside the next band. On the logistic map embedded in six dimensions, the procedure reproduces
the first four flip parameters to $10^{-9}$.

\section{Mechanism: a saddle-node bottleneck}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\paragraph{The fold.} At $\log_{10}T=-0.115$ the 2-cycle ``is it'' dies in a fold at $\alpha_c=0.06279827$ (float64
multiplier $1$ to $3\times10^{-13}$, next modulus $0.037$). The normal form of $G$ has $a=-4.834$ per unit $\alpha$
and $b=-25.4285$, so $ab=122.93$. The stable branch gives an independent estimate: extrapolating its multipliers
through $(1-\mu)^2/(4(\alpha_c-\alpha))\to ab$ yields $123.1$ with a quadratic fit ($120.6$ with a linear one). An
independent implementation of the forward map and solver recovers $\alpha_c$ to $6\times10^{-15}$ and $a$ to
$3\times10^{-12}$. Its $b$, from nested automatic differentiation, is $-25.42846$; our finite-difference value
differs from it by $2.2\times10^{-6}$ relatively, which is the finite-step bias of the difference table. The
resulting $ab=122.927$ leaves every passage prediction of Table~\ref{tab:passages} unchanged.

the bottleneck's middle ($v\approx-3\times10^{-4}$ to $-7\times10^{-4}$, in the same oriented coordinate). From each measured entry point, the
normal form predicts the natural laminar durations within 4--8\% for $\eps\le10^{-4}$, and a natural passage lasts
about 0.6 of a full one. The stripes therefore follow the $N^{-2}$ law of a saddle-node ghost, with about a third of
the full-passage prefactor. Stripe centres located on a float32 cut along $\log_{10}T=-0.115$ give

\section{Bifurcation curves and cusps}
\label{sec:curves}
\paragraph{Period-8 stripe.} Continued for 80 points with a float64 test function, the fold curve of the period-8
orbit keeps its critical multiplier within $1\pm6\times10^{-6}$, follows the stripe's upper edge and caps its top near
$\log_{10}T=-0.065$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stripes}). At $\alpha=0.0717$ the stripe lies between a flip at
$\log_{10}T=-0.128955$ and the fold at $-0.1033462$. Continued downward, the flip curve keeps its multiplier at $-1$
for 115 points to $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0776,-0.402)$, with two reversals of its parameter tangent near
$(0.0750,-0.219)$ and $(0.0746,-0.206)$, and the fold curve keeps its multiplier at $+1$ for 120 points to
$(0.0758,-0.200)$. Located by bisection along the curve (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), the two reversals of the flip
curve, at $(0.074955,-0.219336)$ and $(0.074608,-0.206007)$, carry no second multiplier near the unit circle (the
next ones are $3\times10^{-7}$ and $1\times10^{-5}$). On a flip curve the parameter tangent can vanish only where a
second multiplier reaches $+1$, so both are tight but smooth turns of the curve in the parameter plane, not
codimension-two points.

\paragraph{The accumulation boundary.} The fold curve of the 2-cycle ``is it'', continued from
$(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0628,-0.115)$ in both directions (38 and 126 points, critical multiplier $1$ to $10^{-7}$, no
cusp), traces the boundary of the period-2 region in Figures~\ref{fig:stripes} and~\ref{fig:cross512}. The stripe fan
accumulates on this boundary, as the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} requires. Further along one direction,
outside the plotted window, another multiplier leaves the unit circle (moduli up to 11). Located along the curve,
this second real multiplier reaches $+1$ at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.035628,-0.341724)$, where the eigenvectors of
the two multipliers at $+1$ coincide to $3\times10^{-4}$ degrees: a double multiplier $+1$ with a Jordan block,
the 1:1 resonance of maps (the analogue of a Bogdanov--Takens point).

\paragraph{A period-6 window with shrimp-like anatomy.} The hooks of Figure~\ref{fig:zooms} (bottom) belong to
the period-6 loop ``. -\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe is a''. Float64 orbits (Newton residuals
$10^{-15}$--$10^{-13}$) show that its curved body and its thin leg are one window: the multipliers are $-0.061$ and
$+0.048$ at the body, $-0.564$ lower down and $-0.645$ on the leg. The foot is its period-doubled loop (period 12,
leading multiplier $-0.156$). Inside the body lies a different 6-cycle, ``.The\textbackslash n\textbackslash nThe is
a'', whose leading multipliers are a complex pair, $-0.031\pm0.152i$. One-parameter branches from the body end in
folds in every direction: at $\log_{10}T=-0.0558$ and $-0.0721$ in temperature, at $\alpha=0.08074$ in steering, and,
toward smaller $\alpha$, in two folds only $10^{-5}$ apart. Such a close pair is the signature of a nearby cusp. A
flip at $\alpha\approx0.0797$ then opens the period-12 foot. The body is not effectively one-dimensional either. At two body cells the leading
multiplier is a complex pair, $-0.020\pm0.264i$, or has a second multiplier of comparable size ($-0.261$ against
$0.099$). Below the body, where the window's strip appears to cross a period-5 strip near
$(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0773,-0.0774)$, both orbits continued from their own strips are unstable at the crossing
(leading multipliers $-1.88$ and $-6.59$). The crossing is therefore neither a centre nor a point where two attractors
coexist.

Fold curves that bound a window and meet in cusps, with flip curves inside, form the codimension-two organization of
a shrimp \cite{facanha2013endoskeletons} (complex dynamics of small recurrent networks has been studied in the same
spirit \cite{pasemann2002complex}). In the continued curves (Figure~\ref{fig:z3curves}), a single fold arc bounds the
body on its outer side and a flip arc on its inner side, and the leg lies between them where they converge. The outer
fold arc extends far to the left as a thin arm and ends in a cusp at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.069106,-0.039750)$.
The fold curve through the close fold pair has a second cusp inside the body, at $(0.080097,-0.058344)$, from
which two fold branches descend.

At both reversals the quadratic coefficient $b$ changes sign between consecutive curve points while $a$ stays away
from zero (Table~\ref{tab:z3cusps}).
Because the signs of $a$ and $b$ both flip with the orientation of the critical eigenvector, the test uses
$b\operatorname{sign}a$. A cusp is nondegenerate when the cubic coefficient $c$ of the normal form
$u'=u+a\varepsilon+bu^2+cu^3$ is nonzero where $b$ vanishes. We compute $c$ with the second-order centre-manifold
term, $c=\ell^\top C(r,r,r)/6+\ell^\top B(r,h_2)/2$ with $(I-M)h_2=B(r,r)-2br$ and $\ell^\top h_2=0$, where $M$ is
the monodromy matrix and $B$ and $C$ are the second and third derivatives of its map, obtained by central differences
of float64 Jacobian-vector products. (The code reproduces $c$ to $10^{-6}$ on a map with a known reduction.) At the two
curve points that bracket each sign change, $c$ is nonzero far beyond its step-size uncertainty and has the same sign,
and $b$ changes sign with nonzero slope between them. This supports a nondegenerate cusp inside each bracket. Located along each fold curve (Section~\ref{sec:methods}),
$b\operatorname{sign}a$ falls to below $5\times10^{-6}$ of its bracket values at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.069106,-0.039750)$
on the outer arc and at $(0.080097,-0.058344)$ inside the body. At these same points $c=-1.51\times10^{4}$ and
$-2.38\times10^{5}$, and the unfolding matrix $J$ defined below is regular ($\det J=2416$ and $14.0$): two
numerically located cusps. The
negative sign of $c$ means that near each cusp, two stable period-6 orbits coexist with a saddle between the fold
branches.

A generic codimension-two cusp also needs the two parameters to unfold it independently. Near the cusp the reduced
map is $u'=u+\beta_1+\beta_2u+bu^2+cu^3$, where $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ vanish at the cusp and depend on the
parameters. The unfolding is regular when $J=\partial(\beta_1,\beta_2)/\partial(\log_{10}T,10\alpha)$ has rank two
($\det J\neq0$). We first estimated $J$ at a fold point near the outer-arc cusp (where $b\operatorname{sign}a=14.7$):
$\det J=2737$ (singular values $1630$ and $1.7$), so the estimated unfolding is nonsingular there, as it is at the
located cusp ($\det J=2416$). With these derivatives, the local cubic model predicted the number of period-6 orbits and their
stability at six parameter offsets, written to disk before any orbit was solved. Newton solves started from the
predicted roots and from the base state recovered the predicted number of distinct orbits, with the predicted
stability, at all six. The search was seeded, not exhaustive: orbits that these starts do not reach would be missed.

\section{A period-doubling cascade to chaos}
\label{sec:cascade}
The crescent of Figure~\ref{fig:cross512} is a closed region in which the period-3 loop ``\textbackslash
n\textbackslash nThe existence'' doubles inward. Along the cut $\log_{10}T=-0.14798$, the map's cells show period 3
over 25 columns, period 6 over about 14, period 12 over 4 and period 24 in one column, followed by a chaotic core. The
doubled loops repeat the same leading tokens twice and four times, with alternating probabilities. Locating the flips
along this cut in float64 (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), in the steering coordinate $\alpha$, gives
Table~\ref{tab:cascade}. The first two ratios, $\delta_2=2.844$ and $\delta_3=2.859$ (indexed as in
Table~\ref{tab:cascade}), are far from Feigenbaum's $4.6692$, even though from the second flip on the orbits are
effectively one-dimensional (next modulus $0.0023$, $2\times10^{-6}$, $2\times10^{-12}$). The next ratio,
$\delta_4=3.781$, is markedly larger. The first four flip parameters agree between completed float64 computations on a
CPU and an H100 to $4\times10^{-15}$.

Continuing a just-born doubled orbit past period 48 failed: Newton's method slides onto the half-period orbit, which
is also periodic with the doubled period. We therefore also located the superstable parameters $s_p$, where the
critical multiplier vanishes in the middle of each band (Table~\ref{tab:superstable}). There the orbit attracts, so it
can be reached by forward iteration and cannot slide onto its half-period parent. Through period 384 the superstable
ratios are $3.19$, $4.02$, $4.478$ and $4.625$. The last two come from parameters resolved to $10^{-14}$ and differ
from Feigenbaum's value by $0.19$ and $0.044$; from $4.02$ on, the distance shrinks by factors of $3.4$ and $4.3$.
The flip ratios $2.84$, $2.86$ and $3.78$ rise in step (Figure~\ref{fig:feigenbaum}), and the accumulation point lies
near $\alpha_\infty\approx0.0661250$. The early superstable values are less precise. The other computed Ritz values of
the period-12 and period-24 orbits blur the root, so $s_{12}$ and $s_{24}$ are known only within brackets of
$1.2\times10^{-5}$ and $7\times10^{-7}$, and the ratios $3.19$ and $4.02$ have worst-case ranges $[2.90,3.34]$ and
$[4.02,4.10]$. $s_6$ is less precise still, and $s_3$ was not bracketed.

Why are the early ratios so small? We first considered a crossover. Softmax at temperature $T$ is a smoothed argmax,
so the reduced one-dimensional map might have a nearly corner-like extremum, rounded only on a scale set by $T$. Early
doublings would then see the corner, where ratios tend toward 2, and later ones the smooth quadratic core. This
predicts smaller early ratios at lower temperature. We fixed that prediction before locating the flips along two
lower cuts, and it fails. At $\log_{10}T=-0.156$ the first ratio is $14.93$, five times its value at $-0.148$
(Table~\ref{tab:cascade156}). The cut at $-0.152$ crosses a doubling bubble: its period-12 orbit merges back into the
period-6 orbit near $\alpha\approx0.0657$, so that cut has no complete cascade. The early ratios thus depend strongly
on where a cut crosses the crescent, and we withdraw the crossover explanation. The later ratios at $-0.156$ behave as
universality requires. The next ratios are $3.124$, $4.4988$, $4.6208$, $4.6618$ and $4.6673$. Their deviations from

by a factor of 3.5 to 6.5. The last flip, of the period-384 orbit, lies at the precision floor of its multiplier, a
product of 384 step Jacobians. Near the root the computed $\mu+1$ scatters by $1.8\times10^{-5}$ about a straight line
in $\alpha$, which places the flip within $\pm2.3\times10^{-13}$ and the ratio between $4.6672$ and $4.6675$. The
superstable points along the same cut give the ratios $10.21$, $3.713$, $4.572$ and $4.643$. Before computing them we
fixed the prediction that the last two superstable ratios at a new temperature would lie within 5\,\% of $\delta$.
Both do. Over the corners of the brackets the second lies in $[4.639,4.647]$, limited by $s_{48}$ (a bracket
$2.4\times10^{-9}$ wide; $s_{96}$ and $s_{192}$ are resolved to $5\times10^{-15}$). The first depends on $s_{24}$, which is known only within a bracket of $5.9\times10^{-6}$
(worst-case range $[1.01,4.65]$).

\section{Bridge to sampled decoding}
\label{sec:bridge}
How much of this survives sampling? To find out, we replace the expected embedding by the mean of $m$ sampled token
embeddings (Figure~\ref{fig:bridge}). With the exact map, laminar phases near the fold last 120 and 63 steps at
$\eps=10^{-5}$ and $3\times10^{-5}$. With $m=64$ they last about 10.6 steps, independently of $\eps$ (57\% of steps in
the channel), and with $m=8$ about 7.7 steps (38\%). With single samples ($m=1$, sampled decoding of the same
truncated system), essentially no laminar phases remain (11\% of steps in the channel). The deterministic
intermittency mechanism therefore belongs to the expected-embedding map. Sampling noise turns the bottleneck into a
noise-limited trap whose strength grows with $m$.

\paragraph{Zero-temperature limit and ordinary greedy decoding.} As $T\to0$ the soft-token map becomes greedy
decoding with a one-token memory, $g(t)=\arg\max_s z_s(\text{prefix},E_t)$. This is a map on the vocabulary, and its
cycles are the zero-temperature attractors. For Qwen2.5-0.5B we computed $g$ for all 151\,936 tokens. Unsteered, it
has 1060 cycles, and one of them, the period-5 loop ``![](https:// Hello'', drains 86\,\% of the vocabulary; it is
the largest attractor family of every 0.5B soft-token map. Ordinary greedy decoding with a growing context does not
inherit these cycles (Table~\ref{tab:memory}). From 220 starts (the first tokens of the 20 largest cycles and 200
random tokens), 19 runs end in a short loop within 256 tokens, and the dominant 5-cycle never appears. Steering
along philosophy makes ordinary decoding loop almost always (218 of 220 runs at $\alpha=0.1$), but in other loops,
mostly punctuation such as `` --'', none of them one-token cycles. Greedy decoding that keeps only the last $K$
tokens interpolates between the two. For every $K\ge2$ the loops leave the one-token cycles. Unsteered, looping
collapses to the level of the growing context between $K=16$ and $32$; steered, the share of runs whose loop equals
the growing-context loop rises from 0 at $K=1$ to 58\,\% at $K=64$. The soft-token map's low-temperature attractors
are therefore those of decoding with a one-token memory. Which loop ordinary decoding enters depends on the context
it keeps, but the tendency of steering to drive decoding into loops is shared. In ordinary decoding, repetition is
known to reinforce itself \cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}, a theoretical analysis ties loops to words of
high inflow \cite{fu2021repetition}, and induction heads, which copy
earlier context \cite{olsson2022induction}, are one mechanism by which a longer context can favour loops that repeat
earlier text.

\paragraph{Soft thinking with a growing context.} Soft Thinking \cite{zhang2025softthinking} feeds back the same
expected embedding but appends it to the context, so that every earlier soft token stays visible. We ran that
decoding in float64 from the paper's prompt and displaced the first soft token of twin trajectories by $10^{-10}$,
without renormalization; the distance between the newest soft tokens then shows whether the early perturbation grows.
The context grows, so these are finite-time growth rates of a non-autonomous system, not exponents of an attractor.
In Qwen2.5-7B the perturbation grows exponentially at temperatures inside the one-token map's chaotic range: by 0.77
to 0.82 per step at $\log_{10}T=0.17$ and $0.21$, reaching $10^{-3}$ within 49 to 58 steps, and it reaches $10^{-3}$
within 400 steps at nine other temperatures between $-0.10$ and $0.40$, at 0.02 to 0.18 per step (0.8 at $0.33$ for two
of three twins). It does not grow at the sharpest distributions
($\log_{10}T\le-0.14$) or at nearly uniform ones ($\log_{10}T\ge0.44$), where it stays between $10^{-13}$ and
$10^{-7}$ for 400 steps. In Qwen2.5-0.5B the growth moves to sharper distributions: it appears at six of the seven temperatures from
$\log_{10}T=-0.30$ to $-0.07$ (up to 0.33 per step) and at $0.09$, with a late transient burst at $0.05$, and is absent
from $0.13$ up, which includes its narrow one-token chaotic band. Sensitive
dependence therefore survives when the whole context is kept, and in the 7B model the one-token map locates it.
Qwen2.5-72B, run the same way on two GPUs, stays nearly insensitive. We count a temperature as growing when two of its three
twins reach $10^{-3}$ within 400 steps, a rule fixed before the 72B run that gives 11 of 24 temperatures for the 7B
model and 8 for the 0.5B model. The 72B model grows at one of 24 temperatures on the paper's prompt, and only
transiently: at $\log_{10}T=0.09$ the twins pass $10^{-3}$ at step 123 and fall back to $5\times10^{-6}$ (at $0.13$ one twin of three passes
$10^{-3}$ at step 381). It grows at
none of 24 on the held-out prompt with its narrow unsteered band, including $\log_{10}T=0.326$, between two temperatures
where its one-token map is chaotic; there the distance never exceeds $1.1\times10^{-9}$. It also grows at none of the
eleven steered cells of Section~\ref{sec:size}, including the six where its one-token map is chaotic: there the
distance never exceeds $3\times10^{-7}$ and ends between $1\times10^{-13}$ and $6\times10^{-10}$, and the growing
context holds the next-token entropy at 2.3 to 3.6 nats instead of the one-token map's 7.3 to 11.1. At 72B the growing context therefore damps even
the chaos that steering produces, while the one transient burst shows that the method registers growth at this
scale.

\paragraph{The fold law at 1.5B and 7B.} We tested whether the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, windows
accumulating on the fold of a short cycle with $d_N=C/(N+\delta)^2$, recurs in larger models. The criterion, a
constant $d_N(N+\delta)^2$ within 10\,\% over at least four windows for one offset $\delta$, was fixed before the lines
were computed. In Qwen2.5-1.5B (philosophy direction, layer 14, $\alpha=0.058588$) the period-4 orbit folds at
$\log_{10}T_c=-0.0912029$ (multiplier $1$ to $3\times10^{-14}$, next modulus $0.008$), with normal-form coefficients
$a=-7.16$ and $b=-21.09$ per unit $\log_{10}T$, so $ab=151.0$. A float64 line of 1700 cells above the fold shows
windows of periods $4N$ accumulating on it, from single cells at periods 44--64 to a wide period-12 window. Measured
from $T_c$ to their edges nearest the fold, the four nearest resolved windows (periods 28--40) meet the criterion
within $0.12\,\%$ ($\delta=-0.99$, $C=0.0612$). With the windows farther out the spread rises to $10.3$--$14.0\,\%$;
there each period appears as two runs on the line's $10^{-5}$ grid, and grid-spaced edges no longer identify the fold
edges. In Qwen2.5-7B ($\alpha=0.051429$) the period-2 orbit folds at $\log_{10}T_c=-0.2040156$ (next modulus $0.009$,
$ab=55.7$), and odd windows accumulate on it. The nearest four and five (periods 11--17 and 9--17) meet the criterion
within $4.5\,\%$, and six (to period 7) within $6.1\,\%$. Each window's own doubling or tripling lies on its far side,
as for the 0.5B windows of periods 16--20, so these edges are fold edges. The effective passage fraction
$f=\sqrt{Cab}/\pi$ is $0.97$ at 1.5B and $0.78$--$0.80$ at 7B, against $0.58$ at 0.5B. The fold and its window law
thus recur at all three sizes, while $f$, which the global reinjection sets, differs between the models. With one
fold per model, crossed in different coordinates ($\alpha$ at 0.5B, $\log_{10}T$ at 1.5B and 7B), no trend of $ab$
with size can be read.

\paragraph{A mixture-of-experts model.} Qwen3.5-35B-A3B differs from the dense models in two ways. Thirty of its 40
layers use linear (Gated DeltaNet) attention, with gated full attention in every fourth layer, and each layer routes
a token to 8 of 256 experts plus a shared expert, so that about 3B of its 35B parameters are active. Hugging Face
computes several parts of this architecture in float32 even when the model is cast to float64 (the normalizations,
the delta-rule recurrences, the rotary tables and the router softmax), and some tensors are stored in float32; we
patch these paths, restore the stored tensors exactly, and disable the thinking block that the default chat template
opens (a first run with the block open is kept in the research log). Steered at layer 20, the map behaves unlike
those of the dense models. Unsteered, 128 temperatures over $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$ give no positive exponent (largest
$-0.265$): periodic loops at all but 15 temperatures (``!Hello'' with period 2 at 92 of them, other periods up to 52),
and the remaining 15, above $\log_{10}T\approx0.1$, contract without a detected period. Along philosophy, none of 1024
cells is chaotic (largest exponent $-0.112$). Along hostility, 52 of 1024 cells ($5.1\,\%$) have a positive exponent
and no period, against $28\,\%$ for Llama-3.1 8B on the same plane. Routing is discontinuous: a twin trajectory that
crosses a routing boundary jumps, which can inflate a finite-difference exponent, so we call these cells irregular
rather than chaotic. Whether the contraction comes from the experts or from the Qwen3.5 architecture would take the
dense Qwen3.5-9B as a control, which we have not run.

\section{Chaos and model size}
\label{sec:size}
Is the chaos of the loop a small-model effect that larger models leave behind? We measured the unsteered map of
fifteen models: Qwen2.5 at seven sizes from 0.5B to 72B parameters, OLMo-2 at 1B, 7B, 13B and 32B, Llama-3.1 at 8B
and 70B, Llama-3.3-70B, and the mixture of experts Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, all with the paper's prefix and the settings of
Section~\ref{sec:methods} (1000 transient and 2000 measured steps in float64; chaotic: a positive exponent and no
period). Every checkpoint is stored entirely in bfloat16 (checked against its safetensors metadata), and every map here is the
map of those stored values evaluated in float64 arithmetic; float64 does not make the weights more precise than
stored. The newer sweeps keep the decoder weights in bfloat16 and convert them on use, which gives the same map as
converting them once (bitwise on Qwen2.5-0.5B); the 70B-class models are spread over two GPUs. In those two-GPU runs the final
RMS normalization was computed in float32: the hooks that move tensors between the GPUs keep a layer's original code,
so our float64 version of that layer was bypassed. This makes the computed map rough at about $10^{-8}$ of the
state (its fixed points wander by $10^{-8}$, against $10^{-15}$ on one GPU) and matters only for exponents measured
with perturbations of $10^{-6}$ and below; we found and fixed it at the end of the study and say below which
results it affects. The 7--8B
and 70B-class models had shown a steep decline of chaos with size. Before measuring the others we fixed three
predictions that extrapolate it (\texttt{docs/scaling-predictions.md}): within each family, every model above 8B is
less chaotic than the family's 7--8B model (P1); the chaotic share does not increase with size above 7B (P2); and at
matched next-token entropy, both gain factors of Section~\ref{sec:theory} of the 14B and 32B models lie at or below
those of the 7B model (P3). After a review pointed out that a single prompt and a fixed temperature window could
mislead, we froze a fourth test across ten prompts (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-test.md}).

\paragraph{In a fixed temperature window, chaos seems to fade with size.} Table~\ref{tab:scale} and
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} give each model's chaotic share of the temperatures in $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$, the range
common to all sweeps, and the chaotic length of its whole temperature axis. In two families the largest models are
nearly free of chaos in this window. Qwen2.5-7B is chaotic at
12.7\,\% of the temperatures in the common range, and the 14B, 32B and 72B models at none. Llama-3.1-8B is chaotic at
19\,\%, Llama-3.1-70B, from the same release and training recipe, at 2.3\,\%, and Llama-3.3-70B, which differs from
Llama-3.1-70B only in post-training, at none. In the third family the order fails: OLMo-2-32B is the most chaotic
OLMo model, at 16.2\,\% against 6.2\,\% at 7B, 3.8\,\% at 1B and none at 13B. (The 32B model is a later release,
0325, than the 7B and 13B models, 1124, so within OLMo-2 size and release are confounded.) The frozen predictions P1
and P2 hold for Qwen2.5 and fail for OLMo-2. Below 7B we predicted no order, and none appears (Qwen2.5 0.5B, 1.5B and
3B: 1.4, 1.5 and 0\,\%).

\paragraph{Across prompts, the chaos moves to higher temperature instead.} The fixed window hides where larger models
are chaotic. The frozen prompt-blocked test compares Qwen2.5-7B and -14B on ten system prompts and openers (two for
development, eight held out), over $\log_{10}T\in[-0.4,0.9]$ (48 temperatures) and then over the temperature interval
where each model's next-token entropy rises most (24 temperatures), with twins at offsets $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-7}$; a
temperature counts as chaotic only when both exponents are positive (Figure~\ref{fig:prefixscale}). It predicted that
the 14B model would be less chaotic. Both predictions fail: on all eight held-out prompts the 14B model is chaotic at
more of the 48 temperatures (9 to 17, against 0 to 7 for the 7B), and its largest exponent is lower on only three.
Every chaotic temperature of the 14B model, on all ten prompts, lies between $\log_{10}T=0.36$ and $0.90$, at and
above the temperature where its next-token distribution spreads ($0.35$--$0.65$); the 7B model's chaos lies mostly
between $0.21$ and $0.43$, around its own, lower transition ($0.21$--$0.46$). On the paper's prompt the 14B model is
chaotic at 12 of the 48 temperatures, between $0.52$ and $0.90$; its sweep in Table~\ref{tab:scale}, which ends at
$0.6$, saw only the lower edge. Within Qwen2.5, then, the larger model is not less chaotic: its distribution stays
sharp up to higher temperature, and its chaos sits there. The decline in the fixed window is largely a shift. A
second frozen test (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-olmo.md}; stage 1 only, four held-out prompts) asked whether the OLMo-2
order survives other prompts. It does, with small counts: OLMo-2-32B is chaotic at more of the 48 temperatures than
OLMo-2-7B on three of the four prompts (4, 5, 3 and 0 against 3, 0, 1 and 3), as predicted, and its chaos again lies
somewhat higher ($\log_{10}T=0.10$--$0.35$ against $-0.01$--$0.21$).

\paragraph{What sets it: the gain of the loop.} The gain split of Section~\ref{sec:theory} shows which factor removes or
keeps the chaos (Figure~\ref{fig:gainscale}). We compare medians of the two factors in bins of the mean next-token
entropy on the orbit; the bins hold 1 to 17 of each model's 24 temperatures, so single medians are noisy. Within one
training recipe, from Llama-3.1-8B to Llama-3.1-70B, the readout factor changes by at most 0.3 per step in every bin
above one nat (below one nat the 8B model has a single temperature), while the network factor is lower by 0.5 to 1.3
per step between 1 and 10 nats (6--10 nats: median $-0.98$ from 3 temperatures against $+0.28$ from 4) and by only
0.18 above 10 nats: in these two checkpoints the larger model damps changes of its fed-back token. Llama-3.3-70B, which
differs from Llama-3.1-70B in post-training, has a much lower readout factor below 3 nats (1--3 nats: $-2.33$ from 7
temperatures against $-0.10$ from 6) and a similar one above 6 nats. In Qwen2.5, both factors of the 14B and 32B
models lie below the 7B's in every bin between 1 and 10 nats, as the frozen prediction P3 states (the 6--10 nat bin
only with the 7B's wider gain split, from one temperature), and both factors of
the 72B lie below the 7B's in every bin. Above 10 nats, where the distribution is nearly uniform, the 14B and 32B
models instead have the larger network factor ($+2.98$ and $+1.17$ against $+0.19$), and those are the bins of their
chaotic temperatures above $\log_{10}T=0.3$. Below 3 nats the readout factor dominates at 32B and 72B, because the
next-token distribution stays sharp up to high temperature (for the 72B, entropy at most 0.15 nats up to $T=2$). In
OLMo-2, P3 fails, and the readout factor tracks the chaos: OLMo-2-13B, with no chaotic temperature, has a lower
readout factor than OLMo-2-7B in all three bins between 1 and 10 nats ($-1.0$ to $-1.4$ against $+0.06$ to $-0.72$
per step) although its network factor is higher in two of them, whereas OLMo-2-32B, the most chaotic OLMo model, has a
higher readout factor than the 7B in all three ($+0.10$ to $+0.35$), positive as in the chaotic 7--8B models. These
are descriptive comparisons of trained checkpoints, not interventions on size or post-training: the split is a
decomposition along the evolving tangent direction, and equal entropy does not imply equal embedding covariance.
An intervention separates the two factors at 72B. If its loop were stable only because its distribution jumps from
sharp to flat, holding the distribution in between should destabilize it. We therefore choose each step's
temperature so that the next-token entropy equals a target and sweep the target instead of the temperature
(entropy-clamped decoding; the map stays deterministic, and the entropy holds to $10^{-14}$). The temperature then
depends on the state, $T=\tau(x)$ with $H(x,\tau(x))=H_\star$ for the entropy $H$, so the derivative of the clamped
map $\hat F(x)=F(x,\tau(x))$ gains a rank-one term, $D\hat F=D_xF-\partial_TF\,D_xH/\partial_TH$. We therefore take
its exponents from twins of the clamped map itself, temperature solve included, and use no fixed-temperature
derivative or gain split for it. The safeguarded solve is only piecewise smooth, but on these orbits one-step central
differences at $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$ agree with those at $10^{-4}$ within $6\times10^{-6}$. Held at any of 16 targets
between 1 and 11 nats, Qwen2.5-72B has only periodic orbits, the echo of period 2 on the paper's prompt and periods 1
to 19 on a held-out prompt, with twin exponents between $-0.08$ and $-4.5$ that agree within 0.004 across offsets
$10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$, whereas Qwen2.5-0.5B under the same clamp is chaotic at 6.1 nats. The 72B loop thus stays
stable even where its readout is spread, which points to damping by the network. Nor does post-training make the 72B
stable. A test frozen before
its data (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-72b-base.md}) predicted that the pretrained base model of the same size would be
chaotic at three or more temperatures more than Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct on at least three of the four held-out prompts.
It fails on all four: the base model is chaotic at 1, 0, 0 and 0 of the 48 temperatures (and at 2 on the paper's
prompt; computed after the precision fix, with 500 transient and 1000 measured steps, half the Instruct model's
horizon), although its next-token distribution spreads over 2.5 to 5.5 times the temperature range (intermediate-entropy
breadth 0.28 to 0.61 decades against 0.11 to 0.22) and from lower temperatures on. At 72B, then, neither the sharpness
that post-training adds nor post-training itself explains the stability. What remains is the network's damping of the
fed-back token, which the gain split shows at fixed temperature (the 72B's network factor lies below the 7B's in every
entropy bin), and the breadth that orders the fifteen models fails for this pair. The damping holds when the whole
context is kept as well: in Soft Thinking's growing context (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}), an early perturbation grows at
none of eleven steered cells, six of them chaotic as one-token maps, and at one of 48 unsteered temperatures on two
prompts, transiently.

\paragraph{Can a cheap property predict it?} Section~\ref{sec:theory} confines chaos to temperatures where the
readout is neither saturated nor uniform, so a model whose next-token distribution jumps from sharp to flat over a
small range of temperature leaves little room for it. The breadth of this intermediate regime, the decades of
$\log_{10}T$ in which the mean entropy on the orbit lies between 1 and 10 nats, ranges from 0.014 (Qwen2.5-72B) to
0.69 (OLMo-2-32B). It orders the fifteen models' chaotic length (Spearman $+0.55$) somewhat better than their parameter
count does ($-0.44$; Figure~\ref{fig:scale}, right), but it misses the chaos of Qwen2.5-1.5B and -14B at nearly
uniform distributions, above its 10-nat bound. This analysis was adapted after the OLMo-2-32B result, and the breadth
is measured on the loop's own orbits, which chaos itself spreads, so the test is partly circular. A loop-free version,
frozen before it was run (\texttt{docs/start-state-predictor.md}), uses one forward pass per model and prompt: the
entropy profile of the logits at the loop's start state, and the one-step gain there. Its primary prediction fails: the
start-state breadth on the paper's prompt orders the fourteen dense models' chaotic length with Spearman $+0.30$,
below the frozen threshold of $0.5$. Its median over ten prompts reaches $+0.52$, just above it ($p=0.06$; $+0.44$
against the complete sweeps), and the one-step gain at the start state is negative in every model, the most chaotic
ones included ($-0.5$ to $-3.4$ per step), and orders nothing ($-0.32$). No property of a single forward pass that we
tested predicts the loop's chaos usefully: as for the period-8 orbit of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, it has to be measured
on the loop.

hostility plane, at the layer of Goodfire's 70B SAE and at equal norm, 4.3\,\% of cells are labelled chaotic along the

a random live feature, against 28, 29, 39 and 27\,\% for the same four directions in Llama-3.1-8B (on one GPU);
Qwen2.5-72B along hostility gives 0.8\,\%: eight cells, all at $\log_{10}T=0.26$--$0.30$. Recomputed in float64
throughout, six of the eight are chaotic, with exact tangent exponents of 0.23 to 0.36 that the twins at all four
offsets from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-7}$ reproduce within 0.01; five have nearly uniform next-token distributions (11.0 to
11.1 nats) and one alternates between sharp and flat behind a period-2 leading token. The same temperatures without

\paragraph{What this implies for larger models.} Size does not set this chaos in a simple way. In Qwen2.5, the 14B
model's chaos did not vanish but moved to higher temperature, to where its next-token distribution spreads; in OLMo-2
the largest model was the most chaotic; post-training changed the chaos of two 70B Llama checkpoints of the same size;
and at 70B, chaos that passes a three-offset test remains, rarer, with and without steering. The shift does not
continue to Qwen2.5-72B. A prompt-blocked test frozen before its data (\texttt{docs/prefix-scale-72b.md}) predicted
that the 72B model would be chaotic at fewer of the 48 temperatures than the 14B on at least three of four held-out
prompts. It is chaotic at 0, 1, 0 and 0 of them, against 14, 9, 11 and 15, so the prediction holds on all four. These
lines ran before the precision fix (1000 transient and 2000 measured steps, twins at $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-6}$, chaotic
only when both exponents are positive); no temperature on them is positive at only one offset, and the one chaotic
temperature ($\log_{10}T=0.319$, exponents 0.35 and 0.34) is chaotic again when recomputed in float64 throughout,
with an exact tangent exponent of 0.34. With the paper's prompt, where it showed no robust chaos up to
$\log_{10}T=1.2$, that makes five prompts on which the 72B model is nearly free of unsteered chaos. Its next-token distribution jumps from sharp to nearly uniform within less
than the grid spacing (0.03 decades), so a narrow chaotic band at the jump would be missed by the grid. Fine lines
through the jump, computed in float64 throughout with exact tangent exponents, find only periodic orbits on the
paper's prompt and on three of the four held-out prompts, and on the fourth a band of chaos about 0.02 decades wide
just above the jump ($\log_{10}T=0.319$--$0.336$; exponents up to 0.40, which twins at $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$ reproduce
within 0.004), at nearly uniform distributions, as the 14B model's chaos but far narrower. A fixed temperature window is therefore the wrong yardstick, and so is size alone. A model's chaos follows the
gain of its loop where its own next-token distribution is spread, no single forward pass we tested predicts it, and
to find it in a larger model one has to run the loop there, across prompts. For frontier models this means two
things: their unsteered chaos may be rare, if, like the largest models here, their distributions spread over a narrow
range of temperature; and steering still drives both 70B-class models that we steered, Llama-3.3-70B and
Qwen2.5-72B, into chaos.

\section{A second system: recurrent depth}
\label{sec:huginn}
Huginn-0125 iterates a recurrent block at fixed input tokens \cite{geiping2025recurrent}. For a GSM8K-style
\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} prompt, the native loop converges to a fixed point whose leading Jacobian pair is
$0.588\pm0.771i$ (modulus $0.970$). For the relaxed loop $h\leftarrow(1-\lambda)h+\lambda F(h)$, whose multipliers
are $1-\lambda+\lambda\mu$, this predicts that the pair crosses the unit circle at
$\lambda_c=2(1-\operatorname{Re}\mu)/|1-\mu|^2=1.0777$, transversally and away from the strong resonances (at
0.156 of a revolution per step): a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation. Below it the latent state
decays; above it the state settles on a closed curve. A fit of the squared amplitude gives $\lambda_c=1.0778$, with
$A/\sqrt{\lambda-\lambda_c}=0.52$--$0.54$. A stable circle that grows like the square root of the distance is the
signature of a supercritical bifurcation; we did not compute its first Lyapunov coefficient directly. Causal attention makes the loop block lower-triangular in
token positions. The first position to oscillate is the ``.'' of the prompt, and all later positions follow. On the
invariant circle the motion turns by $0.156$ of a revolution per step, matching the argument of the critical pair.
The rotation number changes smoothly, by about $10^{-4}$ over $\lambda\in[1.079,1.092]$, and no return within 60
steps was found at $10^{-3}$ of the radius. This is consistent with quasi-periodic motion, although locking in narrow
high-order tongues cannot be excluded. From $\lambda\approx1.094$ the circle deforms or a second frequency appears,
near where a second complex pair destabilizes the fixed point ($\lambda=1.0814$). Fixed-point and Jacobian stability
of looped models has been studied before \cite{yang2026stars}; what this adds is the predicted crossing, its amplitude
law and the rotation number.

\paragraph{The spectrum predicts the relaxation response.} With the full instruction template, the GSM8K prompts'
fixed points look different: the leading multipliers surround the origin, a real $0.914$, complex pairs of modulus
$0.85$--$0.89$ at many angles and negative real values near $-0.8$, with no near-critical pair. Because the prefill
Jacobian is block lower-triangular in positions, its spectrum is the union of per-position blocks, and template
positions contribute identical eigenvalues to every prompt. Such a spectrum makes three predictions before any task is
scored. First, within the computed spectral estimates of these prompts, no fixed relaxation can speed convergence
much. With leading multipliers spread around the origin at many angles, any $\lambda$ that shrinks one group enlarges
another, and the best $\lambda^*=1.03$--$1.06$ lowers the rate from $0.914$ to only $0.908$--$0.911$. (A finite
computed spectrum does not show that no other polynomial scheme could do better; Anderson acceleration, tried below,
did not.) Second, under-relaxation slows convergence ($\lambda=0.55$: predicted $0.953$, measured $0.951$ on one
prompt). Third, over-relaxation destabilizes the negative modes first, at
$\lambda_c=2\operatorname{Re}(1-\mu)/|1-\mu|^2=1.074$--$1.126$ (five prompts), in a flip or a Neimark--Sacker
bifurcation close to $-1$. The approach to it is visible: on the prompt with $\lambda_c=1.074$, the measured rate at
$\lambda=1.05$ is $0.955$ against a predicted $0.956$ (native $0.864$). Elsewhere the spectral radius bounds the rate
measured over the accessible window from above, by up to $0.05$, because the slowest modes carry little amplitude
there. Independently, \citet{wang2026taps} report a Huginn GSM8K drop from $19.41\,\%$ to
$8.34\,\%$ at a constant step of $1.2$; at $0.8$ GSM8K is $19.79\,\%$, while their four-task mean falls slightly.
Their trajectory-based scheduling analysis does not compute the prompt-specific spectral thresholds studied here.

\paragraph{Does the task notice? (development data).} On ARC-Challenge \cite{clark2018arc} questions scored by
log-likelihood, where one prefill per answer choice is exactly the analysed recurrence, the spectra again predict
convergence. Rates agree to $0.002$--$0.006$ where the slowest mode dominates, and beyond each question's $\lambda_c$
($1.075$--$1.149$ for four questions) the residual stops decaying. For 6 of 30 development questions the native fixed
point is itself unstable ($\rho(1)=1.009$--$1.273$; the fixed point, located with a relaxed transient, has a real
multiplier beyond $-1$ or a complex pair outside the circle). The native loop then oscillates (relative amplitude
$2\times10^{-2}$ for one of them), and relaxation to $0.55$ or $0.75$ restores convergence to $10^{-6}$. For the
other 24, no unstable mode was detected in the computed spectral estimates up to $\lambda=1.020$--$1.149$ (median
$1.095$); partial Ritz spectra cannot exclude an undetected unstable mode. Question by question, an
estimated spectral feature ranks the question's residual. This feature, $\hat\rho_q(\lambda)$, is the largest
$|1-\lambda+\lambda\mu|$ over the computed Ritz values of the question's gold-answer prefill, and its Spearman
correlation with the residual is $0.73$ at $\lambda=1$ and $0.75$ at $1.10$ over the 30 questions (development
data). In this historical analysis the residual was the all-choice aggregate entering the 32nd recurrence call, so
this is a predictive association across related maps and precisions, not a same-state contraction identity. It is
consistent with the latent orbits that \citet{geiping2025recurrent} observed on some tokens of harder questions, here
traced to the fixed point's spectrum. The item-level part of the frozen test below uses matched gold-row token IDs and
a per-row residual with an explicit clock.

Accuracy, however, tolerates the instability (300 training questions, 32 steps): it is $0.367$ native, $0.387$ at
$\lambda=1.10$ and $0.373$ at $1.15$, where the latent state oscillates with relative size $0.08$ and $0.23$. Only at
$1.25$ (size $0.68$) does it fall, to $0.300$. Yet the oscillation does reach the answers. Reading every step from 24
to 32 on 100 questions, the margin between the correct choice and its best rival moves by $0.11$ nats per step
natively, $0.37$ at $1.10$ and $0.95$ at $1.15$, in alternating directions, and the chosen answer changes within these
nine steps for 12\,\%, 16\,\% and 33\,\% of the questions. The changes go both ways, so the accuracy at any one step
stays the same on average. Under-relaxation instead costs 4--5 points at 8--16 steps and catches up by 32 (a slower
transient), and Anderson acceleration neither speeds the transient nor gains accuracy. Testing the accuracy
consequence therefore needs a task in which such step-to-step changes can compound.

GSM8K generation is such a task. With eight chain-of-thought examples in the lm-evaluation-harness format, greedy
decoding and 32 recurrence steps per token (development problems 0--119, every arm on the same problems and initial
states), accuracy is $0.375$ natively, $0.425$ at $\lambda=1.05$ and at $1.10$, $0.29$ at $1.15$ (17 problems lost and
7 gained, one-sided McNemar $p=0.03$) and $0.16$ at $1.20$ (29 lost, 3 gained, $p=10^{-6}$). Under-relaxation to
$0.8$ costs nothing at 32 steps ($0.41$). The break lies between $1.10$ and $1.15$, at or just above the spectral
thresholds ($\lambda_c=1.074$--$1.126$ for the GSM8K prompts; median upper stability limit $1.095$ for the ARC
development questions). Generation breaks earlier than log-likelihood scoring, consistent with answer changes that
compound over tokens. The collapse at $1.2$ is the one \citet{wang2026taps} reported.

A frozen test on fresh problems confirms this. Its design and analysis were pushed before any test item was read,
after two rounds of independent review. On 200 GSM8K test problems, accuracy is $0.330$ natively, $0.395$ at
$\lambda=1.05$, $0.255$ at $1.15$ and $0.095$ at $1.20$. The two pre-stated predictions pass: accuracy declines at $1.20$ ($-23.5$
points; 50 problems lost and 3 gained, exact one-sided McNemar $p=3\times10^{-12}$), and $1.05$ loses no more than
four points (difference $+6.5$, one-sided 95\,\% lower bound $+2.5$). At $1.15$ accuracy falls by $7.5$ points
($p=0.01$, reported descriptively). Development and test agree closely (Figure~\ref{fig:huginn-gsm8k}).

The same frozen design tested the item-level prediction on 64 held-out ARC-Challenge questions. The first 16
spectra preceded the read-out generation; the remaining ones were completed afterward under a recorded amendment, with
the producer attesting that read-out outcomes had not yet been inspected, and duplicate copies were resolved by a rule
fixed in advance. Every question passed the admission gates (fixed-point residual at most
$2.6\times10^{-6}$; identical gold-row token IDs in every arm); for 13 questions one of the three Arnoldi sets was
partial, which the design admits for an estimated feature. The pre-stated statistic, the mean of the within-arm
Spearman correlations between $\hat\rho_q(\lambda)$ and the gold row's residual entering the 32nd update, is $0.701$
(95\,\% bootstrap interval $[0.576,0.779]$; $0.68$, $0.73$, $0.73$ and $0.67$ at $\lambda=1$, $1.05$, $1.08$ and

point's estimated spectrum therefore ranks each question by the size of the gold row's residual entering update 32;
a residual is not in general a distance to the fixed point. Descriptively, the share of questions with a
detected unstable mode rises from $8\,\%$ natively to $34$, $69$ and $84\,\%$ at $\lambda=1.05$, $1.08$ and $1.10$.
An independent implementation reproduced both parts of the frozen test from the saved data.

\section{Steering toward hostility}
\label{sec:hostility}
A second steering direction tests whether the organization of the philosophy-steered map is specific to one
concept. We steer along a valence direction, the contrast of hostile statements against matched kind ones, built
in the manner of contrastive activation addition \cite{rimsky2024steering}; its cosine with the gratitude
direction is $-0.04$. Sampled self-dialogue under the same steering is a different system, with a growing context
and sampling noise; we report that study separately and follow the direction here in the deterministic map.

\paragraph{The hostility direction in the deterministic map.} Steering the soft-token map of
Section~\ref{sec:map} along the same hostility direction gives a plane with chaos (a positive largest exponent in
10\,\% of a coarse $128\times192$ float32 scan) and with periodic text loops that are hostile: ``Yes, that's crap''
(period 6), ``a lie is'' (3), ``STUP\ldots'' (3), a loop on the Chinese word for insult and its 2-cycle, and others.
They appear at $\alpha$ above about $0.035$ (the insult loop) and above $0.065$ (the rest). Around the period-6 loop
the windows form a dome (Figure~\ref{fig:hostile-cascade}), and toward higher temperature its texts gain one ``!Yes''
after another (periods $6,8,\ldots,20$). In float64 the windows of periods 12 to 20 are born in folds on their
low-temperature side and end in flips or $+1$ crossings on the other, like the stripes of Sections~\ref{sec:adding}
and~\ref{sec:mechanism}. The period-6 window ends in a fold at $\log_{10}T=-0.0991$, and the continuations of the
period-8 and period-10 windows failed to converge.

The windows accumulate on the fold of the 2-cycle ``!Yes'' at $\log_{10}T_c=-0.0462465357$ ($\alpha=0.083$; critical
multiplier $0.9999999954$, next modulus $0.050$). From period 16 on, $1/\sqrt{\log_{10}T_c-\log_{10}T_{\rm fold}}$
rises by $2.235$ per lap. The fold's normal form ($a=-1.168$, $b=44.53$ with $\varepsilon$ in $\log_{10}T$) predicts
$\sqrt{|ab|}/\pi=2.296$ per lap for full passages through the bottleneck, within 3\,\% and without a fitted
parameter. An independent float64 forward and automatic-differentiation implementation reproduces this fold
(location to $6\times10^{-10}$ relative, a simple real $+1$ multiplier with next modulus $0.050$) and its coefficients
($ab$ to 8 parts per million). In that implementation, controlled passages from fixed entrances at $\log_{10}T$
offsets $-10^{-5}$, $-3\times10^{-5}$ and $-10^{-4}$ took 119/59, 61/31 and 27/13 iterates of $F^2$ (full
channel/from its midpoint), against prospectively frozen quadratic forecasts of 118/61, 61/32 and 27/14. Five of the
six are within one iterate; the smallest-offset midpoint missed by two, failing the frozen all-six criterion.
Finite-endpoint (arctan) forecasts, also frozen, were within $0.923$ iterates. These are controlled local transits. At
the window distances the finite-channel correction to the asymptotic passage time is large, so the agreement of the
window slope with the full-passage coefficient does not by itself show where the natural dynamics reinjects. The same
type-I intermittency that organizes the philosophy-steered map thus generates a hostile cascade.

A second region, near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.06,0.20)$, shows arms of period-7 and period-8 windows spiralling around
the insult loop. In float64 the insult fixed point doubles into a 2-cycle at $\alpha\approx0.041$ and $0.079$, and
that 2-cycle has a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation in the spiral region: at $\alpha=0.062$ its complex pair crosses the
unit circle near $\log_{10}T=0.1656$, turning $0.12$ of a revolution per step of the 2-cycle. This does not explain
the arms. The two invariant circles born at a 2-cycle's Neimark--Sacker point are exchanged by the map, so orbits
locked on them have even periods, near 16 for this rotation, whereas the arms include period 7; and of four float32
arm cells, only one kept its period in float64. In dissipative flows, windows wind in nested spirals around
periodicity hubs \cite{vitolo2011hubs}, and in the R\"ossler system the hub's shape follows from a conjugacy to
unimodal maps \cite{jones2012rossler}; we have not tested whether the arms here wind around such a hub. Float64 zooms
show more structure. In one arm a period-7 domain
carries period-14 crescents with period-28 cores along its lower edge, between period-1 and period-2 regions and a
chaotic band. In the cascade the windows are nested crescent arcs inside a dome bounded by the ``!Yes'' 2-cycle
region, and near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.087,-0.069)$ the period-12 and period-14 windows bend into hooked, nested
arches. These are the map-level features that accompany shrimp (a periodic body with doubling crescents; hooked
windows).

In float64 the period-7 body is bounded by folds. At $\log_{10}T=0.1925$ the orbit exists between $\alpha=0.065966$
and $0.071188$, and at $\alpha=0.066$ it ends in a fold at $\log_{10}T=0.19263$. Below that, the multiplier passes
through $-1$ twice (at $0.1919$ and $0.1899$), so the period-14 crescent is a doubling bubble. Further down, the
branch folds twice ($0.18797$ and $0.18921$, with three coexisting period-7 orbits in between) before a third flip.
All crossings are simple (next modulus below $1.5\times10^{-3}$). An S-shaped pair of folds is what a cusp of the
fold curves leaves on a nearby cut. When these boundaries are continued in the plane in float64
(Figure~\ref{fig:hubshrimp}), each of the two fold curves reverses its parameter tangent once. There the oriented
quadratic coefficient $b$ of the fold's normal form passes through zero (upper fold: $-23.9$ to $+244.6$ between
adjacent points, with $a\approx2.0$; lower fold: $+31.2$ to $-58.7$, with $a\approx0.55$) while $a$ stays away from
zero. Located along the curves, the two cusps lie at $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.067213,0.198983)$ and
$(0.066611,0.189382)$. There $b\operatorname{sign}a$ is below $1.3\times10^{-5}$ of its bracket values,
$c=-1.41\times10^{6}$ and $-5.61\times10^{5}$, and $\det J=58.2$ and $55.2$, all evaluated at the located points.
The flip curve of the period-14 bubble lies inside the body and closes around the map's period-14 cells. The cubic
coefficient is nonzero at the points that bracket both sign changes: $c\approx-1.4\times10^{6}$ to $-2.0\times10^{6}$ (step agreement $5\,\%$; the
smallest steps converge like the square of the step, and extrapolation leaves the sign) and $-5.8\times10^{5}$ to
$-5.3\times10^{5}$. This supports nondegenerate cusps inside both brackets, and $c<0$ agrees with the three
coexisting period-7 orbits, two of them stable, found between the S-shaped folds. At a fold point near the lower
cusp (where $b=31.4$) the estimated unfolding is nonsingular too ($\det J=53.5$ in the same coordinates, singular
values $262$ and $0.20$). Its local predictions at six frozen offsets matched the orbits that Newton solves seeded at
the predicted roots and the base state recovered: in number at all six and in stability at five. The miss, at the largest
offset beyond the edge of the region with three orbits, marks the range of the cubic model. The hostile period-7 body
thus has the same skeleton as the period-6 window of Section~\ref{sec:curves}.

Its interior is shrimp-like too. At $\log_{10}T=0.1830$, float64 bifurcation diagrams through the body
(Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade}; 1400 cells per line) show the period-7 orbit doubling to periods 14 and 28 and entering
chaos (largest exponent up to $+0.1$) for $\alpha\approx0.0659$--$0.0673$. Windows of periods 28, 35 and 42 sit
inside the chaotic band, and the orbit then undoubles through 28 and 14 back to 7; the exponent touches zero at every
doubling. Newton's method converges at map cells to primitive orbits of periods 14, 28 and 21 (residuals
$3\times10^{-15}$, $2\times10^{-14}$ and $5\times10^{-13}$; spectral radii $0.08$, $4\times10^{-4}$ and $0.006$).
Periods 21, 35 and 42 are three, five and six times the body's period: the satellite windows expected in the chaotic
bands of a period-7 cascade. Continued in temperature, the period-14 orbit flips into period 28 near
$\log_{10}T=0.1841$ and back near $0.1857$, the two ends of a doubling bubble that holds period 28 and chaos. Lower
down, its branch is S-shaped (folds near $0.1824$ and $0.18244$, with three coexisting period-14 orbits between them)
before another flip near $0.1819$. All these crossings are simple (next modulus below $4\times10^{-5}$). A $160^2$
float64 zoom of the lower body (Figure~\ref{fig:hubzoom}) shows this anatomy in the plane. The period-14 domain
encloses a period-28 ring around a chaotic core. Period-21 windows sit in the chaotic region as thin crescents with
legs, and curves of very negative exponent, where the orbits are superstable (the leading multiplier crosses zero),
run through the windows as the ``endoskeleton'' of shrimps does \cite{facanha2013endoskeletons}. In this zoom
18\,\% of cells are chaotic.

\paragraph{No one-dimensional shrimp centre.} In a shrimp of a one-dimensional map, two superstable curves of the
window's orbit cross at a doubly superstable centre \cite{gallas1993structure,facanha2013endoskeletons}. We continued
one period-7 orbit over a $31\times31$ grid of the body's upper part and followed the sign of its leading multiplier
(Figure~\ref{fig:superstable}). Its superstable curves form two arcs that approach within $1.3\times10^{-3}$ in
$\log_{10}T$ near $(\alpha,\log_{10}T)=(0.0700,0.206)$ and do not cross. A five times finer grid of that approach
(1155 cells) shows why. There the leading multiplier is tiny (median modulus $9\times10^{-4}$) and as small as the next
one, and in 221 cells the two form a complex pair, in two wedges that flank the gap. The map's derivative has no zero
eigenvalue, so the leading multiplier can change sign only by exchanging places with the next one or by merging with
it into a complex pair. Where a one-dimensional map would place its doubly superstable centre, the orbit is
two-dimensional, and a scalar superstable curve is not defined. Next to the body, the 7-step return maps of chaotic
cells are single-humped in every phase, with doubled strands in two phases, the trace of a thin second dimension
(Figure~\ref{fig:returnmapsbody}). The body is therefore not the shrimp of a one-dimensional map. Whether it is the shrimp of an effectively
two-dimensional map, as in the H\'enon family, is open.

\paragraph{Chaos behind a repeating text.} Along the temperature line of Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade} (left), a longer
test (2000 measured steps after 1000 transient steps, twins at offsets $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$, whose exponents agree
to within 0.0031 in every chaotic cell) finds 182 chaotic cells among 1400 between $\log_{10}T=0.176$ and $0.191$. In
65 of them the leading token repeats exactly for all 2000 steps while the state moves chaotically; in 59 of these the
period of the text is a multiple of 7, most often 7 or 28. Figure~\ref{fig:hiddenbands} shows the strongest case
($\log_{10}T=0.18995$, largest exponent 0.091 at both offsets). Its 2000 sampled states fall into seven separated
groups, one per phase of the text, visited in a fixed order and at least 0.098 of the state norm apart (a property of
the samples, not a proof of separated invariant sets), and each group lies inside one cell of the partition by leading
token, so the text \texttt{I!Yes\ heels$\hookleftarrow\hookleftarrow$pee$\hookleftarrow\hookleftarrow$}
repeats at every one of the 2000 steps we followed. Inside a group the sampled states lie close to a curve (a
strongly anisotropic cloud). Read once per repetition through the probability of the last token, the sampled return
relation is well approximated by a smooth map with a single quadratic maximum, the shape of the logistic map: a
polynomial of degree 8 fits it to $4\times10^{-4}$ of its range, and the fitted map's Lyapunov exponent, 0.647 per
repetition, is within 2\,\% of the full map's 0.636 per seven steps, measured with twin trajectories in all 896
dimensions. The scalar approximation holds across most of the cascade. Rerun with the leading-token sequences saved, 62 of the 65 cells are again
chaotic behind a repeating text, and in every one of them several phases of the text (a median of ten) give
single-valued return maps; in 59 cells the best-fitting phase has a single interior stationary point (a maximum in
36, a minimum in 23). If the dynamics were exactly one-dimensional and each phase's probability a smooth invertible
coordinate on it, all single-valued phases would give the same asymptotic exponent, since exponents do not change
under such a change of coordinates. We therefore
fit each phase's map by a polynomial whose degree is chosen by cross-validation over random folds of the return-map
pairs, and take the median over the phases. It
matches the full map's exponent within 10\,\% in 51 of the 62 cells (median discrepancy 3.7\,\%; within 10\,\% in 23
of the 24 cells with seven-token text, which give 285 repetitions in 2000 steps; Figure~\ref{fig:reduction}). This
analysis was adopted after we inspected an outlier of our first, pre-specified analysis (a fixed degree of 8 at the
single best-fitting phase), which gave 49 of 62 and underfitted one cell whose maps are steep and have two turning
points; we report both. An exploratory temporal split (fit on the first 60\,\% of each record, test on the last
30\,\%) gives small value-prediction errors at the best scalar phase, chosen on the test segment itself, but in 30 of
61 cells two- or three-delay predictors improve the held-out error by 20\,\% or more, which the pre-stated rule counts
against scalar sufficiency. These results support an empirical scalar description, not a proved one-dimensional
reduction: a low-residual fit does not show that the observable is invertible, and even exact agreement of exponents
would not establish a one-dimensional invariant curve (two uncoupled logistic maps observed through one coordinate
agree in the same way). An independent implementation reproduced the strongest cell (the same leading token at all
2000 steps; exponent 0.089 against our 0.091; scalar discrepancy $+2.3\,\%$ under the first rule and $+1.5\,\%$
under the amended one) and both cross-cell counts (49 and 51 of 62). At two further cells it chose, it found good
agreement after the amendment in one (text period 10, $+0.2\,\%$, although its fitted maps have both a maximum and a
minimum) and a persistent discrepancy of about 18\,\% in the other (period 28), despite a small value error. These
are the chaotic bands that follow a period-doubling cascade, and a smooth unimodal map is the setting of Feigenbaum's
universality \cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}; a fitted maximum on the sampled range does not by itself establish
that theorem's hypotheses, and we have shown the scalar approximation for this cascade only. A reader of the text sees a loop. Only the probabilities, or the state, reveal the
chaos.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What theory allows, and what is new.} Chaos and periodic windows occur in many nonlinear feedback
systems. In a specified ensemble of random recurrent networks with asymmetric couplings, chaos sets in above a
critical gain, sharply in the large-network limit, and finite networks pass through limit cycles of increasing
complexity on the way \cite{sompolinsky1988chaos}; how the transition looks depends on the architecture and the
transfer function \cite{kadmon2015transition}. Discrete-time random networks, $x(t+1)=\tanh(gJx(t))$, iterate a map
as our loop does: their fixed point loses stability when the gain times the spectral radius of the couplings exceeds
one, the first bifurcation of large networks is a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation, and chaos follows by a quasi-periodic
route, through a torus and frequency locking, over a range of gain that narrows as the network grows
\cite{doyon1994bifurcations}. Our largest model narrows differently: Qwen2.5-72B passes from a confident echo to a
nearly uniform fixed point within 0.03 decades of temperature, and its chaos is confined to narrow bands, whereas in
random networks chaos is generic above the transition. External input can suppress that chaos
\cite{rajan2010stimulus}, and deep networks at initialization show an order-to-chaos transition in signal propagation
\cite{poole2016exponential,schoenholz2017deep}. In our map, temperature plays the role of an inverse gain and the
steering vector that of an input. Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes the correspondence concrete through an exact
chain-rule factorization, with the softmax derivative $(\operatorname{diag}p-pp^{\top})/T$ in the place of the slope
of the transfer function; the identification with the random-network transition remains an analogy, not a transfer
of that ensemble's theorem. Feigenbaum scaling holds for a class of one-dimensional maps with a quadratic maximum
\cite{feigenbaum1978quantitative}, and periodic windows, shrimp-shaped domains with their cusps and spirals, and
doubling cascades are found in many dissipative two-parameter systems
\cite{gallas1993structure,bonatto2008periodicity,vitolo2011hubs,facanha2013endoskeletons} and in experiments
\cite{stoop2010real}; intermittency near folds is classical \cite{pomeau1980intermittent}. These results motivate
looking for chaos, windows and shrimp-like structure in a language model's feedback map, but they do not imply it. They do not say whether a trained language model
realizes it, where in its parameter plane, with which texts, or whether the theory's quantitative predictions hold for
that model. This paper shows that the models we studied do realize it, with concrete texts as attractors, so that a
bifurcation changes what the model writes, and that the quantitative predictions hold in the cases we tested.

As far as we know, no earlier work has computed periodic orbits and their multipliers, continued fold and flip
bifurcation curves and located cusps in the feedback map of a released pretrained language model. Nor do
we know of earlier quantitative predictions from such a model's derivatives, for these objects, that were confirmed
prospectively, as ours were for passage times at new folds (23 of 24 forecasts within one iterate, with the all-row
criterion failing by one row) and for the orbits recovered and their stabilities at frozen offsets near numerically
located cusps; derivative-based normal-form predictions in a task-trained looped transformer precede ours
\cite{kim2026phase}. A frozen spectral prediction of the response to relaxation is confirmed on held-out data in
Huginn, and one periodic orbit of the full transformer map of
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct is proved by interval arithmetic; we know of no earlier instance of either for a language model.
\paragraph{Is steering the origin of the chaos?} Steering adds a fixed vector to the residual stream, so chaos along
a steering axis could belong to the push rather than to the model. At $\alpha=0$, however, the map is the unsteered
model's own feedback map, with temperature as its only parameter. The $\alpha=0$ columns of the hostility maps contain
cells with positive finite-horizon exponents in Llama-3.1 8B (26 of 64 temperatures), Qwen2.5-7B (15 of 64) and
OLMo-2 7B (11 of 64), while the near-zero columns of the 0.5B and 1.5B maps contain one such cell each (of 128 and
64). A longer float64 sweep of the unsteered 0.5B map (512 temperatures over $\log_{10}T\in[-1,0.3]$, 1000 transient
and 2000 measured steps) finds a narrow chaotic band at $\log_{10}T\approx0.15$--$0.21$ (7 temperatures, exponents up
to $0.28$), with a period-5 loop at 437 of the 512 temperatures. The same long sweep finds 96 chaotic temperatures in
Llama-3.1 8B, between $\log_{10}T=-0.20$ and $0.24$ (exponents up to $0.66$), interleaved with periodic windows of
periods 2 to 52; within $\log_{10}T\in[-0.3,0.3]$ its chaotic share, 41\,\%, equals that of the coarse column.
Qwen2.5-7B has 65 chaotic temperatures between $\log_{10}T=-0.07$ and $0.28$ (exponents up to $0.74$), and OLMo-2 7B
has 32 between $0.04$ and $0.14$ (up to $0.30$). Steering is therefore not needed to reach chaotic regimes in any of
these four models. Temperature alone produces chaos with embedded windows, broadly in the 7B and 8B models and in
a narrow band in the 0.5B model (Figure~\ref{fig:alpha0}). Across the fifteen models of Section~\ref{sec:size}, the
regular-grid sweeps find unsteered chaos in eleven of the fourteen dense models (in Llama-3.1-70B at two
temperatures that pass a three-offset test), but not in Qwen2.5-72B, Llama-3.3-70B, OLMo-2-13B or the mixture of
experts; finer lines found candidates in the first two, and the Qwen2.5-72B window, recomputed in float64
throughout, is periodic.

Steering does widen the chaotic regions, but not as a property of the concept: along norm-matched random directions,
the 0.5B maps are as chaotic as along the concept directions (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}). On the same $128^2$ grid,
7.6, 8.3 and 12.5\,\% of cells are chaotic for three Gaussian directions with the philosophy vector's norm, against
6.8\,\% for philosophy and 7.5\,\% for gratitude. In all five maps the chaotic share rises from about 1\,\% at
$|\alpha|<0.01$ to between 5 and 22\,\% at larger $|\alpha|$, and a chaotic region at high temperature, with windows
reaching down, recurs. In this model, chaos under steering is therefore a generic response to perturbing the residual
stream at this norm. Rotating the steering direction makes the same point continuously: along the great circle from
one Gaussian direction to another, $90^{\circ}$ away, at constant norm, the chaotic regions move and change shape, but
the chaotic share stays between 13 and 24\,\% at every one of 103 angles (Figure~\ref{fig:morph}; a video of the
rotation is in the supplementary material). The direction does select the texts. The largest attractor family in all five maps is the
unsteered period-5 loop, but beyond it only 26 to 56\,\% of one map's cells carry an attractor text that appears
anywhere in another map. Llama-3.1 8B agrees. On its hostility plane, two norm-matched random directions at the same
layer give 26 and 17\,\% chaotic cells against 28\,\% along the concept direction; all three carry 34 to 44\,\% near
$\alpha=0$, and the random directions thin out at large $|\alpha|$. At layer 19, where a sparse autoencoder trained on
this model reads the residual stream \cite{goodfire2025sae}, the concept vector rebuilt there gives 28\,\%. The
decoder direction of the autoencoder feature most specific to hostility on held-out texts (chosen by a rule fixed
before any map; cosine $0.10$ with the concept vector) gives 29\,\%, a random live feature 27\,\% and a Gaussian
direction 39\,\%, all at the same norm. In this model, chaos therefore occurs along autoencoder-feature,
contrastive and random directions alike, with direction-dependent prevalence; at 70B the directions differ more
(Section~\ref{sec:size}).

\paragraph{What is and is not claimed.} The objects studied here are invariant sets and bifurcations of an exactly
defined smooth map built from a frozen model's own computation. We do not claim that ordinary sampled generation is
chaotic in this sense (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}), nor that any window is a certified shrimp. The period-6 window and
the hostile period-7 body have, numerically, shrimp-like organization: fold curves with numerically located cusps (simple
critical multiplier; quadratic coefficient vanishing at a point where the cubic coefficient is nonzero and the
unfolding is regular) and flip curves inside, and in the hostile body curves of very negative exponent, where the
orbits are superstable, run through the windows. That is less than the full anatomy of a shrimp. For the hostile body
we computed the superstable curves of one continued orbit. They do not cross, and at their closest approach the orbit
is two-dimensional, so it has no one-dimensional shrimp centre (Figure~\ref{fig:superstable}). We have not determined
the windows' symbolic sequences or tested for a periodicity hub \cite{gallas1993structure,jones2012rossler}. In a map that is effectively one-dimensional with a single critical
point, a primitive orbit cannot be doubly superstable, so true shrimp centres would require a second critical point in
the reduced dynamics. A generic codimension-two
cusp also requires the two unfolding coefficients to depend on the two parameters with rank two. We estimated this at
a fold point near one cusp of each window (determinants $2737$ and $53.5$), and there the local model's
predictions at twelve frozen offsets matched the orbits recovered by Newton solves seeded at the predicted roots and
the base state, in number at all twelve and in stability at eleven; the search was not exhaustive. At the nine
located cusps themselves, $|\det J|$ lies between $14$ and $2416$. These are float64
computations validated by step-size checks (rung 1), not computer-assisted proofs. At the two continuation points
that bracket the lower cusp of the hostile body, an independent implementation of the model reproduced the
normal-form coefficients, including the sign change of $b$: $|b|$ within $1.2\,\%$ and $c$ to two figures, with the
opposite orientation of the null vector, which flips the signs of $a$ and $b$ together.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The detailed orbit studies cover one model family (Qwen2.5, 0.5B and 1.5B), with coarse
maps and three float64 orbits at 7B; the two other families (OLMo-2 7B and Llama-3.1 8B) have maps, including
$128^2$ refinements for Llama, but no continued orbits. The size comparison of Section~\ref{sec:size} uses regular
temperature grids that can miss narrow windows, a window that missed most of the larger models' chaos, and mostly
one twin offset per label. The two-GPU runs of the 70B-class models computed their final normalization in float32
until we fixed it; their labels rest on offsets of $10^{-6}$ or more, and the cells we rechecked, the Qwen2.5-72B jump
window and steered candidates in float64 throughout and samples of the Llama maps at three offsets, behave as stated,
but the rest of those maps were not recomputed. Neither were the four coarse lines of the frozen 72B test; after the
fix we computed only fine lines through their entropy jumps, one of which passes through their single chaotic
temperature.
The maps use one prompt (Section~\ref{sec:size} adds ten for Qwen2.5-7B and -14B, and four for Qwen2.5-72B, its base
model, and OLMo-2-7B and -32B), and steering uses contrastive vectors, checked against norm-matched random and sparse-autoencoder
directions in two models. The system is a constructed loop: Section~\ref{sec:bridge} extends it to memories of up to sixteen soft tokens and to the growing context of Soft Thinking, but sampled decoding of discrete tokens is a different system. The evidence consists of numerical
validation, cross-checks and prospectively frozen passage tests (including failed all-row criteria), with a
computer-assisted proof for only one orbit at one parameter point. Chaos labels are finite-horizon estimates (300
measured steps after 300 to 500 transient steps), and no chaotic cell is proved chaotic. A computer-assisted proof of chaos, such as a horseshoe verified by covering
relations, would need enclosures of the map over boxes many orders of magnitude larger than the period-8 proof's
(radius $2^{-640}$). With the ball arithmetic used there, a box of radius $2^{-80}$ already loses the positivity
of an RMS normalization at layer 3 (the enclosures overestimate through the layers), so such a proof needs other
verified methods. A horizon check retested
random cells of two float64 maps (Figures~\ref{fig:teaser} and~\ref{fig:stripes}) with 1000 transient and 2000
measured steps at two twin offsets: 86 and 92 of 96 cells labelled chaotic stayed chaotic (all but one of the rest
settled on periodic orbits), all 96 periodic cells in each map stayed periodic, and 18 and 20 of 32 cells with neither
label turned out to be slow periodic transients. Near the accumulation of a cascade the labels are less reliable, because orbits there converge
slowly or wander chaotically before they settle (transient chaos \cite{lai2011transient}): on
the hostile line of Figure~\ref{fig:hubcascade} (left), 135 of the 235 cells labelled chaotic from 400 measured steps
stayed chaotic and 91 were periodic. The Feigenbaum ratios are
finite-order values that rise toward $\delta$ (the last is within 1\,\%); the limit itself is not computed. All
orbit, bifurcation and chaos results concern the constructed map, not sampled generation with a growing context. With
sampling, the bridge of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} tests only whether the bottleneck's laminar phases survive the noise;
its other tests are deterministic (greedy decoding, longer fed-back memories and Soft Thinking's growing context).

\paragraph{Steering strength is a bifurcation parameter.} In the soft-token map, small changes of the steering
strength switch the model between distinct repeating texts, chaos and coexisting attractors. Near a fold curve,
behaviour becomes intermittent, with laminar phases whose duration the fold's normal form predicts in advance. This argues for treating
an activation-steering coefficient like any control parameter of a nonlinear system. Its bifurcation set should be
characterized before an operating point is chosen, history dependence should be expected near boundaries, and a
monotone, smooth dose response should not be assumed. Outputs that fall into verbatim repetition at high steering
doses are naturally treated as quality failures. In the present framework such loops are candidate attracting
periodic orbits, and the dose at which they appear is a boundary that can be mapped like the others. The
deterministic map is where such sets can be defined precisely and computed numerically. Whether its boundaries
predict boundaries of sampled behaviour is open. The bridge of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} shows that sampling noise
shortens the laminar phases of the bottleneck, by an amount that depends on how many samples are averaged.

\paragraph{What this suggests for interpretability and alignment.} Our results come from open models of 0.5B to
72B parameters and a constructed loop, but they suggest three things about models whose outputs feed back into their inputs, as in
self-dialogue, agent loops and pipelines of model calls. First, where the loop is chaotic, a perturbation of
$10^{-6}$ grows to order one within roughly 15 to 50 steps (the largest unsteered exponents of the 7B and 8B
models are 0.30 to 0.88 per step). Even a complete account of single forward passes then predicts a long trajectory
only if the state is known far more precisely than any interpretation supplies. Stepwise summaries can be
inconclusive: every step of the period-8 orbit expands some direction while the cycle contracts, so the ordered
product of the step Jacobians, not a summary of each step, captures the loop's stability
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}). What remains predictable are the invariant sets, their
statistics and the boundaries between them. This extends the analysis of trained recurrent networks through their
fixed points \cite{sussillo2013opening,maheswaranathan2019reverse} to the periodic orbits, chaos and bifurcations of a
pretrained transformer. Second, feature dictionaries say which features an attractor occupies, but the boundaries
between attractors, which decide what a small change does, are properties of the map's derivatives. In our 8B controls,
a sparse-autoencoder feature direction, contrastive and random directions all drove the map into chaos (27 to 39\,\%
of cells); at 70B the hostility-related directions gave more chaotic cells than the random ones (4.3 and 3.5\,\%
against 1.1 and 0.2\,\%; Section~\ref{sec:size}). Steering acts through the gain of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, which is not
specific to concept-labelled directions, but a direction's geometry and its meaning can be correlated. Text can
also hide the dynamics: in the hostile cascade, a seven-token text repeats exactly while the state underneath is
chaotic (Figure~\ref{fig:hiddenbands}), so a monitor that reads only the emitted text would call a chaotic regime
periodic.
Third, conclusions drawn at one operating point need not transfer: which loop a
model enters depends on how much context it keeps (Table~\ref{tab:memory}), on the path by which a boundary is
approached, and on the sampling noise (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}). Treating a looped model as a dynamical system, with
regimes and boundaries to be mapped, rather than as a function to be evaluated point by point, seems to us the
appropriate stance for both interpretability and safety evaluation.

\paragraph{Is the chaos useful?} The chaos we map is not a source of new content. The chaotic cells of
Table~\ref{tab:transcripts} recombine the phrases of the neighbouring loops in an order that did not settle over the recorded horizon: the
trajectory wanders among the ghosts of nearby periodic orbits, which gives variety in sequence but not in substance.
The periodic loops are the familiar degeneration of decoding into repetition
\cite{holtzman2019degeneration,xu2022loop}. Neither regime is good text, and the diversity of ordinary generation
comes from sampling and a growing context, which wash out much of this structure (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}). What
the chaos does matter for is predictability and control. Positive finite-horizon exponents limit open-loop
prediction of a trajectory at fixed state precision (our experiments do not establish mixing or limits on feedback
stabilization); a stable attractor is predictable, but it can also hold a model in a state it should leave, such as the
hostile loops of Section~\ref{sec:hostility}. On the paper's prompt, the largest Qwen2.5 and Llama
models sit near the stable end, but across prompts a 14B Qwen model is more chaotic than the 7B, at higher
temperatures, and OLMo-2-32B is among the most chaotic models we tested (Section~\ref{sec:size}). Whether regimes
near the boundary between order and chaos are especially useful, as has been argued for computation in dynamical
systems and recurrent networks \cite{langton1990computation,bertschinger2004realtime} and shown for signal propagation
in deep networks at initialization \cite{poole2016exponential,schoenholz2017deep}, is open for language models: our
maps locate such boundaries but do not test it.

\paragraph{Which findings might reach frontier models.} Today's frontier systems include closed models such as
GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 and open-weight mixture-of-experts models such as the GLM-5 family. Some of our
findings rest only on structure that every smooth softmax-readout language model with a fixed prefix shares and should
carry over: the factorization of the loop's derivative (a covariance for tied embeddings, a cross-covariance
otherwise), contraction at sufficiently high temperature, the reduction of zero-temperature attractors to greedy
cycles of whatever memory the loop keeps, and the role of a steering coefficient as a bifurcation parameter. Others
belong to particular weights and will not carry over as such: the attractor texts, the positions of windows, folds and
cusps, and every number we report; a second checkpoint of the same architecture already has different
attractors (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Size gives no guarantee of stability. In a fixed temperature window the unsteered
chaos nearly vanished in the largest Qwen2.5 and Llama models, but across prompts a 14B Qwen model was more chaotic
than the 7B, at higher temperatures, OLMo-2-32B was the most chaotic OLMo model, and post-training changed the chaos
of two Llama checkpoints of the same size (Section~\ref{sec:size}). For an open frontier model we would search for
chaos where its own next-token distribution spreads, across prompts. Our one mixture-of-experts model,
Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, gave no positive exponent without steering and irregular cells only under hostility steering, and
hard routing falls outside the smooth theory of Section~\ref{sec:theory} (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Reasoning models
open a thinking block that changes the prefix, post-training may remove some attractors and create others, and
deployed inference samples tokens, in low precision, over long contexts; our bridge results show that sampling
shortens laminar phases and that a longer context changes which loops are entered. Closed models permit none of these
computations: the map needs the input embedding matrix, full next-token distributions and a way to feed a vector in
place of a token, and its analysis needs derivatives. For closed models the method and the questions transfer, not
the numbers.


\section{Evidence and reproducibility}
\label{sec:evidence}
We grade claims on an evidence ladder. Rung 1 means numerically validated (tolerances, residuals, convergence,
precision varied); rung 2, cross-checked by an independent estimate, implementation or hardware; rung 3, a
computer-assisted proof.

\paragraph{Search scale.} The soft-token results come from an exploratory search. An initial census of the raw
data at 00:19 UTC on 14 October 2026 counted 34 distinct maps (987\,308 cells, 27 maps in float64) in six models,
each with one prompt and one of three steering directions or none, plus one partial map. Of these, 29 maps and the
partial one appear here; the five omitted are early discovery or side maps. Later additions are not in that count:
the steering-control maps of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, the mixture-of-experts maps, and the sweeps, lines and maps
of the models of Section~\ref{sec:size}.

language model (Ouro-1.4B) was explored and is not reported; its apparent chaos was a long transient. Every window

  dyadic $\log_{10}T$), the expected-embedding map of the stored Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct checkpoint (all 24 layers, the

  period-8 orbit, which is the unique multiple-shooting root in a product of eight boxes of coordinate radius
  $2^{-640}$ around refined centres, and the orbit is locally attracting. Ball arithmetic (Arb
  \cite{johansson2017arb}) encloses the map and its Jacobian over each box ($8\times896$ directions); a Krawczyk-type
  inclusion \cite{krawczyk1969newton,moore2009interval,rump2010verification} for the preconditioned shooting system gives existence and uniqueness, separation of the first point
  from the points one, two and four steps later gives the primitive period, and a norm bound on powers of the
  enclosed monodromy gives attraction. The map is the real-number map of the stored constants, read as exact
  dyadic rationals: rotary angles, exponentials and square roots are real functions enclosed by the balls, not
  floating-point tables. An independent audit in exact rational arithmetic rechecked the saved witnesses (the
  scalar inequalities, the primitive-period separations and the row norms of the saved monodromy powers); it did
  not recompute the enclosures of the model or the dense matrix products, which rest on one execution. The proof
  trusts the Python, FLINT and Arb stack and the stored weights. It does not exclude other period-8 points in the
  first box, and it says nothing about the window's boundaries or global organization. The record
